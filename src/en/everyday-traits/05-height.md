## A trait of thousands of genes

Unlike [Mendel's](../../heredity/mendels-laws/) peas, height does not fall into categories: it forms a continuous **bell curve**. In the early twentieth century, statistician Ronald Fisher showed that this is what to expect when a trait depends on **many genes** with small effects that add up. It was the start of quantitative genetics.

A century later, studies have confirmed it. In **2022**, the GIANT consortium analysed the DNA of **5.4 million people** and found more than **12,000 variants** linked to height, spread across about 7,200 regions covering around a fifth of the genome. Each adds or subtracts barely a millimetre. In people of European ancestry, these variants explain most of the heritability.

## Heredity and environment

Twin studies show that in rich countries, about **80 %** of the differences in height between people are due to genetic differences. That does not mean the environment does not matter; it means that when almost everyone eats well, what most separates one person from another is their genes. See [genes and environment](../../heredity/genes-and-environment/).

The environment shows up clearly in **history**. According to a 2016 NCD-RisC study, **Spanish men** born in the late twentieth century are on average **more than 10 cm taller** than those born a century earlier, with a similar increase in women. Genes have not changed much in four generations: what changed was **nutrition**, fewer childhood infections and better healthcare.

## Can a child's height be predicted?

Paediatricians' classic formula takes the **average of the parents' heights** and adds 6.5 cm for a boy or subtracts 6.5 cm for a girl. It is right within about 8-10 cm. DNA-based **polygenic scores** now predict height with similar accuracy in Europeans, but much worse in other populations, because most genetic studies have been done on people of European ancestry.

## When a single gene makes the difference

Besides the thousands of small-effect variants, there are changes in a **single gene** with a large effect:

- **Achondroplasia**, caused by a variant in *FGFR3*, is the most common cause of disproportionate short stature.
- The **SHOX** gene, on the X and Y chromosomes, explains part of the short stature in [Turner syndrome](../../diseases/turner-syndrome/) and some cases of familial short stature.
- Variants in **growth hormone** genes can cause treatable short stature.
