## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | Searching for relatives of an unknown sample in genealogy databases and rebuilding their family tree |
| **Case that made it famous** | The Golden State Killer, arrested in 2018 |
| **Difference from police databases** | Analyses hundreds of thousands of variants and looks for distant cousins, not exact matches |
| **Debate** | Privacy of users who never consented to police use |

## How it works

[Police databases](../../genetics-and-society/police-dna-databases/) only find someone if their profile is already registered. **Investigative genetic genealogy** works differently:

1. A profile of **hundreds of thousands of variants** (SNPs), like that of ancestry tests, is obtained from the crime sample (or from unidentified remains).
2. It is uploaded to a **genealogy database** that allows this, and people who share stretches of DNA are sought: second, third or more distant cousins.
3. Using public records, obituaries and social media, genealogists **rebuild the family trees** of those cousins until they find a branch where the wanted person might be.
4. DNA is obtained from possible candidates (for example from a discarded item) and confirmed with traditional forensic analysis.

As explained in [genetic data privacy](../../genetics-and-society/genetic-data-privacy/), with databases of a few million people almost anyone can be found through some cousin.

## The Golden State Killer

Between **1974 and 1986** a man committed at least 13 murders and about 50 rapes in California and was never identified. In **2018** investigators uploaded a DNA profile from a crime scene to **GEDmatch**, a free website where users share their test results. They found distant cousins, rebuilt their tree with the help of genealogist Barbara Rae-Venter and arrived at **Joseph James DeAngelo**, a 72-year-old former police officer. He was arrested in April 2018, confessed, and was sentenced to life imprisonment in 2020.

Since then the technique has solved **hundreds of cold cases** of murder and sexual assault in the United States, and has made it possible to identify many unnamed dead people.

## The dilemmas

- **Consent**: users uploaded their DNA to look for family, not to help the police. After the DeAngelo case, GEDmatch changed its rules in 2019 so that profiles can be compared only by those who **expressly opt in**. Other companies, such as 23andMe and Ancestry, do not allow police access without a court order.
- **Relatives**: a person who has never been tested can be located through a cousin's DNA.
- **Errors**: a wrong genealogical lead can point to an innocent person if it is not confirmed with forensic DNA.
- **Regulation**: in the United States only some states regulate it, such as Maryland and Montana. In Europe its use is much more limited by the GDPR and by laws restricting analysis of coding DNA for police purposes, although countries such as Sweden have already used it with authorisation.

## And in Spain?

Organic Law 10/2007 limits the police database to **non-coding markers** and does not provide for the use of private genealogy databases. There is an open debate between those who see the technique as a valuable tool for unsolved cases and those who warn about the privacy of millions of people.
