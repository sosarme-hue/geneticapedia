## A morning in Leicester

On **10 September 1984**, [Alec Jeffreys](../../people/alec-jeffreys/) was studying regions of DNA that are repeated many times, called **minisatellites**, at the University of Leicester. When he developed an X-ray film with samples from his technician and the technician's parents, he saw a barcode-like pattern of bands that was **different in each person**, and in which children shared bands with their parents. He immediately realised he had a way to identify people and establish relationships. He called it **DNA fingerprinting**.

It was first used in **1985** in an immigration case: it showed that a Ghanaian boy was the son of a British woman, and he was allowed to stay in the UK.

## The first criminal case

In 1983 and 1986 two teenage girls were murdered in **Narborough**, near Leicester. A young man confessed to the second crime, but Jeffreys's analysis showed that his DNA **did not match** that of the killer, which was the same in both cases. He was the first person **exonerated** by DNA.

The police then asked about 5,000 local men for samples. The murderer, Colin Pitchfork, persuaded a colleague to give blood in his place, but the colleague talked about it in a pub and Pitchfork was arrested in 1987. His DNA matched. He was the first person convicted using DNA.

## What is analysed today: STRs

Current methods do not use minisatellites but **STRs** (short tandem repeats): sequences of 2 to 6 letters repeated several times in a row, such as GATA-GATA-GATA. The number of repeats varies a lot between people.

1. DNA is extracted from the sample: blood, saliva, semen, hair with a root, skin cells from a touched object.
2. Between **15 and 25 STR regions** are copied at once by [PCR](../../people/kary-mullis/), which makes it possible to work with tiny amounts of DNA.
3. The length of each fragment is measured, which shows the number of repeats.
4. The result is a **profile**: a list of pairs of numbers.

The probability that two unrelated people have the same full profile is below **one in a billion**, and often much lower. Identical twins, on the other hand, share the same profile.

## Other tools

- **Y chromosome**: useful in mixtures of male and female DNA, as in sexual assaults. All men in a paternal line share it.
- **Mitochondrial DNA**: survives better in old or degraded remains such as bones or rootless hairs. All relatives in a maternal line share it.
- **Forensic phenotyping**: predicting eye, hair and skin colour from a sample's DNA, to guide an investigation with no suspects. Its use is regulated and limited in many countries.

## Why it is not infallible

The technique is very reliable, but errors happen:

- **Contamination** of samples in the laboratory or at the scene.
- **Secondary transfer**: DNA can reach an object without the person having touched it, for example via another person.
- **Complex mixtures** from several people, which are hard to interpret.
- **Human error** in labelling or statistical interpretation.

So DNA shows that a person was in contact with a sample, but not necessarily **how or when**. It must be weighed together with the rest of the evidence.
