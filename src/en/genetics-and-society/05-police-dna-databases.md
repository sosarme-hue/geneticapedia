## What a DNA profile is

Police databases do not store people's genomes. They store a **profile**: a list of numbers showing how many times short sequences are repeated at about 15–25 specific points in DNA, called **STRs** (short tandem repeats). These points are chosen in regions that **contain no genes** and in principle reveal neither diseases nor physical traits, but they vary so much between people that the probability of two unrelated people matching is extremely low. See [DNA fingerprinting](../../forensic-genetics/dna-fingerprinting/).

## The big databases

- The **United Kingdom** created the first national DNA database in **1995**, which now holds several million profiles.
- The **United States** has the **CODIS** system, run by the FBI, with more than 20 million profiles.
- **Spain** created its database with **Organic Law 10/2007**, which brings together profiles from the National Police, the Civil Guard and other forces.

## What Spanish law allows

Organic Law 10/2007 establishes that:

- Only **non-coding identifiers** may be stored, which serve to identify a person and determine sex but not to obtain any other information.
- Profiles may be included from **suspects, detainees or people under investigation** for serious crimes (against life, liberty, sexual freedom, terrorism and organised crime, among others), as well as **traces** from crime scenes, **bodies** and unidentified remains, and **relatives of missing persons** who consent.
- The data are **deleted** after a time: for example, when the person is acquitted, or when the limitation period for the offence expires.

## The European Court's limit

In **2008** the **European Court of Human Rights** ruled against the United Kingdom in **S. and Marper**: two people, one of them a minor, who had never been convicted wanted their profiles deleted. The court held that retaining the DNA of unconvicted people indefinitely **violated the right to private life**. The UK had to change its law and delete more than a million profiles.

## International exchange

Since the **Prüm Treaty** (2005), incorporated into European Union law in 2008, police forces in European countries can **automatically compare** their DNA, fingerprint and vehicle registration databases. A "hit/no-hit" system shows whether a matching profile exists in another country, after which the data can be requested through judicial channels.

## The debates

- **Who to include?** Some advocate universal databases of the whole population; most legal experts consider this disproportionate.
- **Bias**: if mainly people who have been arrested are included, certain social groups are over-represented.
- **Familial searching**: finding a relative of a suspect in the database, a technique allowed in some countries and banned in others.
- **Genetic genealogy**: the use of private genealogy databases, with millions of users who are not suspects, has opened a new debate. See [genetic genealogy](../../forensic-genetics/genetic-genealogy/).
