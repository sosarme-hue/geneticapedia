## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **European law** | General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), 2016 |
| **Category** | Genetic data are a "special category", with stronger protection |
| **Main problem** | It is very hard to anonymise a genome |
| **Added risk** | Your DNA reveals information about your relatives |

## Why DNA is different

Genetic data have features that make them unique:

- **They are permanent**: you can change a password, but not your genome.
- **They identify you**: a genome, or even a few hundred variants, can identify a person.
- **They are shared with family**: your DNA says a lot about your parents, siblings, children and cousins.
- **Their meaning changes**: a variant that cannot be interpreted today may reveal something important in ten years.

## Legal protection

The **General Data Protection Regulation** (GDPR), applicable throughout the European Union since 2018, defines **genetic data** and includes them among the **special categories of data**, together with health data and sexual orientation. As a rule, processing them is **prohibited**, with exceptions such as explicit consent, healthcare or research with safeguards. In Spain it is complemented by Organic Law 3/2018 on data protection.

In addition, the new **European Health Data Space**, approved in 2025, will regulate how health data, including genomic data, are shared for care and research across the Union.

## The myth of anonymity

Many studies and companies promise that genetic data are used "anonymously". But several experiments have shown how hard this is:

- In **2013** a team at the Whitehead Institute led by Yaniv Erlich managed to **identify people** who had taken part anonymously in a genetic study, by matching variants on their Y chromosome with public genealogy databases to infer their surname.
- In **2018** another study estimated that with a genealogy database of about 3 million people of European ancestry, almost any American of that ancestry could be identified through a third cousin or closer relative.

So people now speak of **pseudonymised** rather than anonymous data, combined with security measures, controlled access and committees that review who can use them.

## Real risks

- **Breaches**: in 2023 a large genetic testing company suffered a data theft affecting millions of users.
- **Sale or sharing** of data with third parties, for example the pharmaceutical industry. It is worth reading the terms.
- **Bankruptcy or takeover** of companies, which can change what happens to their databases.
- **Police use** of genealogy databases. See [forensic genetics](../../forensic-genetics/).

## Practical tips

- Before taking a test, read **what the company does with your data** and whether you can delete them.
- Choose whether you want to take part in **research** and relative matching.
- Think about your **relatives**: what you share is theirs too.
- If you are tested through public healthcare, your data are protected by biomedical research law and medical confidentiality. See [the law and genetic testing](../the-law-and-genetic-testing/).
