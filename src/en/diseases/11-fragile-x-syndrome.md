## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Gene** | *FMR1*, X chromosome |
| **Mutation** | Expansion of CGG repeats (over 200) |
| **Inheritance** | X-linked, with anticipation |
| **Frequency** | 1 in 4,000-7,000 males and 1 in 6,000-11,000 females |
| **Premutation carriers** | About 1 in 150-250 women |

## What it is

**Fragile X syndrome** is the most common **inherited** cause of intellectual disability and the most common identified genetic cause of **autism**. The name comes from how it was discovered: when cells were grown in a folate-poor medium, the tip of the long arm of the X chromosome looked **pinched**, as if about to break.

### How it shows

In **males** it usually causes:

- **Intellectual disability**, generally moderate, and delayed speech.
- **Autistic features** (in about half), hyperactivity, attention deficit, anxiety, extreme shyness, gaze avoidance, hand flapping and great sensitivity to noise and touch.
- **Physical features** that become more noticeable after puberty: long face, large or prominent ears, broad forehead, very flexible joints and enlarged testes (macroorchidism).
- In 10-20 %, seizures.

In **females** with the full mutation the effects are more variable and usually milder: learning difficulties, especially with maths, social anxiety or shyness. About half have normal or borderline intelligence. The difference is because they have a second, healthy X chromosome, and in each cell one of the two X chromosomes is randomly switched off.

## Genetic cause

It is caused by an **expansion of CGG repeats** in the ***FMR1*** gene on the X chromosome. Normally there are 5 to 44 repeats. When they exceed **200** (full mutation), the gene is "switched off" by **methylation** (an epigenetic mechanism) and stops making the **FMRP** protein. This protein controls the production of other proteins at **synapses**, the connections between neurons, and is key to learning and memory.

| CGG repeats | Category | Consequences |
|---|---|---|
| 5-44 | Normal | None |
| 45-54 | Grey zone | May grow slightly when passed on |
| 55-200 | **Premutation** | Does not cause fragile X syndrome, but can expand in children and cause other problems |
| Over 200 | **Full mutation** | Fragile X syndrome |

## The premutation: more than just carriers

People with a **premutation** do not have fragile X syndrome, but they are not simply carriers:

- **FXPOI**: about 20 % of women with a premutation have **premature ovarian insufficiency**, with menopause before 40.
- **FXTAS**: after age 50, some carriers, especially men (up to 40 % of those over 50), develop a **tremor and ataxia** (unsteadiness) syndrome with progressive cognitive decline.
- **FXAND**: more anxiety, depression and other psychological difficulties.

## Inheritance and anticipation

Fragile X syndrome is the classic example of **anticipation**: repeats tend to grow from one generation to the next, especially when passed on by a **woman**. A grandmother may have a premutation without symptoms, her daughter a larger premutation and her grandson the full mutation.

- A **woman** with a premutation has a 50 % chance of passing on the expanded X in each pregnancy. The risk that it becomes a full mutation depends on its size: low with fewer than 70 repeats and almost certain with more than 100.
- A **man** with a premutation passes it to **all his daughters**, who will carry a premutation (almost never a full mutation), and to **none of his sons**.

So one family may have children with intellectual disability, women with early menopause and grandparents with tremor, with nobody suspecting they share a cause.

## Diagnosis

Genetic testing of *FMR1* (PCR and complementary techniques that measure repeat size and methylation) is recommended for:

- Children with **developmental delay**, intellectual disability or **autism spectrum disorder** of unknown cause.
- Women with **premature ovarian insufficiency**.
- Adults over 50 with unexplained **intention tremor and ataxia**.
- Relatives of a diagnosed person.

The old karyotype, which looked for the "fragile site", is no longer used because it is unreliable. Cascade testing of the whole family is essential, and genetic counselling allows prenatal or preimplantation diagnosis to be offered.

## Treatment

There is no cure, but early intervention makes a difference:

- **Early intervention**, speech therapy, occupational therapy (including sensory integration) and individualised educational support.
- **Symptom treatment**: medication for anxiety, hyperactivity, aggression or seizures when needed.
- **Family support**, as several members are often affected.

Drugs targeting the disease mechanism, such as glutamate receptor modulators, have been tested with disappointing results in humans so far. Trials of new molecules are under way, such as PDE4D enzyme inhibitors, with promising preliminary results, along with strategies to reactivate the silenced gene.
