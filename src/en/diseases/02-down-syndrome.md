| | |
|---|---|
| **Cause** | Extra material from chromosome 21 |
| **Inheritance** | Almost never inherited; occurs by chance |
| **Frequency** | About 1 in 1,000 births worldwide |
| **Detection** | Combined prenatal screening and cell-free fetal DNA in maternal blood; confirmed by karyotype |
| **Life expectancy** | About 60 years in countries with good health care |

## What it is

**Down syndrome** is the most common chromosomal condition in live-born babies and the most common genetic cause of **intellectual disability**. It occurs because cells carry extra material from **chromosome 21**, which subtly alters the balance of hundreds of genes during development.

It is not a disease that one "suffers from" or that can be cured: it is a genetic condition that stays with a person throughout life. It involves intellectual disability of varying degree, usually mild or moderate, characteristic physical features and a higher likelihood of some health problems that are now well understood and can be monitored. Every person is different: many learn to read and write, study, work, play sport and live with varying degrees of independence.

## A little history

The British physician **John Langdon Down** described it in 1866 in residents of an institution he ran, using a racial term now considered unacceptable. For almost a century the cause was unknown. In **1958-1959**, at the Trousseau hospital in Paris, the physician [Marthe Gautier](../../people/marthe-gautier/) counted 47 chromosomes in cells from children with the syndrome. The finding was published in 1959 together with Jérôme Lejeune and Raymond Turpin. It was the first time a chromosomal abnormality had been linked to a human syndrome.

## The three forms

- **Free trisomy 21** (about 95 %): three complete copies of chromosome 21 in every cell. It results from an error in chromosome sorting, called **non-disjunction**, when the egg (over 90 % of cases) or sperm is formed.
- **Translocation** (about 3-4 %): the extra chromosome 21 is attached to another chromosome, often number 14. In some of these cases one parent carries a **balanced translocation** with no health consequences but an increased risk of recurrence in other pregnancies. So if the baby has a translocation, the parents are tested.
- **Mosaicism** (1-2 %): only some cells have the trisomy. Features may be milder, although this varies a lot.

## Is it inherited?

In the vast majority of cases **no**: it happens by chance while reproductive cells form. The likelihood **increases with maternal age**: at 25 it is about 1 in 1,200 pregnancies, and at 40 about 1 in 100. Even so, because younger women have more children overall, a large share of children with Down syndrome are born to mothers under 35.

After having a child with free trisomy 21, the risk of recurrence in another pregnancy is about 1 %, or the age-related risk if that is higher. When a translocation is inherited, the risk depends on its type and which parent carries it, and is explained in a genetic counselling appointment.

## Features and health

The most frequent physical features are almond-shaped eyes with a fold at the inner corner, a flat facial profile, a single crease across the palm, short stature and **hypotonia** (low muscle tone) in the early years. None is exclusive to Down syndrome or present in everyone.

The most important health problems, which are checked systematically, are:

- **Congenital heart disease** (40-50 %), especially atrioventricular septal defect. An echocardiogram is done in the first weeks; many are successfully operated on in the first year.
- **Digestive problems**: duodenal atresia, Hirschsprung's disease, coeliac disease.
- **Thyroid**: hypothyroidism is common at any age, so it is checked regularly.
- **Hearing and vision**: glue ear, hearing loss, cataracts, short-sightedness, keratoconus.
- **Sleep apnoea**, which can affect behaviour and learning.
- **Leukaemia**: the risk in childhood is higher than in the general population, though still low.
- **Instability of the cervical spine**, to bear in mind for some activities.
- **Alzheimer's disease** in adulthood, at earlier ages than in the general population, because chromosome 21 contains the *APP* gene, whose protein forms amyloid plaques.

Health-supervision guidelines, such as those of the American Academy of Pediatrics or the **Spanish Health Programme for People with Down Syndrome**, set out which check-ups to do at each age.

## Diagnosis

### During pregnancy

In Spain all pregnant women are offered **first-trimester combined screening** between weeks 11 and 14, which combines maternal age, ultrasound measurement of the fold at the back of the fetal neck (nuchal translucency) and two blood markers. If the risk is high, **cell-free fetal DNA testing in maternal blood** can be offered, which detects over 99 % of cases with very few false positives. These are **screening** tests: diagnosis is only confirmed by an invasive test, **chorionic villus sampling** or **amniocentesis**, which carry a small risk of miscarriage.

### After birth

It is suspected from the baby's features and confirmed by a blood **karyotype**, which also distinguishes free trisomy, translocation and mosaicism.

## Early intervention and adult life

**Early intervention** from the first months, with physiotherapy, speech therapy and stimulation, improves motor development, language and independence. **Inclusive schooling**, supported employment programmes and supported independent living have transformed expectations in recent decades.

**Life expectancy** has risen from about 10 years in 1960 to around **60 years** today in countries with good health care, largely thanks to heart surgery. This brings new challenges: premature ageing, and preventing and diagnosing Alzheimer's disease in adults.

## Current research

- Trials of drugs and vaccines against **amyloid** in adults with Down syndrome, a key group for studying Alzheimer's prevention.
- Studies of which chromosome 21 genes most influence learning and memory.
- In the laboratory, the extra chromosome 21 has been "silenced" in cultured cells, a line of basic research still very far from clinical use.

## What to ask at your appointment

Questions you can take with you. Not all of them will have an answer on day one, and your care team may add others.

- Which heart, thyroid, eye and hearing checks does my child need, and how often?
- Which early intervention service can we be referred to?
- Does the karyotype show a free trisomy, a translocation or mosaicism?
- If it is a translocation, should we as parents have a karyotype?
- What is our risk in a future pregnancy and which tests can we be offered?
