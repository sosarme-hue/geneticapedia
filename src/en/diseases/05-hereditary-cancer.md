| **How much is inherited** | About 5-10 % of cancers |
| **Most common syndromes** | Hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (*BRCA1*, *BRCA2*) and Lynch syndrome |
| **Inheritance** | Almost always autosomal dominant |
| **What is inherited** | An increased risk, not cancer itself |
| **Where to go** | Cancer genetic counselling units in public hospitals |

## All cancer is genetic, little of it is inherited

Every cancer arises from changes in the DNA of some cells, which begin to divide uncontrollably. But almost always these are changes **acquired** during life: by chance when DNA is copied, through ageing, or through factors such as tobacco, alcohol, ultraviolet radiation or some infections. These changes are **only in the tumour** and are not passed on to children.

Only about **5 to 10 %** of cancers[[5]] are due to an **inherited** variant, present from birth in every cell, that greatly increases the risk. Another 15-20 % show familial clustering, more cases than expected, from a mix of many small-effect genes and shared environment.

## Why an inherited variant increases risk

Many hereditary cancer genes are **tumour suppressor** or **DNA repair** genes: their normal job is to brake cell division or fix errors. We have two copies of each. Someone who inherits one altered copy starts life with half the work done: the other copy only has to fail in one cell during life for that cell to lose its brake. This is the **two-hit hypothesis**, proposed by Alfred Knudson in 1971 from his study of retinoblastoma.

So although the variant is inherited **dominantly**, what is inherited is a **predisposition**. Not every carrier develops cancer, and when it occurs it is often at younger ages than in the general population.

## The best-known syndromes

### Hereditary breast and ovarian cancer: *BRCA1* and *BRCA2*

The *BRCA1* (chromosome 17) and *BRCA2* (chromosome 13) genes help repair DNA breaks. *BRCA1* was located in 1990 by [Mary-Claire King](../../people/mary-claire-king/).

- They affect about **1 in 300-400** people[[7]], more in some populations, such as Ashkenazi Jews (1 in 40).
- **Lifetime breast cancer risk** in women: about **72 %** with *BRCA1* and **69 %** with *BRCA2* by age 80, compared with about 12 % in the general population[[1]].
- **Ovarian cancer risk**: about **44 %** with *BRCA1* and **17 %** with *BRCA2*.[[1]]
- They also increase the risk of **prostate** cancer (especially *BRCA2*, with more aggressive forms), **pancreatic** cancer and **male breast** cancer.

Other genes with moderate or high breast cancer risk include *PALB2*, *CHEK2*, *ATM*, *RAD51C*, *RAD51D*, *TP53* and *CDH1*.

### Lynch syndrome

It is caused by variants in DNA **mismatch repair** genes: *MLH1*, *MSH2*, *MSH6*, *PMS2* and *EPCAM*.

- It is the most common hereditary cause of **colorectal** and **endometrial cancer**. It affects about **1 in 300** people[[2]], although most do not know it.
- Risk varies greatly by gene: highest with *MLH1* and *MSH2*, lowest with *PMS2*.
- It also raises the risk of ovarian, stomach, small bowel, urinary tract and bile duct cancers.
- The tumours have a characteristic "signature", **microsatellite instability**, which is routinely tested in all colorectal and endometrial cancers to detect families with Lynch syndrome.

### Other syndromes

- **Familial adenomatous polyposis** (*APC* gene): hundreds or thousands of colon polyps from adolescence; without surgery, colon cancer is almost certain.
- **Li-Fraumeni syndrome** (*TP53*): sarcomas, brain tumours, very early breast cancer, leukaemias and adrenal tumours in childhood.
- **Hereditary diffuse gastric cancer** (*CDH1*), which also increases the risk of lobular breast cancer.
- **Multiple endocrine neoplasias** (*MEN1*, *RET*), **retinoblastoma** (*RB1*), **von Hippel-Lindau syndrome** (*VHL*) and other rarer conditions.

## When to suspect it

- Several relatives on the **same side** of the family with the same or related cancers (for example breast and ovary, or colon and endometrium).
- Cancers at **young ages**: breast cancer before 40-45, colon cancer before 50.
- One person with **two different cancers**, or cancer in both breasts.
- **Ovarian**, **pancreatic**, **metastatic prostate** or **male breast** cancer, at any age.
- Many polyps in the colon.
- A **known variant** in the family.

If any of this sounds familiar, talk to your GP. **Cancer genetic counselling units** in public hospitals assess who should be tested, using criteria agreed by the scientific societies.

## How testing works

1. **Genetic counselling appointment**: a family tree of at least three generations is drawn, the probability of a hereditary syndrome is estimated, and the advantages, limits and implications of testing are explained.
2. **Genetic test**: ideally it starts with a relative who **has had cancer**. Today a **panel** of genes related to their cancer type is analysed from a blood or saliva sample.
3. **Result**:
   - **Positive**: a pathogenic variant is found. Prevention is offered and relatives are tested.
   - **Negative**: none is found. If there was a known variant in the family, the person returns to general population risk. If not, risk is estimated from the family history.
   - **Variant of uncertain significance**: it is not known whether it increases risk. It **should not** be used to make decisions; over time, most are reclassified as benign.
4. **Cascade testing**: first-degree relatives (parents, siblings, children) have a 50 % chance of carrying the same variant and are offered a targeted test.

Increasingly, genetic testing is also done **at diagnosis** of some cancers (ovarian, pancreatic, metastatic prostate, triple-negative breast), because the result changes treatment.

## What can be done

Knowing you carry a high-risk variant makes it possible to:

- **Step up surveillance**: for *BRCA* carriers, annual breast MRI from age 25-30[[4]] and mammography from 30-35; for Lynch syndrome, **colonoscopy every 1-2 years** from age 25, which clearly reduces the risk of dying from colorectal cancer.
- **Risk-reducing surgery**: removing the **ovaries and fallopian tubes** (salpingo-oophorectomy) once childbearing is complete, around 35-40[[4]] with *BRCA1* and 40-45 with *BRCA2*, cuts ovarian cancer risk by more than 80 % and reduces overall mortality. Risk-reducing **mastectomy** cuts breast cancer risk by more than 90 %; it is a personal decision best taken calmly and with support.
- **Preventive medication**: daily **aspirin** reduces colorectal cancer risk in Lynch syndrome, according to a long-term clinical trial.
- **Targeted treatments**: **PARP inhibitors** (olaparib and others) exploit the DNA repair defect of *BRCA* tumours and are used in ovarian, breast, pancreatic and prostate cancer. Lynch tumours, with many mutations, often respond very well to **immunotherapy**.
- **Reproductive choices**: prenatal or preimplantation diagnosis, if the person wishes.

## What an online test does not do

Consumer genetic tests that check a few variants, for example three *BRCA* variants common in Ashkenazi Jews, **do not rule out** hereditary cancer: they can give false reassurance. With a suggestive family history, testing should be done in a genetic counselling unit.

## What to ask at your appointment

Questions you can take with you. Not all of them will have an answer on day one, and your care team may add others.

- Given my family history, do I meet the criteria for genetic testing?
- Which genes are in the panel, and what happens if a variant of uncertain significance turns up?
- If I carry a variant, which screening or risk-reducing surgery is recommended, and from what age?
- Does the result change the treatment of a cancer I already have?
- How do I tell my relatives, and which of them should be tested?
