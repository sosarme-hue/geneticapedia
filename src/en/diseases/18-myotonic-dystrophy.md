## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Genes** | *DMPK*, chromosome 19 (type 1) and *CNBP*, chromosome 3 (type 2) |
| **Inheritance** | Autosomal dominant, with anticipation |
| **Frequency** | About 1 in 8,000 people for type 1; higher in some regions |
| **Main features** | Myotonia, muscle weakness, cataracts, arrhythmias, sleepiness |
| **Treatment** | Heart and breathing monitoring, symptom treatment; trials of RNA therapies |

## What it is

**Myotonic dystrophy** is a disease that affects the muscles and many other organs. Its hallmark is **myotonia**: difficulty **relaxing a muscle** after contracting it. A person with myotonia may shake hands and take a few seconds to be able to let go, or have trouble opening their eyes after squeezing them shut.

There are two types. **Type 1** (DM1, or **Steinert** disease, after the physician who described it in 1909) is the more common and severe. **Type 2** (DM2), described in the 1990s, is milder and mainly affects the muscles close to the trunk.

It is the **most common muscular dystrophy in adults**[[1]], and also one of the most varied: it ranges from cataracts at 60 as the only sign to severe forms in newborns.

## Cause: a repeat that grows

DM1 is caused by a **triplet expansion**[[3]]: a three-letter sequence, **CTG**, that is repeated more times than normal in the ***DMPK*** gene. It is the same kind of mechanism as in [Huntington's disease](../huntingtons-disease/) and [fragile X syndrome](../fragile-x-syndrome/).

| Number of CTG repeats | Situation |
|---|---|
| 5–34 | Normal |
| 35–49 | Premutation: no symptoms, but it can grow in children |
| 50–150 | Mild form: cataracts, mild myotonia, often in older people |
| 100–1,000 | Classic, adult form |
| More than 1,000 | Childhood or congenital form |

The curious thing is that the gene does not stop working. The problem lies in the **RNA**: the copy of the gene with the repeat forms an RNA with a long CUG hairpin that gets stuck in the nucleus and **"sequesters" a protein called MBNL1**, which is responsible for correctly cutting and splicing the RNA of many other genes. Without it, muscle, heart and brain genes produce "fetal" versions of their proteins. For example, a chloride channel in muscle works poorly, which explains the myotonia. It is an example of a **disease caused by a toxic RNA**.

DM2 is caused by an expansion of **CCTG** in the ***CNBP*** gene, with a similar mechanism.

## Anticipation

In DM1 the repeat tends to **grow as it passes from parents to children**[[1]], so the disease appears **earlier and more severely** in each generation. This is called **anticipation**. A typical family might have a grandparent with cataracts, a mother with myotonia and weakness as an adult, and a child with the congenital form.

The **congenital form**, the most severe, is almost always inherited from the **mother**. Babies are born with low muscle tone (hypotonia), breathing and swallowing problems, and later have intellectual disability. Many survive the newborn period and gain strength during childhood, but develop the adult symptoms later.

## Symptoms in adults

- **Muscles**: myotonia in the hands and tongue; weakness of the face (with a long face and drooping eyelids), neck, hands and feet. Over the years, difficulty walking.
- **Heart**: problems with electrical conduction and **arrhythmias**, which can cause sudden death. This is the most important complication to monitor.
- **Breathing**: weak breathing muscles, sleep apnoea and infections.
- **Eyes**: early **cataracts**, often before 50[[4]], which may be the first sign.
- **Brain**: excessive **daytime sleepiness**, apathy, fatigue and difficulties with attention and organisation.
- **Hormones**: insulin resistance and diabetes, thyroid problems, low testosterone and early frontal balding in men.
- **Digestive system**: swallowing problems, constipation or irritable bowel.
- **Anaesthesia**: higher risk of complications with certain anaesthetics, something the person should mention before any operation.

## Diagnosis

It is suspected from the combination of weakness, myotonia and family history (early cataracts, arrhythmias). **Electromyography** shows typical myotonic discharges, with a sound like a "motorbike revving up and down". Confirmation is by **genetic testing**, which measures the size of the repeat. When someone is diagnosed, [genetic counselling](../../diagnosis/genetic-counselling/) should be offered to the family, since many relatives have mild forms they are unaware of.

## Treatment

There is not yet a treatment that corrects the cause, but **structured follow-up** saves lives:

- A **yearly electrocardiogram** and, if there are changes, Holter monitoring or an electrophysiology study. Many people need a **pacemaker** or an implantable defibrillator.
- **Lung function tests**, and non-invasive ventilation at night if needed.
- **Cataract surgery**.
- **Mexiletine** for troublesome myotonia, and treatments for sleepiness.
- **Physiotherapy**, moderate exercise, ankle braces.
- Monitoring of glucose, thyroid and swallowing.

## Research

Myotonic dystrophy is one of the most active targets for **RNA therapies**[[2]]. Several drugs in clinical trials use **antisense oligonucleotides** or RNA interference attached to antibodies that carry them into muscle, to destroy the toxic RNA and free MBNL1. Early results in 2024 and 2025 showed improved splicing in muscle and signs of functional improvement, and larger trials are under way. [CRISPR gene editing](../../treatments/crispr-explained/) to remove the repeat is also being studied.

## What to ask at your appointment

Questions you can take with you. Not all of them will have an answer on day one, and your care team may add others.

- How many CTG repeats do I have, and which type of myotonic dystrophy is it?
- How often do I need an ECG or Holter monitoring?
- What precautions should I take before an anaesthetic?
- What can I do about tiredness, sleepiness or cataracts?
- What is the risk for my children, and why can it be more severe in the next generation?
