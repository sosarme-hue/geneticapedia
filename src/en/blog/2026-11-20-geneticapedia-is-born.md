In primary care, genetics comes up more and more often. A patient asks whether she should have "the BRCA test" because her aunt had breast cancer. A father arrives with a report from an ancestry kit bought online saying his son has an "increased risk" of something. A couple wants to understand what their prenatal screening result means.

Behind those questions there is almost always the same difficulty: genetics is full of strange words, frightening numbers and headlines that promise too much.

## What Geneticapedia is

A **free**, **plain-language** encyclopedia, in Spanish and English, to help you understand:

- **The basics**: what DNA, genes and chromosomes are, and how a sequence of letters becomes a protein.
- **Heredity**: from Mendel to epigenetics.
- **Diagnosis**: what each genetic test looks at, how to read a result, genetic counselling and what to expect from mail-order tests.
- **Inherited conditions**: clear fact sheets with their cause, inheritance, diagnosis, treatment and where to learn more.
- **Treatments**: targeted and supportive care, gene therapy, CRISPR, pharmacogenetics and the ethical dilemmas they raise.
- **People**: profiles of the physicians, geneticists, biochemists and biologists who moved genetics forward, from Mendel to CRISPR.
- A **history** of genetics, a **glossary**, a **quiz** and a **sources** page for further reading.

## How it is made

Every article cites its **sources**: scientific papers, clinical guidelines and bodies such as the National Human Genome Research Institute, MedlinePlus, the World Health Organization or the Spanish Ministry of Health. We separate what is proven from what is being researched, and what is being researched from what is being sold.

And a warning we will repeat: Geneticapedia **does not replace** your doctor or genetic counselling. If anything you read worries you, talk to your doctor.

## A cover photo with a story

The cover photo shows **Francis Crick** (right) and **James Watson** (behind) greeting **Maclyn McCarty**. In 1953 Watson and Crick proposed the double-helix structure of DNA, using the diffraction data of **Rosalind Franklin** and Raymond Gosling. Nine years earlier, McCarty, with Oswald Avery and Colin MacLeod, had shown that DNA is the hereditary material. Two steps of the same story in a single image, which we tell in [this post](../avery-macleod-and-mccarty/). We dedicate [another](../rosalind-franklin-and-photo-51/) to Franklin.
