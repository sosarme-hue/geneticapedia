## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Difference between two people** | About 0.1 % of the genome |
| **Where variation lies** | About 85 % within each population, not between populations |
| **Greatest diversity** | In Africa |
| **Better concept** | Genetic ancestry, continuous and mixed |

## We are very alike

Any two people share about **99.9 %** of their DNA sequence. Humanity is a young species that went through bottlenecks and left Africa recently in evolutionary terms (see [the African origin of humanity](../out-of-africa/)). There is more genetic diversity in a group of chimpanzees from a single region of Africa than in all of humanity.

## Variation lies within groups

In **1972** the biologist **Richard Lewontin** analysed the variation of several blood proteins in different human populations and reached a conclusion that whole-genome studies have confirmed: about **85 %** of human genetic variation is found **within each population**, and only a small part distinguishes one population from another.

In other words, two people from the same village can be more genetically different from each other than two people from different continents.

## Variation is gradual

Genetic differences between populations change **gradually** with geographical distance, without sharp boundaries. There is no point on the map where one group ends and another begins. And populations have mixed constantly throughout history. See [Atapuerca and ancient DNA](../atapuerca-and-ancient-dna/).

## Skin colour is misleading

Traditional racial categories are based mainly on **skin colour**, which depends on a small number of genes under strong selection by ultraviolet radiation. Dark skin protects against intense sun; light skin makes it easier to produce vitamin D in low light. Dark-skinned populations in Africa, southern India and Australia are genetically very different from one another. Skin colour says very little about the rest of the genome. See [everyday traits](../../everyday-traits/).

## So does ancestry not matter?

It does, but we should speak of **genetic ancestry**, not races:

- Some variants are more common in certain populations because of their history: [sickle cell disease](../../diseases/sickle-cell-disease-and-thalassaemia/) in areas where malaria was present, [cystic fibrosis](../../diseases/cystic-fibrosis/) in northern Europe, or Tay-Sachs disease in Ashkenazi Jews.
- The response to some drugs varies with ancestry. See [pharmacogenetics](../../treatments/pharmacogenetics/).
- Most genetic studies have been done in people of **European ancestry**, so analyses work less well in other populations. Correcting this is a priority for genetics today.

## A history worth remembering

The idea of biologically separate and ranked human races was used for centuries to justify slavery, colonialism, **eugenics** and genocide. See [history](../../history/). The American Association of Biological Anthropologists summed it up in 2019: race is not an accurate description of human biological variation, but a social category with real consequences.
