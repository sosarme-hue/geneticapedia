## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Molecular clock** | Mutations accumulate at a roughly constant rate |
| **Rate in humans** | About 1.2 changes per 100 million letters per generation |
| **Ancient DNA** | Allows genomes from different eras to be compared directly |
| **Limit** | Estimates have wide margins of error |

## The molecular clock

In the 1960s Emile Zuckerkandl and [Linus Pauling](../../people/linus-pauling/) noticed that differences in haemoglobin between species increased roughly in proportion to how long they had been separated. They proposed the idea of the **molecular clock**: if mutations accumulate at a roughly constant rate, counting the differences between two DNA sequences makes it possible to estimate **when they diverged**.

To calibrate the clock, the **mutation rate** must be known:

- It can be estimated **by comparison with well-dated fossils**, for example the split between humans and chimpanzees.
- Or **directly**, by sequencing parents and children and counting new mutations. In humans each child is born with about **60–70 new mutations**, equivalent to about 1.2 changes per 100 million letters per generation. Most come from the father, and they increase with his age.

Using this clock it is estimated, for example, that the human and chimpanzee lineages split about **6–7 million years ago**, and that modern humans and Neanderthals did so about 500,000–700,000 years ago.

## Ancient DNA as a time machine

The molecular clock has a problem: the rate estimated from fossils and the rate measured in families do not always agree. **Ancient DNA** helped to resolve this. In 2014 [Svante Pääbo](../../people/svante-paabo/)'s team sequenced the genome of a man who lived about **45,000 years** ago at Ust'-Ishim in Siberia. Because the bone had been radiocarbon-dated, it was possible to count how many mutations he was "missing" compared with people today and calculate the mutation rate directly.

## Neanderthal fragments

The same Ust'-Ishim man carried **longer** stretches of Neanderthal DNA than people today. The reason: in each generation, recombination cuts the fragments into smaller pieces. By measuring their length it is possible to calculate **how many generations** have passed since the interbreeding. This is how the mixing with Neanderthals was dated to about 50,000–60,000 years ago.

## Dating migrations and mixtures

With similar methods, geneticists date:

- The **arrival of farmers** from Anatolia in Europe, about 8,000 years ago.
- The **migration from the steppes** of eastern Europe about 5,000 years ago, which changed the genetics of almost the whole continent. See [Atapuerca and ancient DNA](../atapuerca-and-ancient-dna/).
- The **peopling of the Americas** and of the Pacific islands.

## The limits

- The mutation rate **varies** between regions of the genome, between species and with the parents' age.
- The **generation time** assumed (usually 25–30 years) greatly affects the result.
- Dates are **estimates with wide margins**, of thousands or tens of thousands of years.
- Ancient DNA survives poorly in warm climates, so there are few ancient genomes from Africa and the tropics. For very ancient periods, tooth **proteins** survive better than DNA.
