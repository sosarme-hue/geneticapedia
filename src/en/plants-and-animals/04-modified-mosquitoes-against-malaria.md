| **Carrier** | Female *Anopheles* mosquitoes |
| **Strategies** | Sterile males, *Wolbachia* bacteria, gene drives |
| **Gene drive** | A change inherited by almost 100 % of offspring instead of 50 % |

## The deadliest animal

According to the World Health Organization, in 2023 there were about **263 million malaria cases** and about **597,000 deaths**, mostly children under five in Africa. Malaria is spread by female *Anopheles* mosquitoes. *Aedes* mosquitoes also spread **dengue**, Zika and chikungunya, and *Aedes albopictus*, the **tiger mosquito**, is now established across much of Spain.

Insecticide-treated nets and drugs have saved millions of lives, but mosquitoes become resistant to insecticides and the parasite to drugs. That is why genetic tools are being developed.

## Three strategies

### Sterile males

Only females bite. If large numbers of **sterile males** are released, females that mate with them have no offspring and the population falls. The approach has been used against farm pests since the 1950s, with males sterilised by radiation. The British company Oxitec created *Aedes aegypti* mosquitoes carrying a gene that kills female offspring, and has released them in Brazil, the Cayman Islands and Florida. The effect is local and releases must continue.

### Wolbachia bacteria

This is not a genetic modification of the mosquito but an infection. *Wolbachia* lives naturally in many insects, and when introduced into *Aedes aegypti* it **stops the mosquito transmitting dengue**. It also spreads through the population by itself. In a randomised trial in **Yogyakarta, Indonesia**, published in 2021, areas where these mosquitoes were released had **77 % fewer dengue cases**.

### Gene drives

Normally a gene passes to 50 % of offspring, as [Mendel's laws](../../heredity/mendels-laws/) explain. A **gene drive** is a construct built with [CRISPR](../../treatments/crispr-explained/) that copies itself onto the other chromosome, so that **almost 100 %** of offspring inherit it. Within a few generations it can spread through an entire population.

There are two possible uses:

- **Suppressing** the population, for example by breaking a gene that females need to be fertile. In **2018**, Andrea Crisanti's team at Imperial College London made caged populations of *Anopheles gambiae* collapse completely by breaking the *doublesex* gene.
- **Modifying** the population so that mosquitoes cannot carry the malaria parasite, without eliminating them.

The **Target Malaria** consortium, with teams in Burkina Faso, Uganda and Ghana, is working towards the first field trials in Africa.

## Why caution is needed

Once released, a gene drive **cannot be recalled**. Concerns include:

- Effects on **ecosystems**: what happens to animals that eat mosquitoes and to other insects. There are about 3,500 mosquito species, and only a few spread malaria.
- Spread into **other countries** without their consent.
- **Involving the affected communities** in the decision.

The US National Academies and the World Health Organization have published guidance for proceeding **step by step**: laboratory, large cages, isolated islands and only then open releases, with risk assessment and community consultation.
