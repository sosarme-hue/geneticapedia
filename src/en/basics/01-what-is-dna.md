## A recipe written with four letters

**DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the molecule that stores the hereditary information of almost all living things, from a bacterium to a whale. It works like an instruction book: it tells cells which proteins to make, when and in what amount.

That book is written with an alphabet of just four "letters", the **nitrogenous bases**:

- **A**: adenine
- **T**: thymine
- **G**: guanine
- **C**: cytosine

The order of those letters, the **sequence**, is what carries the information, just as the order of the letters in this sentence gives it meaning.

## The double helix

DNA is made of two strands wound around each other like a spiral staircase: the famous **double helix**. The sides of the staircase are sugar (deoxyribose) and phosphate; the steps are base pairs that always fit the same way:

- **A** pairs with **T**;
- **G** pairs with **C**.

This pairing rule is the key to everything. Because each strand "predicts" the other, the cell can separate them and use each one as a template to make a copy. That is how DNA is duplicated every time a cell divides.

The structure was described by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953, relying decisively on the X-ray diffraction images obtained by **Rosalind Franklin** and Raymond Gosling, and on the work of Maurice Wilkins.

## Where is it?

In human cells, almost all DNA is inside the **nucleus**, packaged into **chromosomes**. A small part is in the **mitochondria**, the cell's power stations, and is inherited only from the mother.

If the DNA of a single human cell were stretched out, it would measure about **two metres**. It fits in a nucleus a few thousandths of a millimetre across because it is wound around proteins called histones, like thread on spools, and those spools are folded again and again.

## Numbers to get your bearings

- The human genome has about **3.1 billion base pairs** in each set of chromosomes. Since we inherit one set from each parent, each cell has twice that.
- It contains about **20,000 genes** that code for proteins.
- Less than **2 %** of DNA codes for proteins. The rest is not "junk": much of it regulates when and where genes are switched on, although the function of many regions is still being studied.
- Any two people share about **99.9 %** of their sequence at the level of single letters. The remaining 0.1 %, a few million differences, explains part of our variety.

## DNA and RNA

**RNA** (ribonucleic acid) is a close cousin of DNA. It usually has a single strand, uses ribose instead of deoxyribose and replaces T with **U** (uracil). The cell uses it mainly as a working copy of genes, as you will see in [From gene to protein](../from-gene-to-protein/).
