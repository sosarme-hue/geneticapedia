## Who she was

Barbara McClintock was born in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1902 and earned her PhD in botany at Cornell University in 1927. She was an exceptional **cytogeneticist**, with a unique ability to tell apart the ten maize chromosomes under the microscope and spot tiny changes in them.

## Early discoveries

In 1931, with her student **Harriet Creighton**, she showed that when two traits recombine during inheritance, chromosomes **physically exchange pieces**. It was visual proof that genetic recombination happens on chromosomes. She also described **telomeres** as structures that protect chromosome ends, and ring chromosomes.

In 1944 she became the third woman elected to the US National Academy of Sciences and, in 1945, the first woman president of the Genetics Society of America.

## Genes that jump

From 1942 she worked at Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory in New York. Studying maize cobs with speckled kernels, where colour appeared and disappeared irregularly, she reached a surprising conclusion: there were pieces of DNA, which she called **Ds** (dissociation) and **Ac** (activator), that could **move around** the genome. Inserted next to a gene they switched it off; when they left, it switched back on.

She presented this at the 1951 Cold Spring Harbor symposium. The response was incomprehension: the idea of a stable genome, with genes fixed like beads on a string, was deeply rooted. McClintock stopped publishing in general journals, although she kept working.

## Recognition

In the 1960s and 1970s **transposable elements** were found in bacteria, yeast, flies and humans. McClintock received the **1983 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine** at the age of 81. She remains the only woman to have received it **unshared** in that category.

## Why it matters today

Almost **half the human genome** comes from transposable elements. Most are inactive, but some cause disease by inserting into a gene (cases of haemophilia and muscular dystrophy caused this way have been described), and others have been "recycled" by evolution to regulate genes. Transposons are also used today as **tools** to introduce genes into cells in experimental therapies.
