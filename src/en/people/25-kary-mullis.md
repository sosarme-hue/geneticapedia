## Who he was

Kary Banks Mullis was born in 1944 in Lenoir, North Carolina, and earned his PhD in biochemistry at the University of California, Berkeley. His path was unconventional: he wrote fiction, worked in a bakery and in 1979 joined **Cetus**, one of the first biotechnology companies, as a chemist synthesising short pieces of DNA.

## The idea

By his own account, in April 1983, driving at night to his cabin in northern California, he thought of a way to **multiply** a specific piece of DNA. The **polymerase chain reaction** (PCR) repeats three steps:

1. **Denature**: the DNA is heated to about 95 °C to separate its two strands.
2. **Anneal**: it is cooled so that two **primers**, short tailor-made pieces of DNA, bind to the ends of the region of interest.
3. **Extend**: a DNA polymerase copies the region starting from the primers.

Each cycle **doubles** the amount of DNA. After 30 cycles, a single copy becomes more than a billion.

At first fresh enzyme had to be added every cycle, because heat destroyed it. In 1988 the Cetus team began using **Taq polymerase**, from the bacterium *Thermus aquaticus*, which lives in the hot springs of Yellowstone and withstands heat. PCR could then be **automated** with machines that change temperature by themselves.

## Recognition and controversy

In 1993 Mullis shared the **Nobel Prize in Chemistry** with Michael Smith. PCR was also the product of his team's work at Cetus, which refined it and first applied it to diagnosing sickle cell anaemia in 1985.

Mullis was an eccentric and controversial figure: he denied that HIV causes AIDS and doubted climate change and the ozone hole, positions with no scientific basis that set him against the scientific community. He died in 2019.

## Why it matters today

PCR is almost everywhere: diagnosing infections (the COVID-19 test was a PCR), genetic testing for inherited diseases, forensic medicine, paternity testing, detecting mutations in cancer and studying ancient DNA. It is one of the most widely used techniques in biology.
