## Who she is

Jennifer Anne Doudna was born in Washington, D.C., in 1964 and grew up in Hilo, Hawaii, where her father taught literature. At twelve she read Watson's *The Double Helix* and decided to become a scientist. She earned her PhD at Harvard with **Jack Szostak** (Nobel 2009) and specialised in **RNA structure**: she showed that some RNA molecules fold into complex shapes and act as enzymes (ribozymes). She has been a professor at the University of California, Berkeley, since 2002.

## CRISPR-Cas9

Around 2006 the microbiologist Jillian Banfield suggested she look at some repeated bacterial sequences, CRISPR, whose function was just beginning to be understood. Doudna's group analysed the Cas proteins that accompany them.

In 2011 she met **Emmanuelle Charpentier**, who was working on the Cas9 protein. Their laboratories, one in Berkeley and the other in Umeå (Sweden), collaborated at a distance. In June 2012 they reported in *Science* that Cas9, guided by a tailor-made **RNA**, cuts DNA at exactly the chosen point. It was a **simple, cheap and programmable** gene-editing tool, far easier than earlier ones.

Within months several groups, including Feng Zhang's at the Broad Institute and George Church's at Harvard, showed that it worked in **human cells**.

## Responsibility

Doudna has devoted a great deal of effort to the ethical dilemmas raised by her discovery. In 2015 she convened a meeting in Napa, California, and co-signed a call in *Science* for a **moratorium** on human germline editing, following the Asilomar model. When the Chinese researcher He Jiankui announced the birth of two girls from edited embryos in 2018, Doudna was among those who condemned the experiment. She founded the **Innovative Genomics Institute**, which works on CRISPR applications in health and agriculture with a focus on equitable access.

## Recognition

She received the **2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry** with Charpentier. Her life is told in Walter Isaacson's book *The Code Breaker* (2021).

## Why it matters today

CRISPR is used in thousands of laboratories to study gene function, create disease models and improve crops. The first approved treatment, **Casgevy**, for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassaemia, and trials of editing directly inside the body for diseases such as transthyretin amyloidosis or high cholesterol, all build on her work.
