## Who she was

Mary Frances Lyon was born in Norwich, England, in 1925. She studied zoology at the **University of Cambridge** at a time when women did not yet receive full degrees. She did her PhD in Ronald Fisher's department and then worked in Edinburgh and, from 1955, at the Medical Research Council's radiobiology unit at **Harwell**, where she studied the effects of radiation on mice and led the genetics division until she retired.

## Mottled mice

At Harwell, Lyon studied mice with coat-colour genes on the **X chromosome**. She noticed that females with one normal and one altered copy did not have an intermediate colour but a **mottled** coat: patches of one colour and patches of another.

She also knew two things: that a mouse with a single X (like women with [Turner syndrome](../../diseases/turner-syndrome/)) is a normal female, and that the Canadian Murray Barr had seen a small compact body in female cells, the **Barr body**, which Susumu Ohno had shown to be a condensed X chromosome.

## The Lyon hypothesis

In **1961** she published a one-page note in *Nature* with a revolutionary idea:

1. In each cell of a female, **one of the two X chromosomes is inactivated** early in embryonic development.
2. Which one, the father's or the mother's, is **random** in each cell.
3. Once decided, all daughter cells keep **the same X switched off**.

So females are a **mosaic** of cells. The best-known example is **tortoiseshell and calico cats**: the orange gene is on the X, and their orange and black patches reflect which X is active in each area. Almost all tortoiseshell cats are female.

## Why it matters today

**X inactivation**, also called lyonisation, explains why women who carry X-linked conditions such as [Duchenne muscular dystrophy](../../diseases/duchenne-muscular-dystrophy/), [haemophilia](../../diseases/haemophilia/) or [Fabry disease](../../diseases/tay-sachs-and-lysosomal-diseases/) may have mild or variable symptoms, and why [Rett syndrome](../../diseases/rett-syndrome/) affects each girl so differently. It was one of the first examples of large-scale [epigenetic](../../heredity/epigenetics/) regulation, and it is now known to be controlled by an RNA called *XIST*.
