## Who he was

Gregor Johann Mendel was born in 1822 into a peasant family in Heinzendorf, Silesia. For a poor boy, the only route to an education was the Church, so in 1843 he entered the Augustinian monastery of St Thomas in Brno. The monastery was an intellectual centre, with a library, an experimental garden and friars who taught at the university. Between 1851 and 1853 Mendel studied physics, mathematics and botany at the University of Vienna, where he learned something unusual for a naturalist: **to count, and to think in probabilities**.

## The experiment

Between 1856 and 1863 he grew and crossed tens of thousands of pea plants (*Pisum sativum*). It was a good choice: peas self-pollinate, grow quickly and have traits that come in two clear forms. Mendel followed **seven characters**: seed shape and colour, flower colour, pod shape and colour, flower position and stem height.

He first obtained pure-breeding lines. Then he crossed them and counted the offspring over several generations. In the first generation only one form appeared (the **dominant** one); in the second, the hidden form (the **recessive** one) reappeared in a remarkably constant proportion of about **3 to 1**.

His explanation was revolutionary: each plant carries **two "factors"** for each trait, one from each parent, and passes them on **separately** to its offspring. Today we call those factors **genes**, and their versions **alleles**.

He presented his results to the Natural History Society of Brno in 1865 and published them in 1866 as *Versuche über Pflanzen-Hybriden* ("Experiments on plant hybrids").

## Oblivion and rediscovery

The work went almost unnoticed for 34 years. The mathematics put botanists off, the idea of discrete units clashed with the prevailing belief in "blending inheritance", and his later experiments with hawkweed (*Hieracium*) seemed to contradict him; we now know that this plant often reproduces without fertilisation. In 1868 Mendel was elected abbot, and running the monastery absorbed him.

In 1900 three botanists, Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak, reached similar conclusions and found Mendel's paper. He has been regarded as the **founder of genetics** ever since.

## Why it matters today

Mendel's laws still explain how **single-gene (monogenic)** conditions are inherited, such as cystic fibrosis (recessive) or Huntington's disease (dominant). When a geneticist draws a family tree and calculates a 25 % or 50 % risk, they are using Mendel's reasoning. The exceptions to his laws (linked genes, polygenic inheritance, the influence of environment) are also the starting point of modern genetics.
