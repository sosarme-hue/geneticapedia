## Who he was

Hugo de Vries was born in Haarlem in the Netherlands in 1848, into a family of academics and politicians. He studied botany at Leiden and worked in Germany before becoming a professor at the **University of Amsterdam**, where he spent almost his whole career. He first studied plant physiology, such as water pressure in cells, and coined the term **plasmolysis**.

## Pangenes

In **1889** he published *Intracellular Pangenesis*, proposing that the traits of living things depend on independent particles he called **pangenes**, which pass from parents to children and can combine in new ways. Years later the word **gene**, proposed by the Dane Wilhelm Johannsen in 1909, would derive from it.

## Rediscovering Mendel

During the 1890s De Vries crossed many plant varieties and saw that some traits were distributed among offspring in fixed ratios, such as **3 to 1**. In **1900** he published his results and, on reviewing the literature, found that an unknown monk, **[Gregor Mendel](../gregor-mendel/)**, had reached the same conclusions 35 years earlier. That same year the German Carl Correns and the Austrian Erich von Tschermak published similar findings. All three "rediscovered" Mendel, and 1900 is regarded as the birth year of genetics.

## Mutation theory

Studying evening primrose (*Oenothera lamarckiana*), De Vries noticed that plants with new and very different traits occasionally appeared and passed them on. He called them **mutations** and proposed, in *The Mutation Theory* (1901-1903), that new species arose suddenly through such jumps.

We now know that most of his *Oenothera* "mutations" were in fact unusual chromosome rearrangements, and that evolution proceeds mainly by the accumulation of small changes and [natural selection](../../human-evolution/out-of-africa/). But his central idea, that hereditary changes arise spontaneously and are the raw material of evolution, became a pillar of biology. The word he chose is used every day: see [mutations and variants](../../basics/mutations-and-variants/).

## Why it matters today

De Vries helped unite two ideas that then seemed at odds: Mendel's heredity and Darwin's evolution. That union, completed in the 1930s and 1940s, is the foundation of modern genetics.
