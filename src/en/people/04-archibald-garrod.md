## Who he was

Archibald Edward Garrod was the son of Alfred Garrod, the physician who described uric acid in gout. He trained at Oxford and St Bartholomew's Hospital in London, and also worked at Great Ormond Street, London's great children's hospital. He was interested in the **chemistry of urine**, which was unusual among clinicians of his time.

## The discovery

**Alkaptonuria** is a rare condition in which urine turns black on contact with air; over the years, dark pigment builds up in cartilage and causes arthritis. Garrod studied several affected families and noticed two things:

- The condition appeared in **siblings**, but not in their parents.
- Many of those parents were **first cousins**.

The biologist William Bateson, Mendel's champion in England, pointed out that this pattern fitted a **recessive trait**: the parents each carried a "silent" copy and, being related, were more likely to share it. In 1902 Garrod published "The incidence of alkaptonuria: a study in chemical individuality" in *The Lancet*. It was the **first human disease** explained by Mendel's laws, barely two years after their rediscovery.

In 1908, in his Croonian Lectures to the Royal College of Physicians, he extended the idea to three more conditions (cystinuria, pentosuria and albinism) and proposed that each was due to the **lack of a specific enzyme** in a metabolic pathway. He called them *inborn errors of metabolism*, a name still in use.

## Ahead of his time

Garrod went further: he argued that each person has an inherited "**chemical individuality**" that would explain why we respond differently to foods, drugs or infections. This is the idea behind pharmacogenetics and personalised medicine today.

His work had little impact until Beadle and Tatum formulated the "one gene, one enzyme" hypothesis in 1941. In 1958 the missing enzyme in alkaptonuria was shown to be **homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase**, and in 1996 a Spanish team identified the gene, *HGD*. The first specific treatment, nitisinone, was approved in Europe in 2020.

## Why it matters today

Garrod is the father of **biochemical genetics**. Newborn screening for inborn errors such as phenylketonuria grew out of his way of thinking.
