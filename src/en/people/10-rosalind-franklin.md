## Who she was

Rosalind Elsie Franklin was born in London in 1920 into a well-off Jewish family. She studied chemistry at Cambridge and earned her doctorate on the structure of **coal**, work that was very useful to industry during the war. Between 1947 and 1950 she worked in Paris, where she became an expert in **X-ray diffraction**, a technique that reveals how the atoms of a substance are arranged from the pattern X-rays leave when they pass through it.

## At King's College

In January 1951 she arrived at King's College London to study DNA. Confusion over who was leading the project set her at odds from the start with **Maurice Wilkins**, who saw her as an assistant rather than an independent researcher.

With her PhD student **Raymond Gosling**, Franklin greatly improved the samples and equipment. She discovered that DNA had **two forms**, a dry one (A) and a wet one (B), depending on humidity, and that the **phosphates were on the outside** of the molecule. In May 1952 they obtained an exceptional image of the B form, **Photo 51**, with a cross pattern that clearly indicates a **helix**.

## What happened in 1953

In January 1953 Wilkins showed Photo 51 to James Watson without Franklin's knowledge. Shortly afterwards, Max Perutz passed Watson and Crick a report from his laboratory containing Franklin's measurements. These data were key to the Cambridge model.

On 25 April 1953 *Nature* published **three papers in a row**: Watson and Crick's model, one by Wilkins and colleagues, and one by **Franklin and Gosling** with data that confirmed it. Recent research using documents from the period, such as the 2023 *Nature* article by Matthew Cobb and Nathaniel Comfort, describes Franklin not as the victim of a "theft" but as an **equal collaborator** whose role was obscured for decades, partly by Watson's account in *The Double Helix* (1968).

## After DNA

In 1953 she moved to Birkbeck College and studied the structure of **viruses**, such as tobacco mosaic virus and poliovirus, with first-rate results. Her collaborator **Aaron Klug** received the 1982 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for continuing that line of work. Franklin died of **ovarian cancer** in April 1958, aged 37. The 1962 Nobel Prize is not awarded posthumously.

## Why it matters today

Franklin has become a symbol of women scientists whose work was not recognised in time. But her legacy is above all scientific: experimental rigour, data of exceptional quality and evidence-based reasoning. Institutes, universities and the European Space Agency's Mars rover bear her name.
