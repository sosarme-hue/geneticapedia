## Who he was

Thomas Hunt Morgan was born in Kentucky in 1866. He trained as an embryologist and in 1904 joined Columbia University in New York. At first he was **sceptical** of Mendel's laws and of the idea, proposed by Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri around 1903, that hereditary "factors" travelled on chromosomes. He wanted experimental proof.

## The fly room

To find it he chose a small, cheap and very prolific animal: the **fruit fly**, *Drosophila melanogaster*. A pair produces hundreds of offspring in about ten days, and it has only four pairs of chromosomes. His laboratory, the famous *fly room*, was a narrow room full of milk bottles of flies fed on bananas.

In 1910 a **white-eyed male** appeared instead of the usual red. When Morgan bred it, the trait was not shared equally between males and females: almost only males showed it. The explanation was that the eye-colour gene lay on the **X chromosome**, of which males have only one copy. It was the first time a specific gene had been linked to a specific chromosome: **sex-linked inheritance**.

## The team

Morgan surrounded himself with brilliant students. **Alfred Sturtevant**, aged only 19, realised in 1913 that how often two traits separated during inheritance reflected the **distance** between their genes, and drew the **first genetic map**. **Calvin Bridges** used errors in chromosome sorting to prove that genes lie on them, and **Hermann Muller** would later discover that X-rays cause mutations (Nobel Prize, 1946).

In 1915 they published *The Mechanism of Mendelian Heredity*, which joined Mendel's laws with chromosome biology: the **chromosome theory of inheritance**.

## Recognition

Morgan received the **1933 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine** "for his discoveries concerning the role played by the chromosome in heredity". It was the first Nobel awarded to a geneticist. He shared the prize money with Sturtevant and Bridges.

## Why it matters today

*Drosophila* remains one of biology's most widely used models: more than half of human disease-related genes have a counterpart in the fly. And the concepts of **linkage** and **recombination** born in the fly room are what allowed researchers, decades later, to locate the genes for Huntington's disease and cystic fibrosis.
