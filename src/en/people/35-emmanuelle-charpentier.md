## Who she is

Emmanuelle Marie Charpentier was born in 1968 in Juvisy-sur-Orge, near Paris. She studied biochemistry, microbiology and genetics at Pierre and Marie Curie University and earned her doctorate at the Pasteur Institute, studying how bacteria become resistant to antibiotics. Her career has been very itinerant: New York, Memphis, Vienna, Umeå (Sweden), Braunschweig and Berlin, where since 2018 she has led the Max Planck Unit for the Science of Pathogens.

## The missing piece

Charpentier studied *Streptococcus pyogenes*, the bacterium behind sore throats and other infections. Thanks to Francisco Mojica and others, it was known that bacteria use CRISPR to defend themselves against viruses, and that a protein, **Cas9**, cut the invader's DNA. But it was not clear how it was guided.

In 2011 her group discovered a previously unknown RNA molecule, **tracrRNA**, essential for the guide RNA to mature and for Cas9 to work. She published it in *Nature*.

## Genetic scissors

That same year she met **Jennifer Doudna** at a conference in Puerto Rico and they began to collaborate. In June 2012 they reported in *Science* that Cas9, together with two RNAs, cut DNA at the site specified by the guide RNA. And they took a decisive step: they **fused** the two RNAs into one, easy to design. By changing a few letters of the guide RNA, Cas9 could be directed to **any DNA sequence**. The bacterial system had become a gene-editing tool.

## Recognition

In 2020 Charpentier and Doudna received the **Nobel Prize in Chemistry** "for the development of a method for genome editing". It was the first time two women had shared a science Nobel with no other laureates. The CRISPR patent was the subject of a long dispute between the University of California (Doudna and Charpentier) and the Broad Institute (Feng Zhang), which demonstrated its use in human cells in 2013.

## Why it matters today

Between November 2023 and February 2024 the first CRISPR-based medicine, **Casgevy** (exagamglogene autotemcel), was approved in the UK, the US and the European Union for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassaemia. Charpentier remains devoted to basic research on pathogenic bacteria, and has warned about the risks of editing human embryos.
