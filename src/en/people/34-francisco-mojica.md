## Who he is

Francisco Juan Martínez Mojica was born in Elche, Spain, in 1963. He studied biology at the University of Alicante, where he has spent his whole career. For his thesis he studied *Haloferax mediterranei*, a microorganism (an archaeon) that lives in the extremely salty water of the **Santa Pola salt marshes**.

## Strange repeats

In 1993, while sequencing part of that microbe's genome, Mojica found something curious: a short sequence **repeated many times**, separated by different fragments of regular length. Digging further, he found such repeats in dozens of very different bacteria and archaea, which suggested they must have an important function.

Around 2001, in correspondence with the Dutch researcher **Ruud Jansen**, he proposed a name for these regions: **CRISPR**, for "clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats".

## The key insight

In August 2003, comparing the spacer fragments with databases, Mojica discovered that many matched the DNA of **viruses** and **plasmids** that infect those bacteria. And bacteria carrying a virus's spacer were **immune** to that virus. He concluded that CRISPR was an **adaptive immune system** of microbes: they kept "snapshots" of their invaders in order to recognise and destroy them.

Several top journals rejected the paper. It was published in 2005 in the *Journal of Molecular Evolution*. In 2007 an experiment in the yoghurt industry confirmed that bacteria acquire resistance to viruses by incorporating spacers.

## Recognition

His work was the foundation on which Charpentier and Doudna developed the gene-editing tool in 2012. Mojica did not share the 2020 Nobel Prize, but his role is widely recognised: in 2017 he shared the **Albany Prize** in Medicine with Charpentier, Doudna, Luciano Marraffini and Feng Zhang, and his name has repeatedly been mentioned for major international awards.

## Why it matters today

The CRISPR story began with a **basic science** question about a salt-marsh microbe. Mojica often tells it as an argument for funding research with no immediate application.
