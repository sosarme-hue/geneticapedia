## Who he was

Johannes Friedrich Miescher was born in Basel in 1844 into a family of physicians. He studied medicine, but partial deafness after typhoid made him doubt his future as a clinician. His uncle, the anatomist Wilhelm His, encouraged him to do research instead: he believed the great problems of biology would be solved by understanding the **chemistry of cells**.

In 1868 Miescher went to Tübingen, to the laboratory of Felix Hoppe-Seyler, one of the founders of biochemistry, housed in the old kitchen of a castle.

## The discovery

Miescher wanted to know what **white blood cells** were made of. He needed a lot of cells, and the richest source was the **pus** on bandages sent from a nearby hospital. He washed the bandages, separated the cells and studied their components.

By treating the nuclei with alkaline and then acidic solutions he obtained a substance that behaved like no known protein: it was rich in **phosphorus**, contained no sulphur and resisted pepsin, an enzyme that digests proteins. He concluded that it was a new kind of molecule, specific to the nucleus, and called it **nuclein**. It was DNA, still bound to some proteins.

Hoppe-Seyler, cautious, repeated the experiments before letting him publish. The paper appeared in 1871.

## Afterwards

Back in Basel, Miescher swapped pus for **salmon sperm** from the Rhine, fished in winter, and worked at dawn in unheated rooms so that the substance would not degrade. He found nuclein in the nucleus of every cell he analysed and even suspected it might play a role in fertilisation and heredity, although he eventually dismissed the idea: the molecule seemed too monotonous to carry so much information.

He died of tuberculosis in 1895, aged 51. His student Richard Altmann coined the term **nucleic acid** in 1889.

## Why it matters today

Miescher found the molecule, but it took 75 more years to show that it was the hereditary material (Avery, 1944) and 84 to learn its shape (1953). His story shows how science advances in layers: without that first chemical isolation there would have been nothing to study later. The original Tübingen laboratory is preserved as a museum.
