## Who she was

Marthe Gautier was born in 1925 in Montenils, a village in the Champagne region, into a farming family. She studied medicine in Paris and specialised in **paediatrics** and **paediatric cardiology**. In 1955 she spent a year at Harvard University, where she learned to **culture cells** in the laboratory, a technique still rare in France.

## The discovery

In 1956 it had been established that humans have **46 chromosomes**. On her return to Paris, Gautier joined the department of Professor **Raymond Turpin** at the Trousseau hospital, who had long suspected that "mongolism", as Down syndrome was then called, had a chromosomal cause. Nobody in the department knew how to count chromosomes.

Gautier set up a small laboratory with very modest means, partly paid for out of her own pocket. She cultured **fibroblasts** from skin biopsies of children with Down syndrome and, in May 1958, saw **47 chromosomes** in her preparations: there was an extra small one. As she had no microscope with a camera, the young researcher **Jérôme Lejeune**, who had joined the group, took the slides away to photograph them.

## Sharing the credit

In January 1959 the note was published by the French Academy of Sciences. Lejeune signed **first**, Gautier second, with her surname misspelt ("Gauthier"), and Turpin third. Lejeune presented the finding at conferences and received the international honours. Gautier, sidelined, returned to paediatric cardiology and went on to lead an Inserm research unit on childhood liver disease.

In 2009, at 83, she published her account of events with the geneticist Peter Harper. In 2014 Inserm's ethics committee concluded that her role had been **essential** and that the discovery would not have been possible without her. She died in 2022, aged 96.

## Why it matters today

It was the **first human disorder** attributed to a chromosomal abnormality. It opened up clinical cytogenetics and, with it, prenatal diagnosis. Gautier's story is cited today as an example of the "Matilda effect", the tendency to credit men with women scientists' achievements.
