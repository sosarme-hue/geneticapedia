## Who he was

John Craig Venter was born in Salt Lake City in 1946. He was a poor student and, after high school, took up surfing in California. During the Vietnam War he served as a medical corpsman in a military hospital, an experience that, he said, made him decide to study medicine and science. He earned a PhD in physiology and pharmacology at the University of California, San Diego.

## Shortcuts to reading genes

At the NIH in the early 1990s he devised a method for finding genes quickly: sequencing short fragments of the RNAs cells were using, called **expressed sequence tags** (ESTs). The NIH's attempt to **patent** thousands of those fragments caused a major controversy.

In 1995, at his own institute (TIGR), his team published the **first complete genome of a free-living organism**, the bacterium *Haemophilus influenzae*, using **whole-genome shotgun sequencing**: breaking all the DNA into random fragments, sequencing them and reassembling the genome with powerful computers.

## The genome race

In 1998 he founded **Celera Genomics** and announced it would sequence the human genome in three years, faster and more cheaply than the public consortium. The competition spurred both on. In June 2000 Venter and Francis Collins jointly announced a draft, and in February 2001 they published their versions separately, in *Science* and *Nature*. In 2007 he published his own complete genome, with both copies of each chromosome: one of the first **individual** genomes.

## The synthetic cell

In 2010 his team at the J. Craig Venter Institute chemically synthesised the complete genome of a bacterium (*Mycoplasma mycoides*), transplanted it into another cell and got that cell to function and divide with the artificial DNA. They called it **JCVI-syn1.0**. In 2016 they created a cell with a minimal genome of only 473 genes, to find out what is essential for life.

Venter died in April 2026.

## Why it matters today

Shotgun sequencing and massive computational analysis are the basis of today's **genomics**, from studying ocean microbes to clinical exome and genome analysis. His work also opened debates on gene **patents** and synthetic biology.
