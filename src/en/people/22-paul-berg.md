## Who he was

Paul Berg was born in New York in 1926, the son of immigrants. He served in the Navy during the Second World War and earned his PhD in biochemistry at Western Reserve University in Cleveland. He worked with **Arthur Kornberg**, the discoverer of DNA polymerase, and followed him to **Stanford University** in 1959, where he spent the rest of his career.

## The first recombinant DNA

In the late 1960s Berg wanted to understand how some viruses cause cancer by inserting their genes into cells. To study this, he had the idea of using a monkey virus, **SV40**, as a "vehicle" to carry foreign genes into mammalian cells.

In 1972, with David Jackson and Robert Symons, he cut the DNA of SV40 and that of a bacterial virus (phage lambda, carrying *E. coli* genes) and **joined** them with enzymes. It was the first **recombinant DNA** molecule: DNA from two different organisms combined in the laboratory.

## A pause to think

Before putting that molecule into bacteria, several colleagues warned him of a risk: SV40 causes tumours in rodents, and *E. coli* lives in the human gut. Berg **stopped the experiment**. In 1974 he and other scientists signed a letter in *Science* calling for a voluntary moratorium on certain experiments.

In February 1975 he organised the **Asilomar Conference** in California, where about 140 scientists, lawyers and journalists agreed safety rules according to the risk of each experiment. Asilomar became the model of how a scientific community can **regulate itself** when faced with a new technology. It was invoked again in 2015, when gene editing with CRISPR raised similar dilemmas.

## Recognition

In 1980 he received half of the **Nobel Prize in Chemistry** "for his fundamental studies of the biochemistry of nucleic acids, with particular regard to recombinant-DNA". The other half went to Walter Gilbert and Frederick Sanger for DNA sequencing.

## Why it matters today

Recombinant DNA gave us **human insulin** made by bacteria (1982), growth hormone, clotting factors for haemophilia, many vaccines and monoclonal antibodies. And the Asilomar debate is still alive in the ethics of gene editing.
