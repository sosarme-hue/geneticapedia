## Who he was

Frederick Sanger was born in 1918 in Rendcomb, England, the son of a Quaker doctor. He studied natural sciences at Cambridge and, as a conscientious objector, did his doctorate during the Second World War. He worked in Cambridge all his life, from 1962 at the Medical Research Council's Laboratory of Molecular Biology. He described himself as "just a chap who messed about in a lab".

## First Nobel: reading a protein

In the 1940s nobody knew whether proteins had a fixed sequence of amino acids. Sanger chose **insulin**, a small protein that was easy to obtain. For ten years he cut it into fragments, labelled their ends with a dye and pieced the order together like a jigsaw. By 1955 he had determined the complete sequence of its **51 amino acids**.

He thus showed that every protein has a **unique, exact sequence**, an idea Crick used to formulate the sequence hypothesis and the central dogma. He received the **1958 Nobel Prize in Chemistry**.

## Second Nobel: reading DNA

He then moved on to nucleic acids. In 1977, with Steve Nicklen and Alan Coulson, he published the **chain-termination** or **dideoxy method**. The idea: DNA is copied in the presence of modified "letters" that stop the copying at random. This yields fragments of every length, each ending in a known letter; sorting them by size reveals the sequence.

That same year his team published the **first complete genome** of a DNA-based organism, the virus phiX174 (5,386 bases). In 1980 he received his **second Nobel Prize in Chemistry**, shared with Walter Gilbert and Paul Berg. He is one of only five people with two Nobel Prizes, and was the only one with two in Chemistry until 2022.

## Why it matters today

**Sanger sequencing**, automated with fluorescent dyes, was the method used in the **Human Genome Project**. Although most analyses now use massively parallel sequencing, Sanger sequencing is still used in clinical laboratories to **confirm** specific variants and study single genes. Britain's great genomics centre is named after him: the **Wellcome Sanger Institute**.

Sanger retired in 1983 and spent his last thirty years gardening. He declined a knighthood because he did not want to be addressed differently.
