## Who he was

Maurice Hugh Frederick Wilkins was born in 1916 in Pongaroa, New Zealand, and moved to England with his family at the age of six. He studied physics at Cambridge. During the Second World War he worked on separating uranium isotopes for the **Manhattan Project** at Berkeley. The use of the atomic bomb affected him deeply, and after the war he decided to move into **biology**, like other physicists of his generation, influenced by Erwin Schrödinger's book *What Is Life?* (1944).

## The first images of DNA

At King's College London in 1950, Wilkins and his student **Raymond Gosling** prepared very thin, ordered fibres of DNA from a high-quality sample donated by the Swiss chemist Rudolf Signer. With them they obtained the first X-ray diffraction images suggesting a **regular, crystalline** structure. At a conference in Naples in 1951, one of those images caught the interest of a young James Watson.

## A difficult collaboration

In 1951 **Rosalind Franklin** joined the laboratory, hired by its director, John Randall, to take charge of DNA diffraction. Wilkins thought she had come to assist him; Franklin understood the project was hers. The misunderstanding and their very different personalities created a tense relationship.

In January 1953 Wilkins showed Watson Franklin and Gosling's **Photo 51** without consulting her. That gesture has become one of the most debated episodes in the history of science. After Watson and Crick's model was published, Wilkins and his team spent years confirming the structure more precisely.

## Recognition

In 1962 he shared the **Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine** with Watson and Crick "for their discoveries concerning the molecular structure of nucleic acids and its significance for information transfer in living material". In his autobiography, *The Third Man of the Double Helix* (2003), he acknowledged Franklin's role and regretted not having been able to work with her.

## Why it matters today

Wilkins represents the entry of **physics** into biology, which gave rise to structural biology. The diffraction techniques he helped develop are used today to determine the shape of proteins and to design drugs.
