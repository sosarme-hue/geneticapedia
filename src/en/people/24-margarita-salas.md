## Who she was

Margarita Salas Falgueras was born in 1938 in Canero, Asturias. She studied chemistry at the Complutense University of Madrid. One summer in Gijón she met **Severo Ochoa**, who advised her to do her thesis with **Alberto Sols**. Between 1964 and 1967 she worked with her husband, **Eladio Viñuela**, in Ochoa's laboratory in New York, where among other things she showed the direction in which messenger RNA is read.

## Doing science in Spain in 1967

Back in Madrid, Salas and Viñuela created one of the country's first **molecular biology** groups, with very few resources. They chose a simple model: **bacteriophage phi29**, a virus that infects the bacterium *Bacillus subtilis*. Salas used to say that at first she was called "Viñuela's wife"; when he changed field and she carried on alone with phi29, people began calling him "Margarita Salas's husband".

## What she discovered

Her group discovered that phi29 DNA starts copying itself in a unique way: a **terminal protein** attached to the end of the DNA acts as a "primer". They also studied its **DNA polymerase**, the enzyme that copies the virus's DNA, which turned out to be extraordinary:

- It copies **very long** stretches of DNA without letting go.
- It is very **accurate**, because it proofreads its own errors.
- It separates the two DNA strands by itself, without help from other proteins.

These properties make it possible to **amplify** tiny amounts of DNA, even from a single cell, at a constant temperature. The patent, filed by the Spanish National Research Council (CSIC) in 1989, was for years the most profitable in its history. **Multiple displacement amplification**, based on this enzyme, is used in forensics, single-cell genomics and the study of microbes that cannot be cultured.

## Recognition

She worked at the **Severo Ochoa Molecular Biology Centre** (CSIC-UAM) until her death in 2019. She was the first Spanish woman in the US **National Academy of Sciences** (2007) and a member of the Royal Spanish Academy. She trained several generations of researchers and was a constant advocate of investment in basic science: "without research there is no future".

## Why it matters today

The story of phi29 shows that **basic science**, studying a bacterial virus out of pure curiosity, can end up providing tools used worldwide in medicine and biotechnology.
