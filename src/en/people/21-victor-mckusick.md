## Who he was

Victor Almon McKusick was born in 1921 on a farm in Maine, one of twin brothers. He studied medicine at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, where he spent his whole career. He started as a **cardiologist**, interested in heart murmurs, and came to genetics from there.

## From Marfan to medical genetics

Studying patients with aortic problems, McKusick focused on **Marfan syndrome**, an inherited connective tissue disorder. In 1956 he published *Heritable Disorders of Connective Tissue*, the first book to group and describe these conditions in detail. In 1957 he founded the **Division of Medical Genetics** at Johns Hopkins, one of the first in the world, and trained hundreds of clinical geneticists.

He also studied the **Amish** communities of Pennsylvania, whose isolation and detailed genealogies make it possible to find rare recessive diseases. There he described several syndromes, such as chondroectodermal dysplasia (Ellis-van Creveld syndrome).

## The catalogue

In 1966 he published *Mendelian Inheritance in Man* (MIM), a catalogue of all known inherited traits and diseases in humans, each with its own number. The first edition had about 1,500 entries. In 1987 it became an online database, **OMIM** (*Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man*), now maintained by Johns Hopkins and holding more than 25,000 entries on genes and phenotypes.

MIM numbers appear in scientific papers and genetic reports worldwide.

## Mapping the genome

McKusick was one of the first to call for a **map of human genes**. From 1973 he helped organise the international gene-mapping workshops, and in 1988 he was the first president of the **Human Genome Organisation** (HUGO). Thanks to his insistence, the Human Genome Project was designed from the outset to serve **medicine**.

## Why it matters today

Medical genetics as a clinical specialty, which diagnoses, informs and supports families, owes much to his work. And when a geneticist looks up what is known about a gene or a rare disease, one of the first stops is still OMIM.
