## Same drug, different responses

Two people take the same dose of the same medicine: for one it works, for another it does nothing and in a third it causes side effects. There are many reasons (age, weight, kidneys, liver, other drugs), and one of them is **genes**.

**Pharmacogenetics** studies how genetic variants influence the response to medicines. It is one of the applications of genetics that has reached clinical practice the most.

## How genes have an influence

- **Enzymes that metabolise the drug.** Cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP2D6, CYP2C19, CYP2C9…) transform many medicines. Depending on their variants, a person can be a **poor**, **normal** or **ultrarapid** metaboliser.
- **Transporters** that move the drug into or out of cells.
- **Targets** on which the drug acts.
- **Immune system**: some HLA variants predispose to severe allergic reactions.

## Poor, normal and ultrarapid metabolisers

| Type | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Poor metaboliser** | The drug builds up, or a prodrug is not activated | More side effects; with clopidogrel, less effect |
| **Intermediate** | Somewhere in between | Sometimes needs a dose adjustment |
| **Normal** | Expected response | Usual dose |
| **Ultrarapid** | The drug is cleared or activated too quickly | Codeine: too much morphine |

For *CYP2D6*, for example, between 5 and 10 % of Europeans are poor metabolisers, while ultrarapid metabolisers are much more common in some North African and Middle Eastern populations.

## Examples already in use

| Gene | Drug | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| ***DPYD*** | Fluoropyrimidines (5-fluorouracil, capecitabine), for several cancers | People with DPD deficiency can suffer severe or fatal toxicity. The European Medicines Agency recommends testing **before** starting treatment |
| ***HLA-B\*57:01*** | Abacavir, for HIV | Predicts a severe hypersensitivity reaction. Always tested before prescribing |
| ***HLA-B\*15:02*** | Carbamazepine | Risk of severe skin reactions, especially in people of South-East Asian origin |
| ***CYP2D6*** | Codeine and tramadol | Ultrarapid metabolisers produce too much morphine. That is why codeine is not recommended under 12 years of age |
| ***CYP2C19*** | Clopidogrel, an antiplatelet drug | Poor metabolisers activate it less well and may be less protected after a heart attack or stent |
| ***TPMT*** and ***NUDT15*** | Azathioprine and mercaptopurine | Risk of severe bone-marrow toxicity |

## Clinical guidelines

Two international consortia translate genetic results into dosing recommendations: **CPIC**, in the United States, and **DPWG**, of the Royal Dutch Pharmacists Association. The **PharmGKB** database (now ClinPGx) gathers their guidelines and the drug labels that mention genetic biomarkers. Many medicines authorised in Europe already include pharmacogenetic information in their summary of product characteristics.

## A trial that changed the debate

The European **PREPARE** trial (2023), with almost 7,000 patients in seven countries, including Spain, the UK and the Netherlands, tested 12 genes at once before prescribing and adapted prescriptions accordingly. Patients with a result relevant to their drug had about **30 % fewer** clinically relevant adverse reactions. It was the first large trial to show a benefit from the "pre-emptive panel" approach.

## In practice

Spain's NHS **common portfolio of genetic services**, updated in 2023, includes pharmacogenetic tests of proven clinical value, such as *DPYD* before fluoropyrimidines or *HLA-B\*57:01* before abacavir. In England, *DPYD* testing before fluoropyrimidines is also routine. If you are about to start one of these treatments, ask your doctor whether the test applies to you.

## A result for life

Unlike a blood test, your genotype does not change. A pharmacogenetic result can be useful for future drugs, so it should be recorded in your **medical record** and you should keep a copy.

## What it is not yet

Online tests are sold promising to choose "the ideal antidepressant" or the perfect diet. For some drugs there are useful guidelines, but for many others the evidence is still limited and the genetic result is just **one factor** among many. Decisions should be made with your doctor, who takes everything into account.
