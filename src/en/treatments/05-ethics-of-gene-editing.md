## Two kinds of editing, two debates

- **Somatic editing**: modifies cells in a person's body, such as blood or liver cells. The changes **are not inherited**. It is assessed like any other treatment: benefits, risks and price.
- **Germline or heritable editing**: modifies embryos, eggs or sperm. The changes would be in every cell of the person and **would be passed on to their descendants**. Here the debate goes much deeper.

## The "CRISPR babies" case

In **November 2018**, Chinese researcher **He Jiankui** announced the birth of twin girls whose embryos he had edited to inactivate the *CCR5* gene, intending to make them resistant to HIV. The scientific community condemned it almost unanimously:

- There was no medical need: other ways of preventing HIV transmission existed.
- The editing was imprecise and produced unintended changes.
- The families' consent was inadequate.
- It was done in secret, without oversight.

In 2019 a Chinese court sentenced him to **three years in prison**. The case prompted new rules in China and around the world.

## What happened next

The girls, known as Lulu and Nana, and a third baby born later, have not been publicly followed up. Analyses of the data he himself presented suggested that the editing was incomplete and that the girls might be mosaics, with some cells edited and others not, and with changes different from those intended. China later tightened its rules. He was released in 2022 and has announced new projects, without scientific backing.

## What the rules say

- The Council of Europe's **Oviedo Convention**, ratified by Spain, allows modification of the human genome only for preventive, diagnostic or therapeutic purposes and **only if it does not aim to introduce any modification in the genome of any descendants** (article 13).
- In the European Union, the clinical trials regulation prohibits trials that modify the germline genetic identity.
- In the UK, the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act does not allow genetically altered embryos to be used for treatment, with a specific exception for mitochondrial donation.
- In 2021 the **World Health Organization** published a governance framework and recommended a registry of all human genome editing studies.
- The international summits on human genome editing (Washington 2015, Hong Kong 2018 and London 2023) concluded that heritable editing **remains unacceptable** at this time.

## New debates

- In 2025, several cell and gene therapy societies called for a **ten-year moratorium** on heritable editing of human embryos.
- Also in 2025, private companies appeared claiming to research embryo editing to prevent disease, reopening the discussion on oversight of these techniques outside public systems.
- **Embryo selection using polygenic scores** (choosing the embryo with "less risk" of diabetes or "more" of some trait) is offered in some countries. Scientific societies advise against it because its predictive power is low and it raises ethical problems.

## Mitochondrial donation: a different case

Mitochondria have their own DNA, inherited from the mother. When a woman carries a serious mutation in that DNA, the nucleus of her egg or embryo can be transferred into a donor's egg or embryo with healthy mitochondria. The UK authorised this under licence in 2015, and in 2025 it was reported that **eight children** had been born using the technique, healthy or with mild problems at the time of the study. It does not edit genes, but it does change something heritable, so it has its own ethical debate.

## The underlying questions

- **Safety**: errors would be passed on to future generations.
- **Consent**: the people affected do not yet exist.
- **Justice**: if only the wealthy had access, inequalities could grow.
- **Enhancement versus treatment**: where is the line between avoiding a disease and "improving" traits such as height or intelligence? The latter, moreover, depends on thousands of genes and the environment, which makes it technically unfeasible.
- **Diversity and disability**: many disabled people warn of the risk of treating certain lives as worth less.

## An alternative that already exists

Families at high risk of passing on a serious condition can already use **preimplantation genetic testing**: embryos created by IVF are analysed and only those without the condition are transferred, without editing anything. See [Carrier and preimplantation testing](../../diagnosis/carrier-and-preimplantation-testing/).

## In Spain

The **Criminal Code** (article 159) punishes anyone who manipulates human genes in a way that alters the genotype for purposes other than eliminating or reducing serious defects or diseases. Law 14/2007 on biomedical research regulates research with embryos and stem cells, and Law 14/2006 on assisted reproduction governs which embryos may be transferred. Germline editing for reproductive purposes is not permitted.
