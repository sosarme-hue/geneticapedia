## "Genetic" does not mean "untreatable"

It is a widespread idea: if a disease is in the genes, nothing can be done. It is not true. Even if the DNA does not change, it is possible to act on **what the altered gene causes**: the substance that is missing, the one that builds up, the organ that suffers or the symptoms that limit daily life.

## Treatments aimed at the mechanism

They act on the specific cause of each condition, so they usually work only for that condition, or even only for certain variants.

| Strategy | How it works | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Diet** | Avoiding the substance the body cannot process | Phenylketonuria (low-phenylalanine diet), galactosaemia |
| **Enzyme replacement** | The missing enzyme is given intravenously | Gaucher, Fabry and Pompe diseases |
| **Protein replacement** | Supplying the protein that is not made | Clotting factors in haemophilia |
| **Protein modulators** | Improve how a faulty protein works | CFTR modulators in cystic fibrosis |
| **RNA drugs** | Change how a gene is read or silence its message | Nusinersen in spinal muscular atrophy; drugs that silence transthyretin in hereditary amyloidosis |
| **Reducing damage** | Lessen the main consequence | Statins and other lipid-lowering drugs in familial hypercholesterolaemia; beta-blockers or sartans in Marfan syndrome |

**Gene therapy** and **gene editing** go a step further: they try to correct or compensate for the gene. We explain them in [Gene therapy](../gene-therapy/) and [CRISPR explained](../crispr-explained/).

## Supportive care: just as important

For many genetic conditions there is still no targeted treatment. That does not mean nothing can be done. Good follow-up can completely change the course of the condition:

- **Preventing complications**: heart, eye, hearing or thyroid checks depending on the condition; vaccines; early detection of tumours in hereditary cancer syndromes.
- **Rehabilitation**: physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy and early intervention.
- Adapted **nutrition**.
- **Surgery** when needed, for example for a congenital heart defect or the aorta.
- **Psychological and social support** for the person and their family.
- **Coordination** between specialists, ideally in a reference unit.

In Spain there are National Health System **Reference Centres, Services and Units (CSUR)** for many rare diseases, and in Europe the **European Reference Networks (ERNs)**, which let hospitals in different countries discuss complex cases.

## Read the news carefully

- A treatment being **under investigation** does not mean it is available.
- Working in one condition or variant does not mean it works for all.
- A **registered trial** on ClinicalTrials.gov or the EU Clinical Trials Information System does not prove the treatment works: it only shows it is being studied.
- To check whether a medicine is authorised, look at the European Medicines Agency's EPAR pages or your national regulator (in Spain, **CIMA** at cima.aemps.es) and ask your care team.

## How RNA drugs work

Between the gene and the protein there is a messenger, RNA. Several families of drugs act right there:

- **Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs)**: short chains that stick to a specific RNA. They can get it discarded (like tofersen in ALS caused by *SOD1* mutations) or change how it is spliced, like **nusinersen** in spinal muscular atrophy, which makes the back-up gene *SMN2* produce full-length protein.
- **Small interfering RNA (siRNA)**: destroys a gene's messenger RNA. **Patisiran** and **vutrisiran** reduce abnormal transthyretin in hereditary amyloidosis; **inclisiran** lowers LDL cholesterol with two injections a year.
- **Small molecules that modify splicing**, such as **risdiplam** in spinal muscular atrophy, taken by mouth.

## Medicines made for one person

In 2019 the case of **Mila** was published: a girl with a rare form of Batten disease caused by a unique variant. A Boston team designed, in under a year, an oligonucleotide just for her, **milasen**. Since then initiatives have emerged to create "n-of-1" treatments for ultra-rare variants, with huge challenges in funding, regulation and evaluation, because a classic trial cannot be run with a single patient.

## Transplantation as a genetic treatment

**Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation** (bone marrow) replaces blood cells with those of a healthy donor and cures some inherited conditions, such as severe immunodeficiencies, beta-thalassaemia or sickle cell disease, if a matched donor is found. Liver transplantation is used in some metabolic disorders. Both carry significant risks and are reserved for specific cases.

## Orphan medicines and access

A medicine for a rare disease (fewer than 5 in 10,000 people in the EU) can obtain **orphan designation**, which gives incentives to its developer. After European authorisation, each country decides on pricing and reimbursement: in Spain the Interministerial Commission on Medicine Prices, which for the most expensive drugs uses **outcome-based payment** agreements, monitored through the **VALTERMED** registry. While a drug is not yet reimbursed, **compassionate use** or access in special situations is sometimes possible.
