## Short answer

It depends on what you are looking for. For **curiosity and genealogy** they can be interesting, especially for finding relatives. For **health**, they do not replace a medical test. And before sending off your saliva, it is worth knowing what happens to your data.

## What they do well

- **Finding relatives**: they compare your DNA with millions of users and fairly accurately detect cousins, half-siblings or biological parents.
- **Ancient lineages**: mitochondrial DNA (maternal line) and the Y chromosome (paternal line) point to large branches of humanity.

## What they do less well

- **Origin percentages** ("32 % Iberian, 15 % North African...") are **estimates** that compare your DNA with present-day reference populations. They change from one company to another and from one version to the next. There are no "pure" populations: we are all a mixture. See [human evolution](../../human-evolution/).
- **Health results** test only a few variants, can miss most risk variants and have false positives. A worrying result must be confirmed in a clinical laboratory, and a reassuring one rules nothing out.

## What to think about first

- **Privacy**: your DNA says things about your parents, siblings and children, who have not given permission. In 2023 one of the big companies suffered a data breach affecting millions of users, and in 2025 it filed for bankruptcy, raising questions about what would happen to its databases.
- **Family surprises**: many people have discovered that their father was not their biological father or that they had unknown siblings.
- **Police use**: some databases allow police to search for relatives of suspects. See [forensic genetics](../../forensic-genetics/).
