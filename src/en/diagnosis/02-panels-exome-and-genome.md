## Three levels of zoom

**Massively parallel sequencing** (or next-generation sequencing, NGS) reads millions of DNA fragments at the same time. It is used in three ways, from narrowest to broadest:

- **Gene panel**: analyses a group of genes chosen because they are known to cause a particular kind of problem, for example 30 hereditary cancer genes or 200 cardiomyopathy genes.
- **Exome**: analyses the exons of almost all genes, that is, the parts that code for proteins. It is about 1–2 % of the genome but contains most of the known variants that cause single-gene disorders.
- **Whole genome**: analyses practically all the DNA, including the regions that regulate genes.

## How the test is chosen

| | Panel | Exome | Genome |
|---|---|---|---|
| When it fits | Clear clinical suspicion of a group of disorders | Complex or undiagnosed presentation, especially in children | Undiagnosed after other tests, research and, increasingly, first-line in some hospitals |
| Unexpected findings | Few | More | Even more |
| Uncertain variants | Few | Many | Many |
| Interpretation | Simpler | Complex | Most complex |

In childhood rare diseases, exome or genome sequencing finds the cause in about **one in three** children tested, more when both parents are analysed too (trio testing). That two in three remain undiagnosed shows how much is still unknown.

## What they can miss

Every technique has blind spots. Depending on the method and the laboratory, the following may be poorly detected:

- **Repeat expansions**, such as those in Huntington's disease or fragile X syndrome, which need specific tests.
- **Deletions or duplications** of one or more exons.
- Changes in **highly repetitive** regions or in genes with near-identical "twins", such as *SMN1* and *SMN2* in spinal muscular atrophy.
- **Mosaicism**, when the variant is present in only some cells.
- Variants in **mitochondrial DNA**, if it is not included.
- Variants in **regulatory** regions or deep within introns, outside the exome.

That is why **a negative result does not exclude a genetic condition**: it means that with this test, and with what is known today, the cause has not been found.

## A result that can improve over time

New genes are discovered and variants reclassified every year. **Reanalysing** an exome or genome one or two years later finds a diagnosis in some of the cases that were previously left without an answer. Ask whether your hospital does this and how.

And always: any finding must be matched to the person's **symptoms** (the phenotype) and, if needed, confirmed with another technique.

## How it works, step by step

1. **DNA extraction**, usually from a blood sample.
2. **Library preparation**: the DNA is broken into fragments a few hundred bases long and tagged. For panels and exomes, only the regions of interest are "fished out" with probes.
3. **Sequencing**: the machine reads each fragment many times. **Depth** or coverage (for example 30× for a genome or 100× for an exome) says how many reads cover each position: the more, the more reliable.
4. **Alignment**: software places each fragment where it belongs by comparing it with the human **reference genome**.
5. **Variant calling**: differences from the reference are identified. A typical genome has 4–5 million; an exome, around 20,000–25,000.
6. **Filtering and interpretation**: variants that are common in the population are discarded and those that fit the symptoms and the inheritance pattern are prioritised. This is the hardest part and needs the most expertise.
7. **Confirmation** of the relevant finding, sometimes with another technique, and the **report**.

## Trio testing

Analysing the patient together with **both parents** greatly increases the yield. It shows whether a variant is **new** (*de novo*, absent in both parents), which is very common in severe neurodevelopmental disorders, and whether two variants in the same gene lie on different copies, as a recessive condition requires.

## The genome as a first test

Several health systems now use the whole genome as a first-line test in some situations:

- The UK **100,000 Genomes Project** found a diagnosis in about 25 % of the rare-disease patients analysed, and for some of them it changed treatment. Whole-genome sequencing is now part of the NHS Genomic Medicine Service.
- In **neonatal intensive care** units, rapid genome sequencing, with results in a few days, delivers diagnoses that change the management of critically ill babies.
- In Spain, the **IMPaCT-Genómica** programme of the Instituto de Salud Carlos III and several regional genomic-medicine plans are working to bring exome and genome sequencing into routine NHS practice.

## Turnaround and data

A panel usually takes from a few weeks to two months; an exome or genome, one to several months, except for urgent versions. A genome generates a huge amount of personal data, which must be stored with guarantees of **confidentiality** and can only be used for research with consent.

## Secondary findings

An exome or genome reads thousands of genes that have nothing to do with the reason for testing. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) recommends offering results for a list of **about 80 "actionable" genes**, in which knowing you carry a pathogenic variant makes prevention or treatment possible (such as *BRCA1*, the Lynch syndrome genes or those for familial hypercholesterolaemia). Europe is more cautious. What matters is that the person decides **before** testing whether they want this information.
