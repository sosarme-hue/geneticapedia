## Four different questions

Reproductive tests answer different questions, and it helps not to confuse them:

| Test | Question | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Carrier testing** | Are we at risk of having a child with a recessive condition? | Ideally before pregnancy |
| **Prenatal screening** | How likely is it that this pregnancy has a chromosome condition? | During pregnancy |
| **Prenatal diagnosis** | Does the fetus have this specific condition? | During pregnancy |
| **Preimplantation genetic testing** | Which IVF embryos do not have the family condition? | Before embryo transfer |

Prenatal screening and diagnosis are explained in [Newborn and prenatal screening](../newborn-and-prenatal-screening/).

## Being a carrier

We are all **carriers** of some recessive variants without knowing it. They do not affect us, because we have another working copy of the gene. The problem arises only if both members of a couple carry variants in **the same gene**: then each pregnancy has a **25 %** chance that the child inherits both altered copies.

## Who is offered carrier testing

In public health systems it is offered mainly when there is a reason:

- a relative with a recessive condition, such as cystic fibrosis or spinal muscular atrophy, or a known carrier;
- **consanguineous** couples;
- ancestries in which certain conditions are common, such as haemoglobin disorders in people of Mediterranean, African or Asian origin, or Tay-Sachs disease in Ashkenazi Jewish people;
- egg and sperm donors, who in Spain are tested under assisted-reproduction rules.

There are also **expanded panels** that test hundreds of recessive conditions at once, offered mostly privately and in fertility clinics. They identify more at-risk couples, but bear in mind that:

- a negative result **reduces** the risk but does not remove it: there is always a **residual risk**;
- variants of uncertain significance may appear;
- they should come with genetic counselling before and after.

## What options does an at-risk couple have?

Knowing in advance allows time to decide. The options, all legitimate, include:

- conceiving naturally and, if wished, having **prenatal diagnosis**;
- **IVF with preimplantation genetic testing**;
- using **donor gametes**;
- adoption, or not having children.

## Preimplantation genetic testing

It is done as part of IVF treatment. At 5 or 6 days of development, a few cells are taken from the layer of the embryo that will form the placenta and analysed for the family variant. Only embryos without the condition are transferred.

In Spain it is regulated by **Law 14/2006 on assisted human reproduction techniques**. It is allowed for serious hereditary conditions of early onset that cannot be cured; other cases need authorisation following a report from the **National Commission on Assisted Human Reproduction**. The Spanish NHS includes it in its service portfolio under defined criteria. In the UK, the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority (HFEA) keeps a list of conditions approved for testing.

## Expanded carrier screening

Until recently, only people with a family history or from higher-risk groups were tested. Today **expanded panels** examine hundreds of genes at once in anyone planning a pregnancy.

- In 2021 the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) recommended offering everyone planning a pregnancy carrier screening for **at least 113 genes** with a sufficient carrier frequency.
- European recommendations are more cautious: the European Society of Human Genetics stresses that screening must be voluntary, with counselling before and after.
- In Spain, the NHS offers carrier testing when there is an indication (family history, consanguinity, gamete donors), and many fertility clinics test donors and recipients routinely.

Almost everyone carries some recessive variant. What matters is whether **both** partners carry one **in the same gene**.

## What a preimplantation testing cycle looks like

1. **Preliminary study** of the family to know the variants exactly and design the test.
2. **Ovarian stimulation and IVF**, usually with sperm injection (ICSI).
3. **Biopsy** of a few cells from the trophectoderm (the part that will form the placenta) on day 5–6.
4. **Genetic analysis** of those cells while the embryos are frozen.
5. **Transfer** of an unaffected embryo in a later cycle.

Confirming the result with a prenatal test is recommended, because analysing a few cells has a small margin of error and the embryo may be **mosaic**.

## Screening for aneuploidy (PGT-A)

This is different from testing for a specific condition: it looks for abnormal chromosome numbers in order to choose embryos more likely to implant. Its value in increasing live-birth rates in the general IVF population **has not been demonstrated** and is debated. A large clinical trial found no better cumulative results in women with a good prognosis.

## Cases that need authorisation

For other purposes, such as selecting an embryo that is a **tissue match** for a sick sibling who needs a bone-marrow transplant, Spanish law requires case-by-case authorisation from the health authority, after a favourable report from the National Commission. Choosing the sex of an embryo for non-medical reasons is **not** allowed.
