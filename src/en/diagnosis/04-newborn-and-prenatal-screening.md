## Screening is not diagnosis

**Screening** looks, among many people without symptoms, for those who are more likely to have a disease. An abnormal result **is not a diagnosis**: it means another test is needed to confirm it.

## The heel-prick test

Newborn babies have a few drops of blood taken from the heel, usually between **48 and 72 hours** after birth. The aim is to detect serious, rare conditions early, because when treated from the start their outlook is much better.

In Spain, the National Health System's common portfolio includes a set of conditions screened in every region, among them:

- congenital hypothyroidism;
- phenylketonuria;
- cystic fibrosis;
- medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (MCADD);
- long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (LCHADD);
- glutaric aciduria type I;
- sickle cell disease.

That common list is being extended, and many regions already screen for quite a few more conditions. In the UK, the NHS newborn blood spot test currently covers nine conditions. Ask your maternity unit which ones are included.

## What happens if a result is abnormal

The sample is analysed in a reference laboratory, mainly by **tandem mass spectrometry**, which measures many substances at once in the same spot. If a result is abnormal, the family is called to repeat the test or carry out confirmatory studies. Most of these second tests are normal: the programme is designed not to miss any affected baby, even at the cost of some false alarms. If the diagnosis is confirmed, the baby is referred to a specialist follow-up unit.

Some health systems have added **spinal muscular atrophy** and **severe combined immunodeficiency**, because treatments now exist that work much better the earlier they are given.

## Criteria for screening a disease

Since the classic criteria of Wilson and Jungner (WHO, 1968), a disease is screened only if it is an important health problem, a reliable test exists, there is an **effective treatment** that works better if started early, and the programme's benefits outweigh its harms and costs. That is why not every disease that technology could detect is screened.

## Prenatal screening for chromosome conditions

During pregnancy, screening is offered to estimate the chance of **trisomies** such as 21 (Down syndrome), 18 and 13.

**First-trimester combined screening** (weeks 11 to 13): combines the mother's age, the **nuchal translucency** measured by ultrasound and two substances in the mother's blood (PAPP-A and free beta-hCG). It gives a risk, for example "1 in 1,500".

**Cell-free fetal DNA in the mother's blood** (also called non-invasive prenatal testing, NIPT): analyses fragments of placental DNA circulating in the mother's blood, from week 10. It detects about **99 % of trisomy 21 cases** with very few false positives, but it is still screening: a positive result must be **confirmed** with a diagnostic test.

## Fetal DNA in public health systems

In Spain, cell-free DNA testing is offered in the NHS mainly as a **second-tier** test when combined screening gives an intermediate or high risk, although some regions have extended it. In England, NIPT is offered to people with a higher-chance result from combined screening. A high-risk result must always be confirmed by amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling before any decision is taken, because the DNA analysed comes from the placenta, which occasionally does not match the fetus.

## Prenatal diagnostic tests

- **Chorionic villus sampling** (from week 11): takes a sample of the placenta.
- **Amniocentesis** (from week 15): takes a sample of amniotic fluid.

These are the tests that give a diagnosis. They carry a small risk of miscarriage, below 0.5 % according to recent studies, so they are offered when screening shows a high risk or there is a family history.

## The 20-week scan

The **anomaly scan** looks for structural differences (in the heart, neural tube, kidneys and so on). If one is found, genetic testing of the fetus may be offered: a **chromosomal microarray** or, increasingly, a **prenatal exome**, which finds a genetic cause in some fetuses with anomalies and a normal karyotype.

## Personal decisions

Prenatal screening is **voluntary**. Some families want all the information and others prefer not to have it. Both choices are legitimate and must be respected, with clear information and without pressure.
