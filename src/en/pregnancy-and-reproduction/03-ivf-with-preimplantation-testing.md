## What it is

**Preimplantation genetic testing** combines two techniques: **in vitro fertilisation** (IVF) and **genetic testing** of the resulting embryos. This makes it possible to choose for transfer to the womb only embryos that **have not inherited** the family's condition. It spares the couple from having to consider ending a pregnancy after a prenatal diagnosis.

In **1990**, the team of **Alan Handyside** and Robert Winston at Hammersmith Hospital in London achieved the first pregnancies after testing embryos from couples at risk of X-linked conditions: they selected female embryos, which could not be affected. Since then hundreds of thousands of children have been born worldwide using this technique.

## Types

- **PGT-M** (monogenic): looks for the specific variant of a family condition, such as [cystic fibrosis](../../diseases/cystic-fibrosis/), [Huntington's disease](../../diseases/huntingtons-disease/) or *BRCA1*.
- **PGT-SR** (structural rearrangements): for carriers of balanced chromosome translocations, who have a higher risk of miscarriage.
- **PGT-A** (aneuploidy): counts the chromosomes of embryos. It is sometimes used in older women or after repeated miscarriage, but its usefulness for increasing live births is **highly debated** and professional societies do not recommend it routinely.

## How it works, step by step

1. **Preparatory work**: the family variant is confirmed and the laboratory prepares a test specific to that family, which can take several weeks.
2. **Ovarian stimulation**: the woman has hormone injections for about 10–12 days so that several eggs mature.
3. **Egg collection**: the eggs are retrieved with an ultrasound-guided needle, under sedation.
4. **Fertilisation**: in the laboratory, usually by **intracytoplasmic sperm injection** (ICSI), to avoid DNA from other sperm contaminating the test.
5. **Culture**: embryos grow for 5 or 6 days to the **blastocyst** stage, with about 100–200 cells.
6. **Biopsy**: a few cells are taken from the outer layer (the trophectoderm), which will form the placenta, not the baby.
7. **Genetic testing** of these cells, while the embryos are frozen.
8. **Transfer** of an unaffected embryo to the womb in a later cycle.

## Results

Not every couple gets healthy embryos at the first attempt. The number of embryos depends mainly on the **woman's age**, and in recessive conditions, on average one in four will be affected. The pregnancy rate per transfer of a healthy blastocyst is around **40–50 %** in young women, lower with age. Several cycles are often needed.

The techniques are not infallible: errors can occur because of the tiny amount of DNA analysed or **mosaicism** in the embryo. So confirming the result with prenatal diagnosis during pregnancy is usually recommended.

## What Spanish law allows

**Law 14/2006 on assisted human reproduction techniques** allows preimplantation testing to:

- Detect **serious hereditary conditions of early onset that cannot be cured**. In these cases authorisation from the clinic is enough.
- Other cases, such as **late-onset** conditions or those with incomplete penetrance (for example [hereditary cancer](../../diseases/hereditary-cancer/)), require authorisation from the health authority after a favourable report from the **National Commission on Assisted Human Reproduction**.
- Selecting compatible embryos to treat a sick sibling (the so-called **"saviour sibling"**), also authorised case by case. In 2008 the first Spanish child selected in this way was born in Seville, to treat his brother with a serious anaemia.

It is **not permitted** to choose sex for non-medical reasons or to select traits such as height or intelligence.

The public health system covers preimplantation testing for serious conditions, with waiting lists and age requirements that vary by region.

## The ethical questions

Preimplantation testing raises debates: which conditions are "serious enough", what to do with affected embryos, and the risk of sliding towards selection for non-medical traits. Some companies in other countries already offer "polygenic scores" for embryos to predict complex traits, a practice that European professional societies consider **lacking sufficient scientific basis** and ethically problematic. This is covered in more detail in [ethics of gene editing](../../treatments/ethics-of-gene-editing/).
