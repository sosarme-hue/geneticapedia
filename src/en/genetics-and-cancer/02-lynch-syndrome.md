| **Genes** | *MLH1*, *MSH2*, *MSH6*, *PMS2* and *EPCAM* (mismatch repair) |
| **Inheritance** | Autosomal dominant |
| **Frequency** | About 1 in 300 people |
| **Main cancers** | Bowel (colorectal) and endometrial; also ovary, stomach, small bowel, urinary tract |
| **Prevention** | Colonoscopy every 1–2 years, aspirin, preventive gynaecological surgery in some cases |

## What it is

**Lynch syndrome** is the most common form of hereditary bowel cancer. It is named after the American physician **Henry Lynch**, who in the 1960s insisted, against the scepticism of many colleagues, that some families had an inherited bowel cancer. It used to be called **hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer**, to distinguish it from familial adenomatous polyposis, in which hundreds of polyps form.

It accounts for about **3 %** of bowel and endometrial cancers. It is estimated that **more than 95 %** of people with Lynch syndrome do not know they have it.

## Cause: a proofreader that fails

When a cell copies its DNA, a letter sometimes pairs with the wrong partner, or letters are lost in repetitive stretches. A system of proteins called **mismatch repair** (MMR) checks the copy and corrects these slips, like the spellchecker in a word processor.

In Lynch syndrome, one of the two copies of a gene in this system is altered from birth. If a cell also loses the other copy (the "second hit"), it stops correcting errors and they build up very quickly. A typical sign is **microsatellite instability**: short repeated DNA sequences change length in the tumour.

The risk depends on the gene:

| Gene | Approximate bowel cancer risk to age 70–80 | Endometrial cancer risk |
|---|---|---|
| *MLH1* | 40–50 % | 35–45 % |
| *MSH2* | 35–50 % | 40–50 % |
| *MSH6* | 10–20 % | 40–50 % |
| *PMS2* | Under 10 % | 10–15 % |

These figures come from large family registries, such as the Prospective Lynch Syndrome Database, and vary between studies. In the general population the risk of bowel cancer is about 4–5 %.

## How it is found

There is now a very effective strategy: **testing all bowel and endometrial cancers** to see whether the repair proteins are present (using a technique called immunohistochemistry) or whether there is microsatellite instability. If a protein is missing and there is no other explanation, a **blood genetic test** is offered. This finds people with Lynch syndrome who would otherwise have been missed.

It is also suspected when a family has:

- Several people with bowel, endometrial or other Lynch-spectrum cancers.
- Cases diagnosed before 50.
- One person with two of these cancers.

When the variant is found in one person, testing is offered to relatives (**cascade testing**). Each brother, sister or child has a 50 % chance of having inherited it, and anyone who does not have it returns to the general population risk.

## Prevention and follow-up

- **Colonoscopy** every one or two years from age 25 (or from 30–35 with *MSH6* and *PMS2*, depending on the guidelines). It allows polyps to be removed before they become malignant, and has been shown to reduce deaths.
- **Aspirin**: the CAPP2 trial, with almost 900 people with Lynch syndrome, showed that taking aspirin for at least two years reduced the long-term risk of bowel cancer. European guidelines recommend considering it, weighing up the risk of bleeding.
- **Gynaecology**: people are told about warning signs such as irregular bleeding, and removal of the womb and ovaries may be offered once they have completed their family, usually from age 40.
- Stomach surveillance in some people.

## A treatment that works especially well

Tumours with microsatellite instability carry so many variants that they make many abnormal proteins, which the immune system recognises as foreign. This is why they respond very well to **immunotherapy** with PD-1 inhibitors. In 2015 a study by Dung Le and colleagues showed this, and in 2017 the US regulator approved a cancer drug for the first time based on a **genetic feature of the tumour** rather than the organ where it began.
