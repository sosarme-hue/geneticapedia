Forensic genetics

Famous cases solved with DNA

The Romanovs, Richard III, freed innocents and a miscarriage of justice in Spain: stories in which DNA changed the ending.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

The Romanov family

In July 1918 the last tsar of Russia, Nicholas II, his wife, their five children and four servants were killed by the Bolsheviks in Yekaterinburg. For decades legends of survivors circulated, and several women claimed to be the Grand Duchess Anastasia. The most famous, Anna Anderson, died in 1984 with the mystery unresolved.

In 1991 nine skeletons were exhumed in a nearby forest. In 1993–1994 Peter Gill's team in the UK compared their mitochondrial DNA with that of Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh, the tsarina's great-nephew through the maternal line, and confirmed they were the Romanovs. Two children were missing. In 2007 more remains were found and analyses in 2009 confirmed they belonged to the tsarevich Alexei and one of his sisters: no one survived. An analysis of Anna Anderson's tissue showed she was unrelated to the Romanovs: she was probably a Polish factory worker named Franziska Schanzkowska.

Richard III under a car park

In 2012 archaeologists found under a car park in Leicester the skeleton of a man with a curved spine and battle wounds. Was it Richard III, the English king killed in 1485? In 2014 Turi King's team compared his mitochondrial DNA with that of two living descendants of the king's sister, through the maternal line, and it matched. Together with other evidence, the probability that it was him was calculated as overwhelming. Richard III was reburied in Leicester Cathedral in 2015.

Curiously, his Y chromosome did not match that of the family's present-day male-line descendants, which shows that at some point in the paternal line a child was not fathered by the man believed to be the father.

Innocent people freed

The Innocence Project was founded in New York in 1992 to use DNA to review convictions. In the United States DNA has helped exonerate more than 375 people, many of whom had spent over a decade in prison and some of whom were on death row. In many cases the error was due to mistaken eyewitness identification, the most common cause of wrongful convictions.

A miscarriage of justice in Spain: the Wanninkhof case

In 1999 a young woman, Rocío Wanninkhof, disappeared in Mijas (Málaga). In 2001 Dolores Vázquez was convicted of her murder by a jury, on circumstantial evidence and amid intense media pressure. In 2003, after the murder of another young woman, Sonia Carabantes, in Coín, DNA analysis of traces found in both cases identified the perpetrator of both crimes: a British man, Tony Alexander King. Dolores Vázquez, already released after the trial was annulled, was definitively cleared. The case is often cited as an example of the dangers of trial by media.

DNA also resolves family stories

The Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo in Argentina have used DNA to identify more than 130 grandchildren stolen during the dictatorship. And millions of people have found biological parents, siblings or donors thanks to genealogy tests. See genetic genealogy.
