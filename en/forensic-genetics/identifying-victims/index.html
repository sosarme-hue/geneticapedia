Forensic genetics

Identifying victims

After disasters, attacks and wars, DNA gives back the names of those who lost them. From the stolen grandchildren of Argentina to the mass graves of the Spanish Civil War.

How a victim is identified

When a body cannot be recognised by sight, several techniques are combined: fingerprints, dental records, medical data (implants, fractures) and DNA. DNA is especially useful when remains are badly damaged or fragmented.

The genetic profile of the remains (see DNA fingerprinting) is compared with:

  • Direct reference samples: a toothbrush, a razor, a sample stored in a hospital.
  • Relatives: parents, children or siblings share half of their variants. The probability of kinship is calculated.
  • Mitochondrial or Y-chromosome DNA, when only distant maternal or paternal relatives are available.

Major disasters

  • After the attacks on the World Trade Center in New York in 2001, the city's medical examiner's office undertook the largest DNA identification effort in history. More than two decades later it is still working with new techniques on bone fragments, and about 40 % of victims still have no identified remains.
  • After the Indian Ocean tsunami of 2004, teams from around the world identified thousands of victims, many of them foreign tourists.
  • After the 11 March 2004 train bombings in Madrid, forensic teams identified all 191 people killed, partly through DNA.

The Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo

During the Argentine dictatorship (1976–1983), hundreds of babies born in captivity or abducted with their disappeared parents were given to other families under false identities. Their grandmothers, organised as the Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo, looked for a way to prove who their grandchildren were without the parents.

In 1984 the geneticist Mary-Claire King travelled to Argentina and helped develop the "grandpaternity index", which calculates the probability that a child is the grandchild of particular grandparents from genetic markers. Mitochondrial DNA, shared through the maternal line, was later used. In 1987 Argentina created the National Genetic Data Bank, where families store their DNA. Thanks to it, the identities of more than 130 grandchildren have been restored.

The graves of the Spanish Civil War

In Spain, tens of thousands of victims of the Civil War and the dictatorship are thought to remain in mass graves. Since 2000, family associations and teams of archaeologists and forensic experts have exhumed hundreds of graves. DNA identification is difficult because the remains are old and many close relatives have died: grandchildren, nephews and nieces or mitochondrial DNA are often used.

The Democratic Memory Law 20/2022 made the search for missing persons a responsibility of the State and created a State DNA Bank of Victims of the War and the Dictatorship, where relatives can leave a sample free of charge to make identification easier.

At borders too

Forensic teams work to identify people who die trying to cross the Mediterranean or the desert between Mexico and the United States, so that their families can know what happened to them.
