Genetics and society

Genetic discrimination

Can an insurer or an employer use your genes against you? What the laws protect in Spain, Europe and the United States, and where the gaps are.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

At a glance
What it is Treating someone worse because of their genetic characteristics, not their current health
Areas of risk Life and health insurance, employment
In Spain Prohibited by Law 14/2007 and Law 15/2022 on equal treatment
In the US GINA (2008), with exceptions such as life insurance

The fear

One of the most common reasons people avoid genetic tests, even when they would be useful, is the fear that an insurer or employer will use the result against them. Imagine a healthy person with a BRCA1 variant or at risk of Huntington's disease: might they pay more for life insurance? Could they be turned down for a job?

What the laws say

Spain and Europe

  • The Oviedo Convention prohibits "any form of discrimination against a person on grounds of his or her genetic heritage".
  • The Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (2000) includes genetic features among the grounds on which discrimination is prohibited.
  • Spain's Law 14/2007 on Biomedical Research prohibits discrimination based on genetic characteristics and the use of genetic data for unauthorised purposes.
  • Law 15/2022 on equal treatment and non-discrimination expressly includes genetic predisposition to disease among the prohibited grounds of discrimination.

United States

The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) of 2008 prohibits health insurers and employers from using genetic information. But it does not cover life, disability or long-term care insurance, a gap that worries many patients.

Other models

Since 2001 the United Kingdom has had a voluntary code between the government and insurers: they do not ask for or use predictive test results, except for Huntington's disease in very large life insurance policies. Canada passed a law in 2017 that prohibits requiring genetic tests as a condition for services.

Grey areas

  • Family history: insurers routinely ask about parents' illnesses, which is an indirect form of genetic information.
  • A disease already diagnosed: the law protects predisposition, but an existing disease is treated like any other health condition.
  • Sport and the military: some fields have floated genetic aptitude tests, with little scientific basis.

Discrimination in history

The fear is not unfounded. In the twentieth century, misunderstood genetic ideas justified eugenics: forced sterilisation in the United States, Sweden and Switzerland, and the crimes of Nazi Germany. See history. That is why today's laws insist that no one should be judged by their genes.
