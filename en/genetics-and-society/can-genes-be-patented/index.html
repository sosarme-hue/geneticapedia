Genetics and society

Can genes be patented?

The Myriad case, which reached the US Supreme Court in 2013, changed the rules. What can be patented today, what cannot, and the battle over CRISPR patents.

At a glance
US since 2013 A gene as it exists in nature cannot be patented; synthetic DNA can
Europe The 1998 directive allows isolated sequences to be patented if their industrial application is described
Key case Myriad Genetics and the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes
Current debate Patents on CRISPR and gene therapies

The Myriad case

In the 1990s the US company Myriad Genetics obtained patents on the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, whose variants greatly raise the risk of breast and ovarian cancer. Thanks to them, it was for years the only laboratory allowed to perform the test in the United States, at a price of about 3,000 dollars. Other laboratories could neither offer it nor check its results.

A group of patients, doctors and researchers, backed by the American Civil Liberties Union, sued the company. In June 2013 the US Supreme Court ruled unanimously in Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics that:

  • A segment of DNA as it exists in nature cannot be patented merely because it has been isolated: it is a product of nature, not an invention.
  • Complementary DNA (cDNA), a synthetic copy without the non-coding parts, can be patented because it does not exist in nature.

After the ruling, several laboratories began offering the test immediately and the price fell sharply.

In Europe

European Directive 98/44/EC on biotechnological inventions states that the human body and the simple discovery of a gene are not patentable, but an element isolated from the body, including a gene sequence, can be, provided its industrial application is described. In practice, the European Patent Office greatly narrowed Myriad's patents after challenges from European institutions, and they never had the same effect as in the United States.

The CRISPR battle

Patents on the CRISPR-Cas9 editing technique have been the subject of one of the longest disputes in biotechnology:

  • The University of California (with Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier) filed for the patent in 2012 for general use.
  • The Broad Institute of Harvard and MIT (with Feng Zhang) applied later for use in human cells, but through a fast-track procedure was granted the US patent first.

After years of litigation, in 2022 the US patent office sided with the Broad Institute on use in eukaryotic cells, although an appeals court in 2025 ordered part of that decision to be reconsidered. In Europe the outcome has been different. Meanwhile, drugs that use CRISPR pay licences to one side or the other. See CRISPR explained.

It is ironic that the system that made it possible was described by Francisco Mojica in Alicante without any patent.

The underlying debate

  • For patents: they encourage investment in research, which is expensive and risky.
  • Against: they can block access to tests and treatments, raise prices and prevent others from doing research.

During the Human Genome Project, many scientists such as John Sulston argued that the genome is the heritage of humanity and that its data should be public. This was agreed in the Bermuda Principles of 1996: sequences would be released within 24 hours. See history.
