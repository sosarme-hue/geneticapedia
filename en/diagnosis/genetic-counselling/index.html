What is genetic counselling?

The consultation that helps you decide whether to be tested, understand the result and think about your family.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

Much more than a result

Genetic counselling is a communication process between a person or family and a professional trained in genetics. Its aim is to help:

  • understand a condition and how it is inherited;
  • estimate the risk for the person and their relatives;
  • decide whether to have a test, and which one;
  • understand the result and its consequences;
  • learn about options for prevention, follow-up, treatment or reproduction.

It takes place before the test and after the result arrives.

When to ask for it

Talk to your family doctor if:

  • there are several cases of the same cancer in your family, especially at young ages, or cases of male breast cancer, ovarian or pancreatic cancer;
  • a relative has a known genetic variant;
  • you have a condition that might be hereditary, such as a cardiomyopathy or very high cholesterol from a young age;
  • you have had recurrent miscarriages or a child with a genetic condition or a birth defect;
  • you are planning a pregnancy and there is a family history, or you and your partner are related.

Basic principles

  • Voluntariness. Having a test is always a free choice.
  • The right not to know. A person may decide not to learn their risk, for example in Huntington's disease.
  • Non-directiveness. The professional informs and supports, but does not decide for you, especially on reproductive matters.
  • Confidentiality. The result is yours. Sharing it with your family matters, but the decision is yours, with help to do it.

Children and young people

In general, predictive tests for adult-onset conditions with no prevention available in childhood are postponed until the person can decide for themselves. This is a recommendation of the European human genetics societies.

In Spain

The Biomedical Research Act (14/2007) requires genetic counselling to be offered whenever a genetic test is done for health purposes. Clinical genetics units and cancer genetic counselling units are based in public hospitals, and you reach them through a referral from your doctor.

What a consultation looks like, step by step

  1. Beforehand: you may be asked to gather information about your family (who had which condition and at what age) and previous medical reports.
  2. Family tree: the professional draws at least three generations using standard symbols. This reveals the inheritance pattern.
  3. Risk assessment: with the tree, the examination and the clinical data, the probability of a genetic cause is estimated.
  4. Pre-test counselling: what will be analysed, what might come up (including uncertain variants and unexpected findings), what it means for the family, timescales and costs.
  5. Written informed consent.
  6. Results given in person, with time for questions, plus a written report.
  7. Follow-up: referral to other specialists, psychological support and, where appropriate, testing of relatives.

Telling the family

A genetic result also says something about parents, siblings and children. The law protects your confidentiality, so the decision to share it is yours, but professionals usually encourage you to inform at-risk relatives and offer a family letter that explains the finding and how to ask for an appointment. Testing relatives (known as cascade testing) is one of the most effective forms of prevention, for example in Lynch syndrome or familial hypercholesterolaemia.

The right not to know

Some people prefer not to know a risk, especially when it cannot be prevented, as in Huntington's disease. Spain's Biomedical Research Act (14/2007) and the Oviedo Convention recognise the right not to be informed. In Huntington's disease, most people at risk decide not to take the predictive test, and those who do follow a protocol with several visits and psychological support.

Diagnosing rare diseases

Families with an undiagnosed condition can spend years going from one clinic to another (the so-called diagnostic odyssey, which in Europe lasts around five years on average). The genetics unit coordinates testing and, if needed, access to undiagnosed-disease programmes, such as Spain's SpainUDP at the Rare Diseases Research Institute of the Instituto de Salud Carlos III.

Who provides genetic counselling

Until 2024 Spain had no Clinical Genetics specialty, and counselling was provided by doctors from various specialties and geneticists with other qualifications trained in the field. The decree creating the specialty (2024) opens up specialist training, and more specialists will be available in the coming years. In the UK and many other countries, genetic counsellors are a registered profession working alongside clinical geneticists. Ask your doctor to refer you to the genetics unit of your reference hospital.
