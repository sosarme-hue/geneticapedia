How to read a genetic result

Pathogenic, benign or of uncertain significance. What each classification means and why a result can change over the years.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

Five categories

Since 2015, laboratories have classified variants according to international guidelines from the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP):

Classification What it means What is usually done
Pathogenic Strong evidence that it causes the disease Used to decide follow-up and to test the family
Likely pathogenic Very likely (over 90 %) to cause it Usually treated as if it were pathogenic
Uncertain significance (VUS) Not enough data to tell Medical decisions are not based on it
Likely benign Very likely not to cause disease Usually not reported, or reported as such
Benign Normal variation No consequences

The variant of uncertain significance

This is the result that raises most questions. In broad tests such as large panels or the exome, finding some VUS is common. Most of those that are reclassified over time turn out to be benign.

That is why experts insist that a VUS should not be used to recommend preventive surgery, change treatment or test healthy relatives.

A result can change

The classification depends on what is known today. As more people are tested, a variant can move from uncertain to benign or to pathogenic. It is worth asking the team that ordered the test whether it will be reviewed and how you will be told.

Terms you will see in a report

  • Gene and variant: for example, BRCA2 c.5946del. The "c." indicates the position in the coding sequence.
  • Heterozygous: one copy has the variant. Homozygous: both copies. Hemizygous: only one copy is available, as with X-chromosome genes in males.
  • Penetrance: the probability that a person with the variant will develop the disease.
  • Secondary or incidental finding: a result unrelated to the reason for testing, such as a cancer-risk variant in a test done for another reason. Whether you want this information should be discussed beforehand.

Talk to a professional

A genetic result also affects the family. Interpreting it properly requires genetic counselling (see Genetic counselling). Do not try to draw conclusions with search engines or apps alone.

How a variant is classified

Laboratories follow the ACMG/AMP standards (2015), adopted worldwide. Each variant is assessed with evidence criteria that add up towards pathogenicity or towards benignity:

  • Population frequency: a variant present in many healthy people (databases such as gnomAD gather data from hundreds of thousands) is unlikely to cause a serious rare disease.
  • Type of change: a variant that truncates the protein (nonsense, frameshift) in a gene where loss of function causes disease weighs heavily in favour.
  • Functional data: laboratory experiments showing that the protein stops working.
  • Segregation: whether the variant is found in all affected relatives and not in the healthy ones.
  • De novo occurrence: in a child with a severe condition and healthy parents, a new variant is a strong clue.
  • Computer predictions: they help, but on their own carry little weight.
  • Clinical databases: ClinVar records how other laboratories have classified the same variant.

The combination of criteria gives one of the five categories. Variants in non-coding DNA, or in poorly studied genes, are the hardest.

Example: three different reports on BRCA2

  • "Pathogenic variant c.5946del in BRCA2": the protein is truncated. There is a high risk of breast and ovarian cancer, and surveillance and prevention measures exist. Testing is offered to relatives.
  • "Variant of uncertain significance c.XXXX in BRCA2": we do not know. Decisions are based on the personal and family history, not on the variant. Relatives are not tested to make medical decisions.
  • "No pathogenic variants detected" in a family with many cases: this lowers but does not remove the risk; other genes or factors not studied may be involved.

A negative result is not always reassuring

A result with no findings means that the test that was done found nothing, not that there is no genetic cause. The panel may not have included the gene, the technique may not detect that type of change (such as a triplet-repeat expansion or a large rearrangement), or the cause may not be known yet. That is why a good report always states its limitations.

Reanalysis

Over the years, new genes are described and variants reclassified. Reanalysing an exome or genome one or two years later yields new diagnoses in roughly 10–15 % of cases that were previously negative. Ask whether your laboratory or genetics unit offers it.

Mosaicism and other nuances

Sometimes the variant is present in only some cells (mosaicism), and the report gives a percentage. A variant may also be inherited from a healthy parent, which raises the possibility of incomplete penetrance or that it is not the cause. These nuances should be explained by the geneticist.
