Direct-to-consumer genetic tests

Ancestry and health kits bought online. What they really tell you, what they cannot say and what happens to your data.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

How they work

You order the kit, spit into a tube, post it and a few weeks later you get a report on a website or app. Most of these tests do not read your whole genome: they use a chip (microarray) that looks at a few hundred thousand known positions in the DNA, a small fraction of it.

Ancestry: estimates, not certainties

Percentages such as "32 % Iberian, 18 % North African" are calculated by comparing your DNA with reference populations chosen by each company. Therefore:

  • Two companies can give you different results from the same saliva.
  • Results change when the company updates its references.
  • Genetic boundaries do not match national borders.

They are often very good at finding relatives, which can bring family surprises, such as unexpected paternity or unknown siblings.

Health: be very cautious

  • A chip may miss important variants. For example, some kits analyse only 3 of the thousands of known BRCA1 and BRCA2 variants, the ones most common in Ashkenazi Jewish people. A "negative" result does not rule out a hereditary cancer risk.
  • Chips make errors with rare variants. A 2018 study rechecked, in a clinical laboratory, risk variants that patients brought from consumer tests, and about 40 % turned out to be false positives.
  • Reports on traits such as "caffeine tendency" or "the ideal diet for your DNA" generally have little scientific basis.

If a consumer test shows a result that worries you, confirm it in a clinical laboratory before making any decision, and discuss it with your doctor.

Your genetic data

DNA cannot be changed like a password, and it also reveals information about your relatives.

  • In 2023, one of the largest companies in the sector, 23andMe, suffered unauthorised access that exposed data from about 6.9 million users.
  • In 2025, the same company filed for bankruptcy, and its genetic data passed to another entity.

Before buying, read what they do with your sample and your data, whether they share it for research and how you can delete it. In Europe, the General Data Protection Regulation treats genetic data as a special category requiring enhanced protection.

Why a chip is not sequencing

Most of these companies use a genotyping chip (SNP array) that examines 600,000 to 700,000 known DNA positions. It is a cheap technology and very useful for ancestry and common variation, but it has serious limits for health:

  • It only sees the variants included in the chip's design. Thousands of pathogenic variants are known in BRCA1 and BRCA2, and a typical test looks at a few.
  • It is unreliable for very rare variants. A study using UK Biobank data showed that for very low-frequency variants, most chip positives were false.
  • That is why any health finding must be confirmed in a clinical laboratory before any decision is taken.

Raw data and third-party websites

Many companies let you download your raw data. Some websites "interpret" it for a small fee, often without quality control. Serious misinterpretations have been described, such as people who believed they carried a high-risk variant they did not have. If you do this, treat the results as a clue, not a diagnosis.

Polygenic scores

Some tests offer polygenic risk scores, which add up the effect of thousands of common variants. They are a research tool with modest predictive power for most diseases and worse performance in people of ancestries under-represented in studies, which have been mostly European.

Genealogy and privacy

Genealogy databases have helped solve old police cases and find biological relatives, including gamete donors who believed they were anonymous. When you share your DNA you also share information about your relatives, who have not given consent.

Before you buy one

  • Read what the company does with your sample and data, and whether you can ask for them to be destroyed.
  • Check whether the data are used for research and whether you can opt out.
  • If you are worried about a specific condition because of your family history, do not use a mail-order test: ask for an appointment at a genetics unit.

In short

Useful out of curiosity and for genealogy. Not suitable for ruling out or diagnosing disease. That is what clinical tests with genetic counselling are for.
