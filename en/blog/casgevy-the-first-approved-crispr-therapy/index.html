Casgevy, the first approved CRISPR therapy

Three years ago the United Kingdom approved the first CRISPR-based medicine, for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassaemia. What it does, who it helps and why it still reaches few patients.

From a Nobel Prize to a medicine in eleven years

In November 2023, the UK medicines regulator approved Casgevy (exagamglogene autotemcel), the first treatment based on CRISPR. The United States approved it in December 2023 for sickle cell disease and in January 2024 for beta-thalassaemia, and the European Union in February 2024. Only eleven years had passed since Charpentier and Doudna described the tool.

How it works

Casgevy does not fix the haemoglobin gene. It uses a cleverer trick:

  1. Bone marrow stem cells are taken from the patient.
  2. In the laboratory, CRISPR cuts a switch in the BCL11A gene, which in adults turns off fetal haemoglobin, the kind we have before birth.
  3. After chemotherapy to clear the marrow, the edited cells are returned to the patient.
  4. The new cells make fetal haemoglobin, which replaces the faulty haemoglobin.

In the clinical trial, more than 90 % of sickle cell patients had no severe pain crises for at least a year, and most beta-thalassaemia patients no longer needed transfusions.

Why it reaches few people

  • Price: in the United States it costs about 2.2 million dollars per patient.
  • Complexity: it needs specialist hospitals, months of preparation and intensive chemotherapy, with a risk of infertility.
  • Geography: most people with sickle cell disease live in sub-Saharan Africa and India, where this treatment is not available.

That is why versions that edit cells inside the body, without a transplant, are being developed, as was done in 2025 for baby KJ (see the next post).
