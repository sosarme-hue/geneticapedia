Genes, chromosomes and genome

Three words that are often confused. What each one is and how they fit together.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

In this article

  1. The library comparison
  2. Human chromosomes
  3. What exactly is a gene?
  4. Genotype and phenotype
  5. Sources

The library comparison

A simple way to organise the concepts:

Concept Comparison What it is
Genome The whole library All the DNA of an organism
Chromosome One volume in the library A long, tightly packaged DNA molecule
Gene A recipe within a volume A stretch of DNA with instructions for a function, almost always a protein
Base A letter A, T, G or C

Human chromosomes

Human cells normally have 46 chromosomes, organised in 23 pairs. In each pair, one chromosome comes from the mother and the other from the father.

  • 22 pairs of autosomes, numbered 1 to 22, roughly from largest to smallest.
  • 1 pair of sex chromosomes: XX in most women and XY in most men. The Y chromosome contains the SRY gene, which starts testicular development.

Eggs and sperm carry only 23 chromosomes, one from each pair. When they join at fertilisation, the number 46 is restored.

The number 46 was not established until 1956, when Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan counted them with new techniques. Until then it was believed there were 48.

An ordered picture of a person's chromosomes is called a karyotype. It is used to detect large changes, such as an extra chromosome (trisomy) or a segment moved elsewhere (translocation).

What exactly is a gene?

A gene is a stretch of DNA that the cell "reads" to make a product with a function. In most cases that product is a protein: an enzyme that digests food, the haemoglobin that carries oxygen, the collagen in skin… Some genes produce RNA that does a job by itself.

A typical gene has parts that are translated into protein (exons) and intervening parts that are cut out (introns), as well as neighbouring regions that act as switches (promoters and enhancers).

Each gene occupies a place on a chromosome, called a locus. Since we have two copies of each autosome, we have two copies of each autosomal gene. The possible versions of the same gene are called alleles.

Genotype and phenotype

  • Genotype: the combination of alleles a person has.
  • Phenotype: what is observed or measured, such as blood group, height or a disease.

The phenotype depends on the genotype, but also on the environment, chance during development and interactions between many genes. We explain this in Genes and environment.
