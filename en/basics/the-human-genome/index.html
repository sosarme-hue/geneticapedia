Basics

The human genome

How our complete DNA was read for the first time, what was discovered and what has changed since.

The Human Genome Project

In 1990 an international project began to read the human genome letter by letter. Centres in the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, France, Germany and China took part, with Britain's John Sulston and America's Francis Collins among its public faces. In parallel, the company Celera Genomics, led by Craig Venter, began its own sequencing.

  • In 2001 the first drafts were published.
  • In 2003 it was declared complete: it covered almost all of the gene-containing part of the genome, about 92 % of the total.
  • It cost about 3 billion dollars.

Its data were released openly, with a commitment that anyone could use them.

The gene surprise

Before the project, it was estimated that we would have about 100,000 genes. There turned out to be about 20,000 protein-coding genes, roughly the same as a tiny worm. Human complexity depends less on the number of genes than on how they are regulated and combined.

The complete genome, at last

The 2003 genome had gaps: highly repetitive regions that the technology of the time could not read. In 2022, the Telomere-to-Telomere (T2T) consortium published the first truly complete sequence, end to end, with about 3.055 billion base pairs. The complete Y chromosome followed in 2023.

One genome is not enough: the pangenome

The 2003 reference was built mainly from the DNA of a few people. In 2023 the first draft of the human pangenome was presented, with genomes from 47 people of diverse ancestries, to better represent humanity's diversity. This improves diagnosis in populations that were under-represented.

From 3 billion to a few hundred euros

Sequencing has become dramatically cheaper. Today a whole genome can be read in hours or days for a few hundred euros in the most efficient laboratories. Reading the genome is easy; interpreting it is still the hard part.
