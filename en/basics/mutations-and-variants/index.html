Mutations and variants

What a change in DNA is, why almost all are harmless and when they cause disease.

A word with a bad reputation

In everyday language, "mutation" sounds like something bad. In genetics it simply means a change in the DNA sequence. Most such changes have no effect, some are useful and a few cause disease.

That is why clinical laboratories now prefer the neutral word variant, adding whether it is pathogenic (disease-causing), benign or of uncertain significance.

Types of change

  • Single-nucleotide variant (SNV, or SNP when common): one letter is changed for another.
  • Silent: the codon changes but the amino acid does not.
  • Missense: one amino acid changes. Example: sickle cell disease, in which a glutamic acid is replaced by valine in haemoglobin.
  • Nonsense: a stop signal appears too early and the protein is cut short.
  • Insertions and deletions (indels): letters are added or lost. If the number is not a multiple of three, the whole reading shifts (frameshift).
  • Repeat expansions: a short piece such as CAG is repeated too many times. This causes Huntington's disease.
  • Copy-number variants (CNVs): large segments, which may contain several genes, are duplicated or lost.
  • Chromosome changes: an extra or missing chromosome (such as trisomy 21) or segments swapped between chromosomes.

Inherited or new

  • Germline: present in the egg or sperm and therefore in every cell of the person. They can be passed on to children.
  • De novo: appear for the first time in a person, without being present in the parents. Each newborn has on average about 60 to 70 new variants, almost always without consequences.
  • Somatic: arise during life in some of the body's cells. They are not inherited. The accumulation of somatic variants in certain genes is the basis of cancer.

Where do they come from?

From errors when copying DNA, which the cell almost always corrects, and from damage caused by ultraviolet radiation, tobacco, some chemicals or ionising radiation. That is why protecting yourself from the sun and not smoking also protects your DNA.

Variation is normal

Compared with the reference genome, a person's genome differs at 4 to 5 million positions. That variation partly explains our differences in appearance, in response to drugs or in disease risk, and it is the raw material of evolution.
