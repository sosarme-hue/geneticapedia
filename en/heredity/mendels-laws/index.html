Heredity

Mendel's laws

How a friar in Brno discovered the basic rules of heredity by crossing pea plants.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

The friar and the peas

Gregor Mendel (1822–1884) was an Augustinian friar at St Thomas's Abbey in Brno, now in the Czech Republic. Between 1856 and 1863 he crossed thousands of pea plants and patiently recorded how seven easily distinguished traits were passed on: smooth or wrinkled seeds, yellow or green, violet or white flowers, tall or short plants…

He presented his results in 1865 and published them in 1866. They went almost unnoticed for 34 years, until in 1900 three botanists (Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak) reached similar conclusions and rescued his work. Read his profile.

What he discovered

Mendel proposed that each trait depends on "factors" (today we call them genes) that come in pairs, one from each parent, and that do not blend like paint but are passed on whole.

1. Uniformity. When he crossed a pure-breeding yellow-seeded plant with a pure-breeding green-seeded one, all the offspring were yellow. Yellow is dominant and green recessive.

2. Segregation. When those offspring were crossed with each other, green reappeared in about one in four plants (a 3:1 ratio). Each parent has two copies and passes on only one, at random.

3. Independent assortment. Seed colour and seed shape were inherited independently of each other. Today we know this holds when the genes are on different chromosomes or far apart on the same one.

How to work it out: the Punnett square

If we call the yellow (dominant) allele A and the green (recessive) one a, and cross two Aa plants:

A a
A AA (yellow) Aa (yellow)
a Aa (yellow) aa (green)

Three out of four combinations give yellow seeds and one gives green. It is a probability for each offspring, not a fixed share: in a family of four children, none, one or several may show the recessive trait.

Beyond Mendel

Mendel's rules explain traits that depend on a single gene well. But many traits do not follow such clean ratios:

  • Incomplete dominance: the hybrid is intermediate, like some pink flowers from a cross between red and white.
  • Codominance: both alleles are expressed at once, as in blood group AB.
  • Polygenic inheritance: height or skin colour depend on hundreds of genes and on the environment.
  • Linkage: genes very close together on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together.

"Textbook" traits such as tongue rolling or eye colour are often presented as simple Mendelian examples, but in reality they are more complex.
