Heredity

Inheritance patterns

Autosomal dominant, recessive, X-linked and mitochondrial. How single-gene conditions are passed on.

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

When a condition depends on a single gene, it usually follows one of these patterns. Knowing them helps explain why a condition appears in several generations in a row, or suddenly turns up in a family with no history of it.

Autosomal dominant

One altered copy of the gene is enough to have the condition (or the increased risk).

  • An affected person has a 50 % chance of passing it on to each child.
  • It is usually seen in several consecutive generations.
  • It affects men and women equally.
  • Examples: Huntington's disease, familial hypercholesterolaemia, Marfan syndrome, hereditary cancer due to BRCA1 and BRCA2.

Sometimes the variant appears de novo in the child, with no family history. At other times a carrier does not develop the condition (incomplete penetrance) or has a mild form (variable expressivity).

Autosomal recessive

Two altered copies are needed, one from each parent.

  • The parents are usually healthy: they are carriers, with one altered copy and one normal copy.
  • If both are carriers, each pregnancy has a 25 % chance of an affected child, 50 % of a carrier child and 25 % of a child without the variant.
  • It is more common when the parents are related (consanguinity), because they are more likely to carry the same variant.
  • Examples: cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, spinal muscular atrophy, phenylketonuria.

We are all carriers of some recessive variants without knowing it.

X-linked

The gene is on the X chromosome. Because men have only one X, one altered copy is enough for them to have the condition; women, with two Xs, are usually carriers with mild or no symptoms.

  • A carrier mother has a 50 % chance of passing the variant to each child: sons who receive it will be affected and daughters who receive it will be carriers.
  • An affected father passes it to all his daughters and none of his sons, because sons receive his Y chromosome.
  • Examples: haemophilia A and B, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, red-green colour blindness.

There are also less common X-linked dominant conditions, such as Rett syndrome.

Mitochondrial

Mitochondrial DNA is inherited only from the mother, because the sperm's mitochondria do not pass into the embryo. A mother with a mitochondrial variant can pass it to all her children, but severity varies greatly depending on the proportion of affected mitochondria (heteroplasmy). Example: Leber hereditary optic neuropathy.

What does not fit a pattern

Most common diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure or heart attack, depend on many genes of small effect and on lifestyle. This is called multifactorial inheritance. A family history increases the risk, but it does not make it destiny. We explain it in Genes and environment.
