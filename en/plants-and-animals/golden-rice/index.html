Plants and animals

Golden rice

A rice engineered to make vitamin A that could prevent thousands of cases of childhood blindness. Twenty-five years of science, controversy and court cases.

At a glance
What it is Rice that makes beta-carotene, the precursor of vitamin A, in the grain
Creators Ingo Potrykus and Peter Beyer, published in 2000
Aim To fight vitamin A deficiency where rice is the staple food
Status Approved in the Philippines and Bangladesh; cultivation halted by a Philippine court in 2024

The problem: vitamin A deficiency

Vitamin A is essential for sight and immunity. Its deficiency is the leading cause of preventable childhood blindness worldwide. According to the World Health Organization it affects hundreds of millions of pre-school children, mainly in South Asia and Africa, and every year between 250,000 and 500,000 children go blind because of it; half of them die within a year. It also raises deaths from measles and diarrhoea.

In many of these regions, white rice is almost the only food. The rice grain has no beta-carotene, although the plant's leaves do.

The solution: adding a metabolic pathway

Swiss botanist Ingo Potrykus and German biochemist Peter Beyer spent almost a decade trying to make the rice grain produce beta-carotene. In 2000 they reported in Science that they had succeeded, by adding two daffodil genes and one from a bacterium. The grain was golden yellow, hence the name.

The first version made little beta-carotene. In 2005, researchers at the company Syngenta replaced the daffodil gene with one from maize and increased production more than twentyfold: Golden Rice 2. A study showed that a bowl of this rice could provide a large part of a child's daily vitamin A needs.

A history of obstacles

The creators gave free use to poor farmers, and the companies holding related patents waived their fees. Even so, golden rice took more than twenty years to reach the fields:

  • It had to be crossed into local varieties and tested in field trials.
  • Environmental groups such as Greenpeace strongly opposed it. In 2013, activists destroyed a trial plot in the Philippines.
  • In 2016, more than 100 Nobel laureates signed a letter supporting agricultural biotechnology and golden rice.
  • The Philippines approved commercial cultivation in 2021, the first country to do so. But in 2024, a Philippine appeals court halted it after a lawsuit by environmental and farmers' groups.

The debate

  • For: it is a cheap and sustainable way to bring vitamin A to those who need it most, without changing their eating habits, and farmers pay no royalties.
  • Against: critics favour alternatives such as vitamin A supplements, a more varied diet or vegetable gardens, and worry about local rice diversity and opening the door to other GM crops.

Most nutrition experts believe all strategies are needed together. Supplements have saved many lives, but they do not reach every child.
