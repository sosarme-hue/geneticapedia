Plants and animals

GM and gene-edited crops

What a genetically modified plant is, how it differs from one edited with CRISPR, what the science says about safety and how Europe regulates them. Spain grows more GM maize than any other European country.

At a glance
Transgenic (GM) An organism that has been given a gene from another species
Gene-edited An organism with precise changes to its own genes, often with no foreign DNA
First GM food The Flavr Savr tomato, in the United States, in 1994
In Spain Bt maize, which resists an insect pest, is grown
Safety Major scientific reviews have found no greater health risk than from conventional crops

All farming is genetics

Since domestication around 10,000 years ago, farmers have changed plant genes by choosing the best seeds. The twentieth century added planned crosses and even mutagenesis: exposing seeds to radiation or chemicals to cause random mutations, then looking for useful varieties. Many of the wheat, barley and grapefruit varieties we eat today were made this way, without controversy.

Transgenic crops

The recombinant DNA techniques of the 1970s made it possible to add a specific gene, even from another species. The most widespread examples are:

  • Bt crops: they carry a gene from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis that makes a protein toxic to certain caterpillars, but not to people or other animals. They reduce insecticide use.
  • Herbicide-tolerant crops: weeds can be killed with a herbicide without harming the crop.
  • Virus-resistant papaya in Hawaii, which saved the crop in the 1990s.

Only one GM crop is grown commercially in the European Union, MON810 maize, a Bt variety, and Spain accounts for the great majority of its area, mainly in Aragon and Catalonia, where it protects against the corn borer. Europe does import large amounts of GM soya and maize for animal feed.

Are they safe?

This is one of the most studied questions in agricultural science. In 2016, the US National Academies of Sciences reviewed more than 900 studies and concluded that there is no evidence that foods from approved GM crops are less safe than conventional ones. The World Health Organization and the European Commission, which funded more than 130 research projects over 25 years, reached similar conclusions.

The stronger debates are not about health but about the environment and economics: resistant pests and weeds, dependence on patented seed, the concentration of the market in a few companies, and coexistence with organic farming.

Gene editing

With CRISPR and other techniques, scientists can make precise changes to the plant's own genes: switch a gene off, change one letter. The result is often indistinguishable from a natural mutation or from classic mutagenesis, and no foreign DNA remains. Some examples:

  • A tomato with more GABA, a calming compound, on sale in Japan since 2021: the first CRISPR-edited food to be sold.
  • Wheat with less of the gluten that harms people with coeliac disease, developed partly at the Institute for Sustainable Agriculture of Spain's CSIC in Córdoba.
  • Mushrooms that do not brown, disease-resistant rice and fungus-resistant bananas.

How Europe regulates them

In 2018, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that gene-edited organisms must be regulated as GMOs, with long approval processes and labelling. Many scientists criticised the decision for treating very different changes alike. In 2023, the European Commission proposed new rules for new genomic techniques that would treat plants with small changes that could have arisen naturally as conventional, and in 2025 Parliament and member states reached an agreement to adopt them. Organic products will still be barred from using them.
