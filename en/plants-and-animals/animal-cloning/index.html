Animal cloning

From Dolly the sheep to cloned pets, livestock and endangered species. How a mammal is cloned, why it is so hard and why people are not cloned.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

At a glance
What a clone is An individual with the same nuclear DNA as another
Technique Somatic cell nuclear transfer
First mammal cloned from an adult Dolly the sheep, born in Scotland in 1996
First primates Two macaques, in China, in 2018
Human reproductive cloning Banned in Spain and almost everywhere

Natural clones

Nature has always cloned. Bacteria split into two identical copies, many plants reproduce from cuttings or runners, and in humans identical twins are natural clones: they come from one embryo that splits in two.

How a mammal is cloned

The technique is called somatic cell nuclear transfer:

  1. A body cell is taken from the animal to be copied, for example from the skin or udder.
  2. An egg is taken from another female and its nucleus removed.
  3. The donor cell's nucleus is placed into the empty egg, and a small electric pulse makes it start dividing like an embryo.
  4. The embryo is transferred into the womb of a surrogate mother.

The egg "reprograms" the adult nucleus so that it behaves like an embryo's again. That ability later inspired Shinya Yamanaka to find the factors responsible, which led to induced pluripotent stem cells.

Dolly and what came next

Dolly the sheep was born on 5 July 1996 at the Roslin Institute in Scotland, by the team of Ian Wilmut and Keith Campbell. Her birth was announced in February 1997 and made headlines worldwide: it proved that a specialised adult cell still held all the information needed to build a whole animal. It took 277 attempts. She died in 2003, aged six, of a lung disease common in sheep kept indoors; later studies of her cloned "sisters" showed that clones can age normally.

Since then mice, cattle, pigs, goats, horses, dogs, cats, camels and, in 2018, the first primates, the macaques Zhong Zhong and Hua Hua, have been cloned in Shanghai.

In 2003, a Spanish and French team cloned the bucardo, a Pyrenean ibex that had gone extinct in 2000. The kid died minutes after birth from a lung defect, but it was the first "de-extinction" in history.

What it is used for

  • Livestock: copying highly valuable breeding animals. Their offspring, produced by normal breeding, enter the food chain in some countries.
  • Sport: cloned polo horses have competed in Argentina.
  • Pets: companies in South Korea, China and the United States clone dogs and cats for tens of thousands of euros. The clone has the same genes but not the same personality, and sometimes not even the same coat, which also depends on chance during development.
  • Conservation: in 2020 the United States cloned a black-footed ferret and a Przewalski's horse from cells frozen decades earlier, to restore lost genetic diversity.
  • Research: cloned and edited animals that model human diseases.

Problems and limits

Cloning is still inefficient: in most species, only 1 to 5 % of cloned embryos are born. Many suffer miscarriage, abnormal placentas or large offspring syndrome. The main reason is that reprogramming of the epigenetic marks is incomplete.

Human reproductive cloning is banned in Spain by the 2007 Biomedical Research Act and by the Council of Europe's Oviedo Convention, and it is rejected by the vast majority of scientists for safety and ethical reasons.
