Spinal muscular atrophy

A severe childhood disease that went from having no treatment to having three in just a few years. Cause, types, inheritance and why early diagnosis changes everything.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

Inheritance Autosomal recessive

What it is

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is an inherited disease in which the motor neurons of the spinal cord, the nerve cells that tell muscles to contract, are gradually lost. Without them, muscles weaken and waste, especially those closest to the trunk (hips, shoulders) and the breathing muscles. Sensation and intelligence are not affected: children with SMA are often especially alert and communicative.

Until a few years ago it was the most common genetic cause of death in infants.

Types

SMA is classified by age at onset and the highest motor milestone reached without treatment:

Type Onset Course without treatment
0 Before birth Very severe from birth
1 (Werdnig-Hoffmann) Before 6 months Never sit; most died before age 2 from respiratory failure
2 6-18 months Sit but do not walk; scoliosis and breathing problems
3 (Kugelberg-Welander) After 18 months Walk, although many lose walking over the years
4 Adulthood Mild weakness

Type 1 accounts for more than half of cases. The first signs are a "floppy" (hypotonic) baby who moves the legs little, cannot hold up the head and breathes with the belly.

Genetic cause

It is caused by loss of the SMN1 (survival motor neuron 1) gene on chromosome 5. In about 95 % of cases1 part of the gene is missing from both copies (a deletion of exon 7). The gene makes the SMN protein, which all cells need, but motor neurons are especially sensitive to its lack.

The key detail is that there is an almost identical twin gene, SMN2, that differs by a single letter. Because of that change, most SMN2 RNA is "spliced" wrongly (losing exon 7) and only about 10 % produces working protein. People have between one and eight copies of SMN2: the more copies, the more protein and the milder the disease tends to be. Babies with two copies usually have type 1; with three or four, milder forms.

That "spare wheel" is what two of the three treatments exploit.

Inheritance

It is autosomal recessive: two altered copies of SMN1 are needed. About 1 in 40 to 50 people1 is a carrier without knowing it. If both partners are carriers, each pregnancy has a 25 % chance of an affected child. About 2 % of cases are due to a new variant in one parent.

An important technical detail: about 4 % of carriers6 have two copies of SMN1 on the same chromosome and none on the other, so the standard carrier test, which counts copies, can come back normal. A negative result therefore greatly reduces risk but does not eliminate it.

Three treatments in a few years

In under five years SMA went from no treatment to three, all based on genetics:

  • Nusinersen (approved in Europe in 2017)2: an antisense oligonucleotide, a short piece of RNA that binds to SMN2 RNA and makes it splice correctly, so more full-length protein is made. It is injected into the cerebrospinal fluid by lumbar puncture four times in the first year and then every four months.
  • Onasemnogene abeparvovec (approved in Europe in 2020)3: gene therapy that delivers a working copy of SMN1 to motor neurons using a modified virus (AAV9), in a single intravenous infusion. It is used mainly in young babies. It requires steroids and liver monitoring.
  • Risdiplam (approved in Europe in 2021)5: a molecule taken by mouth every day that also corrects SMN2 RNA splicing. It reaches the whole body.

All three slow the disease, but do not restore motor neurons already lost. Combinations, new routes of administration, such as a version of the gene therapy injected into the cerebrospinal fluid for older children, and drugs that improve muscle strength are being studied.

Alongside drug treatment, physiotherapy, respiratory support (cough assistance, non-invasive ventilation), nutrition and scoliosis management in neuromuscular units remain essential.

Why timing matters

A baby with type 1 SMA can lose most motor neurons in the first months of life. Trials in babies treated before symptoms4, diagnosed because a sibling was affected or through newborn screening, showed remarkable results: most were able to sit and walk independently, something unthinkable without treatment.

That is why SMA is being added to the heel-prick test in many countries. The test detects absence of SMN1 exon 7 in the same dried blood spot used for other diseases. In Spain, several regions already include it, and it is due to be extended to the whole National Health System.

Diagnosis

  • Genetic testing of SMN1 (deletion of both copies) and SMN2 copy number, which guides prognosis and treatment.
  • Electromyography and muscle biopsy, used in the past, are now rarely needed.
  • Carrier testing for relatives and for couples who request it.

What to ask at your appointment

Questions you can take with you. Not all of them will have an answer on day one, and your care team may add others.

  • Which type of SMA is it, and how many SMN2 copies are there?
  • Which treatment is indicated in our case, and when can it start?
  • What respiratory, nutritional and orthopaedic follow-up is needed?
  • Is SMA included in newborn screening where we live?
  • What is the risk in a future pregnancy, and should other relatives be tested?
