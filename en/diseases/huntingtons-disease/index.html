Huntington's disease

An inherited neurological disease that poses, like few others, the dilemma of knowing or not knowing. Cause, symptoms, predictive testing, care and research.

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance
Gene HTT, chromosome 4
Mutation Expansion of CAG repeats (40 or more)
Inheritance Autosomal dominant
Frequency 5 to 10 per 100,000 people of European descent
Onset Usually between 30 and 50 years of age
Treatment Symptomatic and supportive; disease-modifying therapies in trials

What it is

Huntington's disease is an inherited neurodegenerative disease: it causes progressive death of neurons, especially in a deep area of the brain called the striatum (caudate nucleus and putamen), which helps control movement, behaviour and planning. Over time it also affects the cerebral cortex.

It was described in 1872 by the American physician George Huntington, then just 22, based on Long Island families that his father and grandfather, also doctors, had treated. It used to be known as "Huntington's chorea", from the Greek choreia, dance, after its characteristic movements.

Symptoms

It combines three groups of symptoms, which appear in different order and degree in each person and progress slowly over 15 to 20 years:

  • Motor: involuntary, jerky, irregular movements (chorea), clumsiness, gait and balance problems, difficulty speaking and, in later stages, swallowing, which causes weight loss and pneumonia.
  • Cognitive: difficulty planning, organising, concentrating and switching tasks. People lose mental flexibility long before they lose memory.
  • Psychiatric and behavioural: depression (very common), irritability, apathy, anxiety, impulsivity and obsessive behaviour. The risk of suicide is higher than in the general population, especially around the time of diagnosis.

Cognitive and behavioural changes can begin years before the movements, and are often what affects families most. In 5-10 % of cases the disease begins before age 20 (juvenile form), with more rigidity than chorea and sometimes seizures.

Genetic cause

It is caused by an expansion of CAG repeats in the HTT gene on chromosome 4, which holds the instructions for the protein huntingtin. Everyone has these repeats, but in a limited number. When there are too many, huntingtin has an abnormally long tail of the amino acid glutamine, misfolds, forms aggregates and becomes toxic to neurons.

CAG repeats Meaning
26 or fewer Normal
27 to 35 Normal, but may expand when passed to children
36 to 39 Reduced penetrance: the disease may or may not develop, and usually starts later
40 or more The disease will develop if the person lives long enough

The more repeats, the earlier it usually starts, although the number cannot predict the exact age for any individual. Recent studies show that repeats keep expanding inside neurons throughout life, and that DNA repair genes influence age of onset. It is one of the most promising leads for new treatments.

Inheritance

It is autosomal dominant: one copy of the expanded gene is enough to develop the disease. Each child of a carrier has a 50 % chance of inheriting it, whether male or female. When passed from parent to child, especially through the father, the number of repeats can increase and the disease can start earlier in the next generation: this is anticipation.

A small percentage of cases appear with no known history, because an intermediate allele expanded, because the parent died before showing symptoms, or because of unknown paternity.

To know or not to know

Since 1993, when the gene was identified thanks to the Venezuelan families of Lake Maracaibo and the drive of Nancy Wexler, a blood test can show whether a healthy person will develop the disease. This is predictive testing.

Most people at risk do not take it: in many countries, only 10 to 20 %. The reasons are very personal. Some prefer to know in order to plan their lives, work or family; others prefer to live without that certainty. International guidelines state that:

  • It is only done for adults, voluntarily and with informed consent.
  • It involves several appointments of genetic counselling and psychological assessment before the test, time for reflection, results given in person and follow-up afterwards.
  • The right not to know is respected, including that of other relatives (for example, a child's result may reveal a parent's status).

Couples at risk can use preimplantation diagnosis, including an "exclusion" approach that prevents the embryo from inheriting the chromosome 4 of the affected grandparent without revealing the parent's own status.

Diagnosis

In a person with symptoms, diagnosis is based on neurological examination, family history and genetic confirmation of the number of repeats. Imaging (MRI) may show shrinkage of the caudate nucleus but is not essential.

Treatment

There is currently no treatment that cures the disease or has been shown in definitive trials to slow it. But a great deal can be done:

  • Drugs for chorea, such as tetrabenazine or antipsychotics.
  • Treatment of depression, irritability and anxiety, which greatly improves quality of life.
  • Physiotherapy, speech therapy and occupational therapy, to maintain mobility, communication and safe swallowing.
  • Nutrition: many people need extra calories because of the movements.
  • Support for carers and families, care planning and advance directives.

Current research

Research aims to lower the amount of huntingtin or slow repeat expansion:

  • Antisense oligonucleotides, such as tominersen. A large trial was stopped in 2021 for lack of benefit, although the data suggest it might help younger, less advanced patients, and a new study has begun.
  • Gene therapy: a one-off injection into the brain of a virus carrying a microRNA that lowers huntingtin. In 2025 it reported preliminary results suggesting slower progression, which need confirming.
  • Oral molecules that modify how HTT RNA is processed.
  • Drugs targeting the DNA repair genes that promote expansion.
