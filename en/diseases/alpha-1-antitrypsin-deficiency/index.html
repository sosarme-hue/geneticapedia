Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency

An inherited condition that damages the lungs and liver, and is greatly underdiagnosed. Spain has one of the highest frequencies in the world of the S variant, and smoking multiplies the damage.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance
Gene SERPINA1, chromosome 14
Inheritance Autosomal codominant
Main variants Z (severe) and S (mild); the normal one is called M
Frequency of ZZ 1 in 2,000-5,000 people of European ancestry
Consequences Early emphysema, especially in smokers; liver disease
Diagnosed Only a small share of those affected

What it is

Alpha-1 antitrypsin is a protein made by the liver that travels in the blood to the lungs. There it restrains an enzyme, elastase, which white blood cells release to fight infection. Without that brake, elastase slowly destroys the walls of the air sacs and causes emphysema.

The Z and S variants

The SERPINA1 gene has many variants, named with letters according to how they move in a lab test. The normal one is M. The two most important are:

  • Z: the protein misfolds, gets stuck inside the liver and barely reaches the blood. ZZ people have blood levels of about 10-15 % of normal1.
  • S: produces somewhat less protein. On its own it causes little trouble, but the SZ combination does raise risk.

Inheritance is codominant: each copy contributes its share. Spain and Portugal have the world's highest frequency of the S variant2, with about 1 in 10 people carrying it, while Z is more common in northern Europe.

The lungs

ZZ people can develop emphysema in their 30s or 40s1, much earlier than usual COPD, with breathlessness and cough. Smoking is the decisive factor: in ZZ smokers emphysema appears about 10-15 years earlier and life expectancy is much reduced, while many ZZ non-smokers live almost normally. Exposure to dust or fumes at work also matters.

The liver

The Z protein stuck in the liver damages it in a different way:

  • In newborns: it can cause prolonged jaundice and, in some cases, serious liver disease.
  • In adults: chronic hepatitis, cirrhosis and liver cancer, especially after age 50.

Diagnosis

The blood level of alpha-1 antitrypsin is measured and, if low, the gene or protein is studied. The World Health Organization and respiratory societies recommend testing everyone with COPD or poorly controlled asthma, and anyone with unexplained liver disease. Even so, the great majority of affected people are thought not to know. When someone is diagnosed, their siblings and children should be tested.

Treatment

  • Never smoke. This is the most important measure.
  • Vaccines against flu, pneumococcus and other respiratory infections; pulmonary rehabilitation.
  • Augmentation therapy with alpha-1 antitrypsin purified from plasma, by weekly intravenous infusion3, for some people with emphysema and very low levels. It slows lung loss.
  • Lung or liver transplant in advanced cases.
  • Under investigation: RNA interference drugs such as fazirsiran, which switch off Z protein production in the liver, and gene editing to correct the Z variant.

Spain has a national registry, REDAAT, which is part of the international EARCO registry.

What to ask at your appointment

Questions you can take with you. Not all of them will have an answer on day one, and your care team may add others.

  • Which genotype do I have (PiZZ, PiSZ or another), and what is my alpha-1 antitrypsin level?
  • Am I eligible for intravenous augmentation therapy?
  • How often should my lungs and liver be checked?
  • Which vaccines do I need, and how do I avoid smoke and other exposures?
  • Should my siblings and children be tested?
