Phenylketonuria

The first inborn error of metabolism that could be detected in every newborn. Thanks to the heel-prick test and a special diet, people who have it grow up with normal development.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance
Gene PAH, chromosome 12
Inheritance Autosomal recessive
Frequency in Europe About 1 in 10,000 newborns
Detection Heel-prick test, throughout the developed world since the 1960s
Treatment Lifelong low-phenylalanine diet; medication in some cases

What it is

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is an inborn error of metabolism, the phrase coined by Archibald Garrod in 1908. The body cannot convert phenylalanine, one of the amino acids in dietary protein, into another amino acid, tyrosine. Phenylalanine builds up in the blood and is toxic to the developing brain.

Without treatment, children are born apparently healthy, but in the first months they begin to show developmental delay that progresses to severe intellectual disability, plus seizures, behavioural problems, eczema, fair skin and hair (because tyrosine is needed to make melanin) and a "mousy" smell in urine and sweat.

An exemplary medical story

In 1934 the Norwegian physician Ivar Asbjørn Følling studied two siblings with intellectual disability whose mother had noticed a strange smell in their urine. With a simple ferric chloride test he found phenylpyruvic acid in it and described the disease. In the 1950s the German paediatrician Horst Bickel showed in Birmingham, UK, that a low-phenylalanine diet prevented the damage. And in 1961-1963 Robert Guthrie invented the dried-blood-spot test that allowed every newborn to be screened.

Phenylketonuria thus became the first example of a genetic disease whose consequences can be completely prevented by changing the environment: a textbook case of how genes and environment interact.

Cause and inheritance

It is caused by variants in the PAH gene, which makes the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase, active in the liver. More than 1,000 variants are known, with effects ranging from no activity to some residual activity. According to untreated phenylalanine levels, there is classic phenylketonuria (over 1,200 µmol/L), moderate and mild forms, and mild hyperphenylalaninaemia, which sometimes needs no treatment.

It is autosomal recessive: parents are healthy carriers and each pregnancy has a 25 % chance of an affected child. About 1 in 50 people of European descent is a carrier.

In 1-2 % of cases with high phenylalanine the cause is not PAH but defects in making or recycling its cofactor, tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4). These are treated differently, so they are always looked for at diagnosis.

Diagnosis

Phenylketonuria is detected by the heel-prick test, done on every newborn in Spain in the first 48-72 hours of life. Phenylalanine is now measured by tandem mass spectrometry, which analyses dozens of diseases at once. If it is high, it is repeated and confirmed in a metabolic disease unit, which starts treatment within days.

Treatment

Diet

The basis is a low-phenylalanine diet started in the first weeks of life and kept for life:

  • Protein-rich foods are avoided: meat, fish, eggs, milk and dairy, pulses, nuts and ordinary cereals.
  • The amount of natural protein the child can eat is precisely controlled and adjusted according to regular blood tests.
  • Protein needs are met with special formulas of phenylalanine-free amino acids and special low-protein foods.
  • The sweetener aspartame, which contains phenylalanine, must be avoided. That is why products containing it state "contains a source of phenylalanine".

It is a demanding diet that shapes social and family life. For years it was recommended to relax it in adolescence, but high levels in adults are now known to cause problems with attention, mood and executive function, so "diet for life" is advised.

Medication

  • Sapropterin (a synthetic form of BH4): in people who respond, about a third, mostly with mild or moderate forms, it boosts the enzyme's residual activity and allows more protein to be eaten.
  • Pegvaliase: an enzyme of bacterial origin that breaks down phenylalanine. Injected daily under the skin in adults, it allows many to eat a normal diet, although it can cause allergic reactions.
  • Sepiapterin: an oral drug approved in 2025 in the US and Europe, which in trials lowered phenylalanine in a wider range of patients than sapropterin.

Gene therapy and gene editing to restore the enzyme in the liver are being studied.

Maternal phenylketonuria

Women with phenylketonuria who become pregnant with high phenylalanine levels may have babies with microcephaly, intellectual disability and heart defects, even though the babies do not have the disease. So it is vital to plan pregnancy and keep strict control from before conception.

What to ask at your appointment

Questions you can take with you. Not all of them will have an answer on day one, and your care team may add others.

  • Which phenylalanine levels should we keep, and how often should they be measured?
  • Could sapropterin or another treatment help on top of the diet?
  • Which special formulas and foods does the public health system cover?
  • What should the diet look like in adolescence and adulthood?
  • If a woman with phenylketonuria wants to become pregnant, when should she start controlling her diet?
