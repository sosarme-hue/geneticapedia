Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and long QT syndrome

Two inherited heart conditions that can cause sudden death in young, apparently healthy people. When a young person dies suddenly, testing their family saves lives.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

Inherited heart conditions

Some heart diseases are inherited. They are the leading cause of sudden death in people under 351, often athletes or young people with no previous symptoms. In Spain, many hospitals have inherited heart disease units, where cardiologists and geneticists examine each patient's relatives.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

It is the most common inherited heart disease. The wall of the left ventricle thickens without a cause such as high blood pressure. Many people have few or no symptoms, but it can cause:

  • Breathlessness, chest pain or dizziness on exertion, especially if the thickening obstructs blood flow out of the heart.
  • Arrhythmias, such as atrial fibrillation.
  • Sudden death, especially in young people, although it is now uncommon thanks to risk assessment.

It is caused by variants in sarcomere genes, the machinery that makes muscle contract. The most common are MYBPC3 and MYH71. Genetic testing finds the cause in about 30-60 % of cases. It is inherited in an autosomal dominant way, with very variable penetrance and severity.

Treatment: beta-blockers; mavacamten, approved in 2022-20233, the first drug to act on the cause by curbing excessive muscle contraction; surgery or alcohol septal ablation if there is obstruction; and an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator for people at high risk of sudden death. Many can do recreational sport after individual assessment.

Long QT syndrome

This is an electrical problem: the heart is structurally normal, but its cells take too long to "recharge" after each beat. The ECG shows a prolonged QT interval. That delay can trigger a dangerous arrhythmia, torsade de pointes, causing fainting or sudden death.

The genes encode potassium or sodium ion channels, and each type has typical triggers:

Type Gene Typical trigger
Long QT 1 KCNQ1 Exercise, especially swimming
Long QT 2 KCNH2 Sudden noises, such as an alarm clock; emotion; after childbirth
Long QT 3 SCN5A Sleep or rest

Treatment: beta-blockers are very protective2, especially in type 1. Drugs that prolong QT must be avoided, and there are many (some antibiotics, antidepressants and anti-sickness drugs), and low potassium from vomiting or diarrhoea must be corrected. High-risk cases get a defibrillator. People affected should always mention their diagnosis before taking a new medicine.

Sudden death in a young person: what to do

When a young person dies suddenly and unexpectedly, it is recommended to:

  1. Carry out a full post-mortem with examination of the heart and keep a sample for genetic testing: the so-called molecular autopsy1.
  2. Examine first-degree relatives with ECG, echocardiogram and, if a variant is found, a genetic test.

This identifies relatives at risk who can be protected.

What to ask at your appointment

Questions you can take with you. Not all of them will have an answer on day one, and your care team may add others.

  • What is my risk of sudden death, and do I need an implantable defibrillator?
  • Which medicines should I avoid if I have long QT?
  • Which sport can I do, and at what intensity?
  • Am I eligible for a newer treatment, such as cardiac myosin inhibitors?
  • Which relatives should have an ECG, an echocardiogram or genetic testing?
