Turner syndrome

It affects only girls and women, who are missing all or part of one of their two X chromosomes. Short stature and ovarian insufficiency are its main features, and good follow-up changes the outlook.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance
Cause Complete or partial loss of one X chromosome (karyotype 45,X and variants)
Inheritance Almost never inherited: it happens by chance when sex cells form or in the first divisions of the embryo
Frequency About 1 in 2,000–2,500 girls born
Main features Short stature, ovarian insufficiency, heart and kidney problems
Treatment Growth hormone, oestrogen, heart monitoring and follow-up of other organs

What it is

Turner syndrome is a chromosomal condition that affects only females. Instead of two X chromosomes, their cells have only one complete X; the second is missing or altered. It is named after the American endocrinologist Henry Turner, who in 1938 described seven women with short stature and absent sexual development. In 1959, the same year the cause of Down syndrome was found, Charles Ford and colleagues showed that it was due to a 45,X karyotype.

It is the only complete loss of a chromosome compatible with life, but only partly so: the great majority of 45,X embryos do not survive to term. It is estimated to be present in 1–2 % of conceptions and in a significant share of first-trimester miscarriages.

Types and cause

  • 45,X monosomy: about 40–50 % of cases2. All cells have a single X.
  • Mosaicism (for example 45,X/46,XX): some cells have two X chromosomes. This usually gives a milder picture, and some women have spontaneous periods and even pregnancies.
  • Structural changes in the X: an X with a duplicated arm (isochromosome), a ring X, or an X with a missing piece.
  • Y chromosome material in some cells: found in 5–10 %1 and raises the risk of a gonadal tumour (gonadoblastoma), so removal of the gonads is recommended.

Loss of the X does not depend on the mother's age, unlike Down syndrome. In most cases the missing X is the father's.

Why it causes symptoms

Whether a woman has an extra or a missing X matters less than one might think, because in females one X is switched off in each cell, a phenomenon explained in epigenetics. But some genes on the X escape this inactivation and are needed in a double dose. The best known is SHOX, which regulates the growth of the long bones: having only one copy explains much of the short stature.

Features

  • Short stature: the most consistent feature. Without treatment, average adult height is about 20 cm less than that of other women in the population, around 143–147 c2m.
  • Ovarian insufficiency: the ovaries form, but the eggs are lost rapidly, often before birth. Most do not start puberty without treatment and are infertile.
  • Heart and aorta: 25–50 % have a malformation1, mainly a bicuspid aortic valve and coarctation of the aorta. The most serious risk is dilatation and dissection of the aorta, which requires lifelong monitoring.
  • Kidneys: a horseshoe kidney or other changes in a third.
  • Lymphoedema of the hands and feet at birth, a broad or "webbed" neck, a low hairline, small nails.
  • Ears: frequent ear infections in childhood and hearing loss in adulthood.
  • Higher risk of hypothyroidism, coeliac disease, diabetes, high blood pressure and liver changes.
  • Intelligence is normal in the vast majority, although there may be difficulties with maths, spatial orientation and social relationships.

Diagnosis

It can be suspected before birth, from increased nuchal translucency, a cystic hygroma or a heart defect on ultrasound, or from prenatal cell-free DNA screening, which must always be confirmed with a diagnostic test. After birth it is suspected because of lymphoedema or the heart defect, and in childhood above all because of short stature or puberty that does not come. A significant number of cases are diagnosed late, in adolescence.

Confirmation is by a blood karyotype, counting at least 30 cells1 to detect mosaicism. If Y material is suspected, it is looked for with molecular techniques.

Treatment and follow-up

The 2024 international guidelines1 recommend lifelong follow-up by multidisciplinary teams:

  • Growth hormone: started as soon as height falls behind, often at age 4–6. On average it adds about 5–8 cm to adult height1.
  • Oestrogen: started at around 11–12 in low, increasing doses to induce a puberty similar to that of classmates, and continued until the usual age of menopause to protect bones and heart.
  • Fertility: in adolescents with mosaicism, egg preservation may be considered. Many women with Turner syndrome have become mothers through egg donation, but pregnancy carries a high cardiovascular risk and requires a heart assessment beforehand.
  • Heart: an echocardiogram or MRI at diagnosis and regular checks of the aorta and blood pressure.
  • Checks of hearing, thyroid, kidneys, liver, coeliac disease and glucose, plus school and psychological support.

Quality of life

Women with Turner syndrome study, work, have partners and live independently. Life expectancy is somewhat below average, mainly because of cardiovascular complications, which is why good follow-up makes a difference. Early diagnosis also allows growth hormone to be started in time and puberty to be planned.

What to ask at your appointment

Questions you can take with you. Not all of them will have an answer on day one, and your care team may add others.

  • Which heart, kidney, hearing and thyroid checks does she need?
  • When should growth hormone and oestrogen start?
  • What fertility options are there, and when should we discuss them?
  • Which heart precautions are needed if she seeks a pregnancy in the future?
  • Who will coordinate her follow-up in adult life?
