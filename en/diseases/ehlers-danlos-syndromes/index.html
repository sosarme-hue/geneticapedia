Ehlers-Danlos syndromes

A group of 13 connective tissue conditions with very loose joints, stretchy fragile skin and, in some types, a risk of arterial rupture. The most common type still has no known gene.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance
What they are Inherited connective tissue conditions, mainly of collagen
Types 13 under the 2017 international classification
Most common Hypermobile type, with no known gene
Most serious Vascular type, from variants in COL3A1
Inheritance Mostly autosomal dominant; some rare types recessive

Collagen, the body's scaffolding

Collagen is the body's most abundant protein. It forms fibres that give strength to skin, ligaments, tendons, blood vessels and organs. In the Ehlers-Danlos syndromes, because of changes in collagen genes or in the enzymes that process collagen, this scaffolding is weaker or stretchier than normal. They are named after Danish dermatologist Edvard Ehlers and Frenchman Henri-Alexandre Danlos, who described them in the early twentieth century.

Common features

  • Joint hypermobility: joints bend further than normal. It is measured with the Beighton score, from 0 to 91.
  • Frequent dislocations and partial dislocations, sprains and joint pain.
  • Soft, velvety skin that stretches a lot and is fragile, with wounds that heal poorly and easy bruising.
  • Chronic pain and fatigue, often what most affects quality of life.

The main types

Type Genes Features
Hypermobile Unknown The most common. Generalised hypermobility, pain, dislocations; skin little affected
Classical COL5A1, COL5A2 Very stretchy, fragile skin, wide thin scars
Vascular COL3A1 Thin, translucent skin, bruising; risk of rupture of arteries, bowel or uterus
Kyphoscoliotic, arthrochalasia, dermatosparaxis and others Various Very rare

The vascular type is the most dangerous: it can cause unexpected ruptures of arteries or the bowel, often between ages 20 and 402, and pregnancy carries risk. That is why it is so important to identify it. A blood pressure drug, celiprolol, appears to reduce complications3. People affected should carry information about their diagnosis for emergencies.

The hypermobile type, a diagnostic challenge

It is by far the most common, but there is no genetic test: it is diagnosed with clinical criteria set in 20171, which require generalised hypermobility, other connective tissue features and the exclusion of other causes. It overlaps with hypermobility spectrum disorder, and many people wait years for a diagnosis. It is often linked to dizziness on standing, digestive problems and anxiety, although the relationship is still being studied.

Diagnosis and treatment

  • For types with a known gene, a genetic panel confirms the diagnosis. It should always be done when the vascular type is suspected.
  • There is no cure. Physiotherapy to strengthen the muscles that stabilise joints, adapted exercise, pain management and psychological support are recommended.
  • Protecting the skin, warning surgeons beforehand and, in the vascular type, monitoring the arteries.

What to ask at your appointment

Questions you can take with you. Not all of them will have an answer on day one, and your care team may add others.

  • Which type of Ehlers-Danlos do I have, and has it been confirmed by genetic testing?
  • If it is the vascular type, which artery checks do I need?
  • Which exercise and physiotherapy help protect my joints?
  • What can I do about chronic pain and fatigue?
  • What precautions should be taken in pregnancy or surgery?
