Marfan syndrome

A connective tissue disorder affecting the aorta, eyes and skeleton. Cause, signs, diagnosis and why heart monitoring saves lives.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance
Gene FBN1 (fibrillin-1), chromosome 15
Inheritance Autosomal dominant; 25 % new cases
Frequency About 1 in 5,000 people
Main risk Dilatation and dissection of the aorta
Key point Regular echocardiograms and timely preventive surgery

What it is

Marfan syndrome is an inherited disorder of connective tissue, the "cement" that supports organs, blood vessels and bones and gives them elasticity. It was described in 1896 by the French paediatrician Antoine Marfan in a five-year-old girl with very long arms, legs and fingers.

It mainly affects three systems:

  • Cardiovascular: the wall of the aorta, the great artery leaving the heart, is weaker and tends to widen (aneurysm), especially at its root. Without monitoring it can rupture or tear (aortic dissection), a deadly emergency. Mitral valve prolapse is also common.
  • Eyes: the lens may be displaced (ectopia lentis, in about 60 % of cases2), and short-sightedness, retinal detachment, glaucoma and early cataracts are more frequent.
  • Skeleton: tall stature, long arms (arm span exceeds height), long thin fingers (arachnodactyly), very flexible joints, sunken or protruding chest, scoliosis and flat feet.

Other possible signs are stretch marks without weight change, spontaneous pneumothorax and dural ectasia (widening of the membrane around the spinal cord).

Expression is very variable, even within a family: some people have many signs and others very few.

Genetic cause

It is caused by variants in the FBN1 gene on chromosome 15, which holds the instructions for fibrillin-1. This protein forms microfibrils that give structure to elastic fibres and also regulates a signalling molecule called TGF-beta. When fibrillin fails, the tissue is weaker and excess TGF-beta signalling contributes to aortic damage. This finding, from the early 2000s, opened new avenues for treatment.

More than 3,000 different FBN1 variants have been described6.

Inheritance

It is autosomal dominant: each child of an affected person has a 50 % chance of inheriting it. About 25 % of cases2 are due to a new variant, with no family history.

Diagnosis

The revised Ghent criteria (2010)1 combine:

  • Aortic root dilatation, measured by echocardiogram and adjusted for age and body size (Z-score).
  • Ectopia lentis, checked by slit-lamp examination with dilated pupils.
  • A pathogenic FBN1 variant.
  • Family history.
  • A systemic score of skeletal and other signs.

For example, aortic dilatation together with ectopia lentis is enough for diagnosis. Genetic testing is especially useful to confirm doubtful cases, diagnose children and test relatives.

It must be distinguished from other conditions with similar signs, such as Loeys-Dietz syndrome (with more aggressive aneurysms, caused by other TGF-beta pathway genes), vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome or homocystinuria. Many tall, slim people with long fingers do not have Marfan syndrome.

Monitoring and treatment

Life expectancy, which was around 45 years in the 1970s, is now close to normal thanks to monitoring and preventive surgery.

  • Echocardiogram at diagnosis, at six months and then at least yearly, or more often if the aorta is growing. Sometimes MRI or CT of the whole aorta is added.
  • Drugs that reduce stress on the aorta: beta-blockers and angiotensin II receptor blockers (such as losartan). A 2022 meta-analysis3 of the major trials showed that both slow aortic root growth and that their effect is additive.
  • Preventive surgery on the aortic root when it reaches about 50 mm in adults4, or earlier if it grows fast, there is a family history of dissection or a pregnancy is planned. Valve-sparing techniques avoid lifelong anticoagulation.
  • Annual eye checks and monitoring of scoliosis.

Physical activity

Staying active with moderate aerobic exercise (walking, swimming, gentle cycling) is recommended. Contact sports, intense isometric effort (heavy weights) and high-intensity competitive sport are discouraged because of the risk to the aorta and eyes.

Pregnancy

Pregnancy increases the risk of aortic dissection, especially if the root measures more than 40-45 mm2. It should be planned with cardiology and obstetrics, with frequent checks and delivery in a hospital with cardiac surgery. Genetic counselling is recommended to discuss reproductive options.

Warning signs

Sudden, severe chest or back pain, "like tearing", in a person with Marfan syndrome is an emergency: call 112 (or your local emergency number) and mention the diagnosis.

What to ask at your appointment

Questions you can take with you. Not all of them will have an answer on day one, and your care team may add others.

  • What is the diameter of my aorta, and how often should I have an echocardiogram or MRI?
  • Should I take a beta blocker or an angiotensin receptor blocker?
  • Which sports and exertion should I avoid?
  • What precautions do I need if I become pregnant?
  • Should my first-degree relatives be tested?
