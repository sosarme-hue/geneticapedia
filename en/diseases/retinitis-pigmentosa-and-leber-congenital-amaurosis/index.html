Retinitis pigmentosa and Leber congenital amaurosis

Inherited retinal dystrophies are the leading cause of genetic blindness. More than 300 genes can be involved, and one of them got the first gene therapy approved for an inherited disease.

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance
What they are Conditions in which the light-sensing cells of the retina slowly die
Frequency Retinitis pigmentosa affects 1 in 3,000-4,000 people
Genes More than 300 retinal genes; about 100 for retinitis pigmentosa
Inheritance Autosomal dominant, recessive or X-linked
Gene treatment Voretigene neparvovec for RPE65 variants

The retina and its cells

The retina is the layer at the back of the eye that turns light into nerve signals. It has two kinds of photoreceptors: rods, which work in dim light and in the periphery of vision, and cones, which give colour and fine detail in the centre. Keeping them alive needs hundreds of different proteins, and a fault in any of them can cause an inherited retinal dystrophy.

Retinitis pigmentosa

This is the most common. The rods die first, then the cones:

  1. Night blindness: difficulty seeing at night or in the dark, often from childhood or adolescence.
  2. Tunnel vision: peripheral vision is gradually lost. The person bumps into things or does not see someone approaching from the side.
  3. In advanced stages, central vision is affected.

The back of the eye shows pigment deposits shaped like bone spicules, which give the condition its name. It progresses over decades, at a speed that depends greatly on the gene.

It can be inherited in an autosomal dominant way (about 30-40 % of cases), recessive (50-60 %) or X-linked (5-15 %, mainly through the RPGR gene, with men more severely affected). A quarter of cases come with other symptoms, as in Usher syndrome, which combines retinitis pigmentosa and deafness.

Leber congenital amaurosis

This is the most severe and earliest form: the baby is born with very poor vision or loses it in the first months, with wobbling eye movements (nystagmus) and poor pupil responses. About 25 genes are known, such as CEP290, GUCY2D and RPE65. It is almost always autosomal recessive. It should not be confused with Leber hereditary optic neuropathy, a disease of the optic nerve caused by changes in mitochondrial DNA.

The first gene therapy

The RPE65 gene makes an enzyme needed to regenerate visual pigment. In people with two variants in this gene, the photoreceptors survive for a while but do not work. An adeno-associated virus injected under the retina delivers a healthy copy of the gene.

The drug, voretigene neparvovec, was approved in the United States in 2017 and in Europe in 2018: it was the first gene therapy for an inherited disease in the United States. It improves vision in dim light, although it only works for this gene, which causes a small share of cases, and while living cells remain.

Trials are under way for other genes, such as RPGR and CEP290, including CRISPR gene editing inside the eye, as well as gene-independent approaches such as optogenetics and artificial retinas.

Diagnosis and follow-up

  • Examination of the back of the eye, visual fields, optical coherence tomography and electroretinography, which measures the retina's electrical response.
  • Genetic testing with a retinal gene panel finds the cause in about 60-70 % of cases. Knowing the gene gives a prognosis, the risk for relatives and access to trials.
  • Low-vision aids, protection from bright light and, when vision falls a lot, rehabilitation and services for blind people.
