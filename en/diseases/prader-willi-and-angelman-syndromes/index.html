Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes

Two very different conditions caused by the same stretch of chromosome 15. Which one appears depends on whether the change comes from the father or the mother: the best example of genomic imprinting.

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance

Prader-Willi Angelman
Region 15q11-q13 15q11-q13
Missing copy Father's Mother's
Key gene Several, including SNORD116 UBE3A
Frequency 1 in 15,000-25,000 births 1 in 12,000-20,000
Main features Floppiness at birth, then insatiable hunger, short stature Severe intellectual disability, no speech, epilepsy, frequent laughter

One region, two conditions

We inherit two copies of almost every gene, one from each parent, and the two usually work the same way. But a few hundred genes are imprinted: an epigenetic mark switches off the copy from one parent, so only the other parent's copy works.

In a region of the long arm of chromosome 15, several genes work only on the father's copy, and one, UBE3A, works only on the mother's copy in neurons. So:

  • If the father's part is missing, the paternal genes are lost and Prader-Willi syndrome results.
  • If the mother's part is missing, UBE3A is lost in the brain and Angelman syndrome results.

How it happens

Mechanism Prader-Willi Angelman
Deletion of the region Paternal, 65-75 % Maternal, about 70 %
Uniparental disomy: both chromosome 15s from one parent Two maternal, 20-30 % Two paternal, 2-7 %
Imprinting defect Under 5 % About 3 %
Variant in UBE3A Not applicable About 10 %

Almost all cases are new in the family and the recurrence risk is low, except in some imprinting defects and UBE3A variants inherited from the mother, where it can reach 50 %. That is why genetic counselling is advisable.

Prader-Willi syndrome

It was described in 1956 by the Swiss doctors Andrea Prader, Alexis Labhart and Heinrich Willi. It has two phases:

  1. At birth: the baby is very floppy (hypotonic), barely sucks and often needs tube feeding.
  2. From age 2-4: hyperphagia appears, a hunger that is never satisfied because the hypothalamus does not send the fullness signal. Without control of access to food, it leads to severe obesity, the main cause of complications.

There is also short stature, small hands and feet, hypogonadism, mild or moderate intellectual disability and behavioural problems such as tantrums or obsessive behaviour.

Treatment: growth hormone, started in childhood, improves height, muscle tone and body composition. Strict control of the environment, with food locked away, is essential. In 2025 the United States approved the first drug for hyperphagia, diazoxide choline.

Angelman syndrome

It was described in 1965 by British paediatrician Harry Angelman. Children have:

  • Severe intellectual disability and almost no spoken language, although they understand more than they can express and many communicate with signs or tablets.
  • Balance problems and jerky movements.
  • Epilepsy in more than 80 %.
  • A very happy demeanour, with frequent laughter and a fascination with water.
  • Sleep problems.

Treatment: antiseizure medication, physiotherapy, speech therapy and augmentative communication. Research is very active: because the father's copy of UBE3A is intact but switched off, several antisense oligonucleotides aim to switch it on, and are in phase 3 clinical trials.

Diagnosis

A methylation test of the 15q11-q13 region detects more than 99 % of Prader-Willi cases and about 80 % of Angelman cases. The mechanism is then studied, because it changes the risk for other children. In Angelman, if methylation is normal, UBE3A is sequenced.
