Hereditary haemochromatosis

The most common genetic disorder in people of European descent. The body absorbs too much iron and stores it for decades, but if it is caught in time it is treated with something as simple as taking blood.

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance
Gene HFE, chromosome 6 (the C282Y variant in more than 80 % of cases)
Inheritance Autosomal recessive, with incomplete penetrance
Frequency About 1 in 200 people of northern European origin has two copies of C282Y; somewhat fewer in southern Europe
Diagnosis Blood tests (ferritin and transferrin saturation) and genetic testing
Treatment Regular blood removal (venesection)

What it is

Hereditary haemochromatosis is a disorder in which the gut absorbs more iron than the body needs. Because the body has no active way of getting rid of excess iron, it is slowly deposited in the liver, pancreas, heart, joints, skin and endocrine glands.

The process is silent. A person with the risk variant may take in a few extra milligrams a day for decades, and symptoms do not usually appear until the age of 40–60 in men and somewhat later in women, who are partly protected because menstruation and pregnancy make them lose iron regularly.

Why it happens

Iron is essential for making haemoglobin, but in excess it is toxic: it promotes free radicals that damage cells. Its absorption is therefore tightly controlled by a hormone made by the liver, hepcidin. When there is enough iron, hepcidin rises and "closes the door" in the gut.

In haemochromatosis the liver does not make enough hepcidin. The door stays open and iron keeps coming in even when there is too much. The HFE protein is part of the system that senses how much iron is in the blood, and when it fails, the signal to make hepcidin weakens.

In 1996 John Feder's team identified the HFE gene and its main variant, C282Y, which swaps a cysteine for a tyrosine at position 282 of the protein. Another common variant, H63D, has a much milder effect. Rarer forms not linked to HFE are caused by variants in the genes for hemojuvelin (HJV), hepcidin itself (HAMP), transferrin receptor 2 (TFR2) or ferroportin (SLC40A1). Some of these, known as juvenile haemochromatosis, cause severe symptoms before the age of 30.

Inheritance with nuances

HFE haemochromatosis is autosomal recessive: the variant must be inherited from both parents. But having two copies of C282Y (being "homozygous") does not mean becoming ill. It is a classic example of incomplete penetrance:

  • Many homozygous people have a somewhat raised ferritin, but only a minority develop organ damage.
  • In a large Australian study, about 28 % of homozygous men and 1 % of women developed iron-related disease over the years.
  • Sex, alcohol, excess weight and fatty liver, other genetic variants and diet all play a part.

The C282Y variant probably arose in a single ancestor in northern Europe about 6,000 years ago, and is especially common in Ireland, Scotland, Brittany and Scandinavia. It has been suggested that absorbing more iron may have been an advantage at times of poor diets.

Symptoms

The first symptoms are vague and easily mistaken for something else: tiredness, joint pain (especially in the knuckles of the second and third fingers, the "painful handshake" sign), loss of libido or abdominal pain.

If iron keeps building up, it can cause:

  • Liver: enlargement, fibrosis and eventually cirrhosis, with a greatly increased risk of liver cancer (hepatocellular carcinoma).
  • Pancreas: diabetes. It used to be called "bronze diabetes" because it came with skin pigmentation.
  • Heart: cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias.
  • Joints: arthritis that resembles osteoarthritis and may not improve with treatment.
  • Glands: hypogonadism, with erectile dysfunction or loss of periods.
  • Skin: a bronzed or greyish tone.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis starts with a blood test:

  • Transferrin saturation (how much of the protein that carries iron is occupied) is the earliest sign. A value above 45 % raises suspicion.
  • Ferritin reflects the body's iron stores. Note that it also rises with inflammation, alcohol, obesity or fatty liver, so a high ferritin alone is not enough to diagnose haemochromatosis.

If both are raised, HFE genetic testing is done. Being C282Y homozygous with high iron confirms the diagnosis. Sometimes an MRI scan is used to measure liver iron, and a liver biopsy is needed only in particular cases, such as suspected cirrhosis. Guidelines recommend assessing liver fibrosis when ferritin is above 1,000 µg/L.

When someone is diagnosed, their brothers and sisters should be tested, as they have a 25 % chance of also being homozygous.

Treatment

The treatment is old, simple and very effective: venesection, which means removing blood regularly, just like a blood donation. Each session of about 450–500 ml removes around 200–250 mg of iron, because the body uses it to make new red blood cells.

  • In the initial phase, blood is taken every week or two until ferritin falls to about 50 µg/L. This can take months or more than a year.
  • In the maintenance phase, a few sessions a year are enough.
  • In many countries, including the UK, this blood can be donated if the person meets donor requirements.

If venesection is not possible (because of anaemia or heart problems), iron chelators or erythrocytapheresis, which removes only red cells, are used.

People are also advised to avoid alcohol if there is liver damage, not to take iron or vitamin C supplements, and not to eat raw shellfish, because a marine bacterium, Vibrio vulnificus, which thrives on iron, causes severe infections in them.

Outlook

If treated before cirrhosis or diabetes develops, life expectancy is normal. Tiredness and some liver changes improve, although joint damage and established cirrhosis do not go away. Early detection is therefore the key. Population screening is debated, but at present testing is mainly recommended for relatives and for people with abnormal iron tests.
