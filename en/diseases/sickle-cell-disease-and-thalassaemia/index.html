Sickle cell disease and thalassaemia

Haemoglobin disorders are the most common serious inherited diseases in the world. Cause, symptoms, the link with malaria, diagnosis and treatments, including the first CRISPR therapy.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance
Genes HBB (beta chain, chromosome 11); HBA1 and HBA2 (alpha chain, chromosome 16)
Inheritance Autosomal recessive
Worldwide frequency Over 300,000 births a year with sickle cell disease and tens of thousands with severe thalassaemia
In Spain Sickle cell disease in the heel-prick test; thalassaemia carriers are common
Treatments Hydroxyurea, transfusions, transplantation, gene therapy and CRISPR editing

Haemoglobin

Haemoglobin is the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to the tissues. In adults it is made mainly of two alpha and two beta chains (haemoglobin A). Before birth the fetus makes another form, fetal haemoglobin, with gamma chains instead of beta, which captures oxygen from the placenta more efficiently. After birth a genetic "switch" turns fetal haemoglobin off and adult haemoglobin on.

Variants in the genes for these chains cause haemoglobinopathies. There are two large groups:

  • Structural variants, in which haemoglobin is made with one amino acid changed. The most important is haemoglobin S, of sickle cell disease.
  • Thalassaemias, in which too little of one of the chains is made.

Sickle cell disease

The cause

It is caused by a change in a single letter of the HBB gene: an adenine replaced by a thymine, which swaps the glutamic acid at position 6 of the beta chain for a valine. It was the first disease explained at the molecular level, by Linus Pauling in 1949 and Vernon Ingram in 1957.

The altered haemoglobin (HbS) clumps into rigid fibres when oxygen is low and bends red cells into a sickle shape. These rigid, sticky cells:

  • break down early (living about 10-20 days instead of 120)1, causing chronic anaemia;
  • block small blood vessels, causing very intense pain crises and damage to almost any organ.

Symptoms and complications

Symptoms begin around 6 months of age, when fetal haemoglobin disappears:

  • Vaso-occlusive crises: intense pain in bones, chest or abdomen, often triggered by cold, dehydration, infection or stress. In babies, painful swelling of hands and feet.
  • Serious infections, because the spleen is damaged in the first years and stops working.
  • Acute chest syndrome, a lung complication that can be fatal.
  • Stroke, even in childhood.
  • Chronic damage to kidneys, eyes, bones (hip necrosis), heart and lungs; leg ulcers; priapism.

It shortens life: in low-income countries, most affected children die before age 5 without diagnosis or treatment.

Inheritance and frequency

It is autosomal recessive. People with a single HbS copy have sickle cell trait: they are usually healthy, although in extreme conditions, such as severe dehydration or high altitude, they can have complications.

Every year more than 300,000 children3,7 are born with sickle cell disease worldwide, most in sub-Saharan Africa, India and the Middle East. With migration it is increasingly common in Europe: in Spain it is included in the heel-prick test in every region.

The link with malaria

Why is such a harmful variant so common? Because carriers are protected against severe malaria6. In regions where malaria has been common for thousands of years, carriers survived longer and had more children, and the variant reached frequencies of up to 20-30 %. Anthony Allison proposed this in 1954, and it is one of the clearest examples of natural selection in humans. Something similar happens with thalassaemias.

Thalassaemias

In thalassaemias too little alpha chain (alpha-thalassaemia) or beta chain (beta-thalassaemia) is made. Red cells are small and pale, and the bone marrow tries to compensate by making more. They are common around the Mediterranean (the name comes from the Greek thalassa, sea), the Middle East, India and Southeast Asia. In Spain between 0.5 and 2 % of the population carries beta-thalassaemia, depending on the area.

There are different degrees:

  • Carrier (thalassaemia trait or minor): mild or no anaemia, small red cells. It is very common and often mistaken for iron deficiency. It needs no treatment, and taking iron when it is not low can be harmful. It is important to know before a pregnancy, in case the partner is also a carrier.
  • Thalassaemia intermedia or non-transfusion-dependent: moderate anaemia.
  • Thalassaemia major (severe beta-thalassaemia or Cooley's anaemia): severe anaemia from the first year of life, needing transfusions every 2-4 weeks2 for life. Transfusions build up iron in the heart, liver and glands, so iron chelators are needed.
  • In alpha-thalassaemia, the most severe form (absence of all four alpha genes) is usually incompatible with life before birth.

Diagnosis

  • Newborn screening for sickle cell disease.
  • Full blood count: small red cells (low MCV) with normal iron point to thalassaemia.
  • Haemoglobin electrophoresis or chromatography: identifies HbS and measures the HbA2 and fetal fractions, raised in beta-thalassaemia.
  • Genetic testing: confirms the type, is essential in alpha-thalassaemia and allows prenatal or preimplantation diagnosis.
  • Carrier screening for couples of Mediterranean, African or Asian origin planning a pregnancy.

Treatment

Sickle cell disease

  • Infection prevention: daily penicillin in childhood and vaccines.
  • Hydroxyurea: increases fetal haemoglobin1, which stops HbS forming fibres. It reduces pain crises, acute chest syndrome, transfusions and mortality. It is standard treatment from 9 months of age.
  • Annual transcranial Doppler ultrasound between ages 2 and 16 to detect stroke risk, with regular transfusions if it is high.
  • Prompt, adequate pain treatment during crises.
  • Bone marrow transplantation from a matched sibling: can cure, especially in children.

Thalassaemia major

  • Regular transfusions and iron chelation (deferoxamine, deferasirox, deferiprone).
  • Luspatercept: a drug that improves red-cell maturation and reduces transfusion needs in adults.
  • Bone marrow transplantation from a matched donor.

Gene therapy and gene editing

Haemoglobin disorders have been pioneers of genetic medicine:

  • Exagamglogene autotemcel (Casgevy), approved in the UK in November 2023, in the US in December 2023 and in the European Union in early 20244,5, is the first CRISPR-based medicine. The patient's own blood stem cells are collected, a "switch" in the BCL11A gene is edited with CRISPR to turn fetal haemoglobin back on, and the cells are reinfused. In trials, over 90 % of patients with sickle cell disease were free of severe crises for at least a year, and the great majority of patients with thalassaemia no longer needed transfusions.
  • Lentiviral gene therapies, which add a working haemoglobin gene to stem cells.

These are complex treatments: they require prior chemotherapy to clear the bone marrow, with effects on fertility, long hospital stays and a price of millions of euros per patient. The great challenge is to make them available in the countries where the disease is most common.

What to ask at your appointment

Questions you can take with you. Not all of them will have an answer on day one, and your care team may add others.

  • Which form do I have exactly: sickle cell disease, beta-thalassaemia major or intermedia, or trait?
  • Do I need hydroxyurea, transfusions or iron chelation?
  • Am I a candidate for a bone marrow transplant or gene therapy, such as CRISPR editing?
  • Which vaccines and measures protect me from infections and pain crises?
  • Should my partner be tested before a pregnancy?
