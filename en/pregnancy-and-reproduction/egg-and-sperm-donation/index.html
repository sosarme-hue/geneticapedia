Pregnancy and reproduction

Egg and sperm donation

Using eggs or sperm from a donor is an option when there is a genetic condition a couple do not want to pass on. How donors are selected, which genetic tests they have and what the law says.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

When it is an option

Gamete donation is used mainly for infertility, but it is also an option for genetic reasons:

  • When one partner has a serious dominant condition, such as Huntington's disease or myotonic dystrophy, and does not want to pass it on.
  • When the woman carries an X-linked condition, such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy or fragile X syndrome.
  • When both partners carry the same recessive condition and prefer not to use preimplantation testing.
  • When preimplantation testing is not possible or has failed several times.

With a donated gamete the risk of passing on the family condition is removed, or greatly reduced. In return, the child does not share the genetics of one parent, something each couple experiences differently.

How donation works in Spain

Law 14/2006 establishes that donation is:

  • Anonymous: recipients and children can know general information about the donor, but not their identity, except in exceptional circumstances of danger to the child's life or health. There is an open debate about changing this, as countries such as the UK, Sweden and Portugal have done.
  • Altruistic: it cannot be for profit, although donors are compensated for inconvenience and travel costs. Compensation is higher for egg donation, which requires hormone stimulation and a needle procedure.
  • Limited: each donor may give rise to a maximum of six children born in Spain.

Donors must be over 18 and in good health. Under the regulations, egg donors are at most 35 and sperm donors at most 50. Both undergo a medical and psychological assessment.

Genetic tests for donors

The law requires donors to be tested to rule out, according to current scientific knowledge, genetic, hereditary or infectious diseases that could be passed to children. In addition to a personal and family medical history and infection tests, in practice many clinics carry out:

  • A karyotype, to rule out chromosome abnormalities.
  • Carrier screening, often expanded to hundreds of recessive conditions.
  • Testing for cystic fibrosis and spinal muscular atrophy, the most common.

When the recipient carries a recessive condition, a donor who does not carry the same one is chosen. This greatly reduces the risk but does not remove it completely: no test detects every variant.

DNA tests and anonymity

Direct-to-consumer genetic tests have changed the picture: many donor-conceived people have now found their donor or half-siblings by comparing their DNA in genealogy databases. The anonymity guaranteed by law is, in practice, increasingly difficult to maintain. See direct-to-consumer genetic tests.

Talking to children

Family associations and psychologists recommend telling children how they were conceived, naturally and from an early age. Studies show that children who have always known cope better than those who find out by surprise in adolescence or adulthood.
