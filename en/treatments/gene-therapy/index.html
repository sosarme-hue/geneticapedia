Gene therapy

Treating a disease by adding, changing or silencing genes. From the early failures to approved treatments.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

The idea

If a disease is caused by a gene that does not work, why not give cells a copy that does? That is the basic idea of gene therapy. Today the term also covers silencing harmful genes and editing them.

How the gene reaches the cells

DNA does not enter cells on its own. Vectors are used, almost always viruses modified so they cannot cause disease:

  • Adeno-associated viruses (AAV): widely used to deliver genes to the liver, eye, muscle or neurons. They do not integrate into the cell's DNA.
  • Lentiviruses: integrate into the DNA and are used to modify stem cells or immune cells in the laboratory.

Inside or outside the body

  • In vivo: the vector is injected directly into the patient, into the blood, the eye or the cerebrospinal fluid. This is usual with AAVs, for example in spinal muscular atrophy (onasemnogene abeparvovec), RPE65 retinal dystrophy or haemophilia B (etranacogene dezaparvovec).
  • Ex vivo: cells are taken from the patient (usually blood stem cells or lymphocytes), modified in the laboratory with a lentivirus and returned. This is how therapies for ADA deficiency, metachromatic leukodystrophy (atidarsagene autotemcel) and CAR-T cells work.

A history of ups and downs

  • 1990: Ashanti DeSilva, a 4-year-old girl with severe immunodeficiency due to ADA deficiency, receives the first authorised gene therapy treatment in the United States.
  • 1999: Jesse Gelsinger, aged 18, dies from a severe immune reaction in a trial. The field slows down and oversight is tightened.
  • Early 2000s: in a French trial for another immunodeficiency, several children develop leukaemia because the vector integrated next to a cancer-promoting gene. Vectors are redesigned.
  • 2016: Europe approves Strimvelis for ADA deficiency.
  • 2017: the United States approves voretigene neparvovec (Luxturna) for an inherited retinal dystrophy caused by the RPE65 gene, and the first CAR-T therapies, in which a patient's lymphocytes are modified to attack some blood cancers.
  • 2019–2020: gene therapy arrives for spinal muscular atrophy.
  • 2022–2023: gene therapies for haemophilia A and B.

Results that have changed lives

  • In type 1 spinal muscular atrophy, babies who without treatment never sat up or lived beyond two years have learned to sit, and some to walk, especially if treated before symptoms appear, which underlines the importance of newborn screening.
  • In haemophilia B, a single infusion has allowed most patients to stop preventive factor injections for years.
  • In metachromatic leukodystrophy, treating children before symptoms largely prevents neurological decline.

Safety: lessons up to 2025

High doses of AAV can damage the liver or trigger serious immune reactions. In 2025, deaths from acute liver failure were reported in patients treated with a gene therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy, which led to restrictions on its use in some groups and a review of its benefit–risk balance. In Europe, the European Medicines Agency did not authorise that product. These are reminders that such therapies require expert centres and long-term follow-up.

Current challenges

  • Price: several cost over a million euros per patient, raising problems of access and sustainability for public health systems.
  • Durability: it is not always known how many years the effect lasts.
  • Immunity: many people have antibodies against AAVs and cannot receive them, and they can normally be given only once.
  • Safety: treated patients must be monitored over the long term.

Spain, a pioneer in academic CAR-T

In 2021 Spain's medicines agency (AEMPS) authorised ARI-0001 (varnimcabtagene autoleucel), a CAR-T therapy developed and manufactured by Hospital Clínic de Barcelona for adult acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, the first academic CAR-T authorised in Europe under the hospital exemption. It shows how public hospitals can produce advanced therapies at lower cost.

Beware of unregulated offers

Some clinics offer unauthorised "gene therapies" or "stem cell therapies", often abroad and at high prices. A genuine treatment is offered in hospitals, within registered clinical trials or with authorisation from the European Medicines Agency or a national regulator.
