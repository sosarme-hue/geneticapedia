FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Short, clear answers to the most common questions about genetics, inheritance and genetic tests.

01

Is cancer inherited?

Hardly ever. Only 5 to 10 % of cancers are due to an inherited variant. When to suspect it and what to do.

Read →02

What does it mean to be a carrier of a genetic condition?

Having one altered copy of a gene without having the condition. We are all carriers of something; it only matters if your partner carries the same thing.

Read →03

Is an ancestry test worth it?

It can be fun and find relatives, but its origin percentages are estimates, it is not a reliable medical test, and you hand over data that affect your whole family.

Read →04

Does public healthcare cover genetic tests?

Yes, when there is a medical reason. In Spain genetic tests are part of the National Health System's common portfolio and are requested through specialists.

Read →05

If my father or mother has a genetic condition, will I get it?

It depends on the type of inheritance, and it is almost never 100 % certain. How to work out the risk and when to be tested.

Read →06

Can I change my genes with diet or exercise?

Your DNA sequence does not change, but how your genes are used does. What epigenetics says and what it does not.

Read →07

What is a "variant of uncertain significance"?

A DNA change for which it is not yet known whether it causes disease. It is not a positive result and should not be used to make medical decisions.

Read →08

Why are brothers and sisters different?

Because each child receives a different mix of their parents' genes. Two siblings share on average half of their variants.

Read →09

Is it risky to have children with a cousin?

The risk of a genetic condition or malformation increases, but less than people often think: from about 2–3 % to about 4–6 % for first cousins.

Read →10

Do twins have the same DNA?

Identical twins have almost the same DNA, but not exactly. Non-identical twins are as genetically similar as any pair of siblings.
