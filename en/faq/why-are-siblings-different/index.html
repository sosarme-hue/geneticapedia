FAQ

Why are brothers and sisters different?

Because each child receives a different mix of their parents' genes. Two siblings share on average half of their variants.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

Short answer

Because every egg and every sperm carries a unique combination of each parent's genes. Two siblings (other than identical twins) share on average half of the variants they inherit from their parents, not all of them.

A little more

Each of us has 23 pairs of chromosomes. When eggs and sperm are formed, two things happen:

  1. Random sorting: from each pair, one chromosome or the other goes in. That alone gives more than 8 million possible combinations per parent (2 to the power of 23).
  2. Recombination: before sorting, the chromosomes in each pair swap pieces, so each chromosome passed on is a new mosaic of the grandfather's and grandmother's.

The result is that the number of possible combinations is practically infinite. That is why two siblings can have different eye colours, very different heights, or one inherit a condition and the other not.

Gregor Mendel glimpsed this with his peas: it is his law of independent assortment. See Mendel's laws.

Add to this the environment, experiences and chance during development, which make even identical twins different. See do twins have the same DNA?.
