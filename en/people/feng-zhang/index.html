People

Feng Zhang

The bioengineer who adapted CRISPR to edit genes in human cells in 2013, and later developed tools to edit RNA and detect viruses.

Who he is

Feng Zhang was born in 1981 in Shijiazhuang, China, and at 11 emigrated with his mother to Des Moines, Iowa. At high school he was already volunteering in a gene therapy lab. He studied chemistry and physics at Harvard and earned his doctorate at Stanford, where he helped develop optogenetics, a technique to control neurons with light. In 2011 he opened his lab at the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard.

CRISPR in human cells

In 2012 Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna showed that the bacterial protein Cas9, guided by an RNA, could cut DNA in a test tube. The big question was whether it would work in animal and human cells, where DNA is packed in the nucleus.

In January 2013, Zhang's team reported in Science that they had edited genes in human and mouse cells with CRISPR-Cas9, and could edit several genes at once. The same issue included similar work by George Church. Zhang shared his tools with thousands of labs worldwide through a non-profit repository, which helped CRISPR spread extremely fast. See CRISPR explained.

The patent war

The Broad Institute and the University of California, where Doudna works, fought for years over CRISPR patents in eukaryotic cells. The US patent office sided with the Broad in 2022, although litigation has continued in the courts and the situation in Europe is different. See Can genes be patented?.

Beyond Cas9

His lab has discovered and adapted other tools:

  • Cas12a (Cpf1), another DNA-cutting enzyme that cuts differently.
  • Cas13, which cuts RNA instead of DNA, allowing RNA to be edited without changing the genome.
  • SHERLOCK, a Cas13-based diagnostic test that can detect viruses such as Zika or SARS-CoV-2 with high sensitivity.
  • Systems for delivering genes into cells using human proteins instead of viruses.

Shared credit

The 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went only to Charpentier and Doudna. Many consider that the roles of Zhang, and of Spain's Francisco Mojica, who discovered the CRISPR repeats, were also decisive. In 2017 the BBVA Foundation honoured Charpentier, Doudna and Zhang together with Mojica.

See also: Ethics of gene editing and Gene therapy.
