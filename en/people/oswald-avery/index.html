Oswald Avery

He set out to improve the treatment of pneumonia and ended up proving, in 1944, that genes are made of DNA. He never received the Nobel Prize.

Who he was

Oswald Theodore Avery was born in Halifax, Canada, in 1877 and grew up in New York. He studied medicine at Columbia University and from 1913 worked at the hospital of the Rockefeller Institute. There he devoted almost his entire career to pneumococcus, the bacterium that then caused most fatal pneumonias. He was a quiet, meticulous researcher who shunned the limelight; his colleagues called him "Fess", for professor.

The starting point

In 1928 the British medical officer Frederick Griffith had observed something strange: when mice were injected with harmless pneumococci together with virulent pneumococci killed by heat, the mice died, and live virulent pneumococci appeared in their blood. Something had passed from the dead bacteria to the living ones and had transformed them in a heritable way.

The experiment

For more than ten years, Avery, Colin MacLeod and later Maclyn McCarty purified this "transforming principle". One by one, they removed the components of the extracts:

  • Enzymes that destroyed proteins did not stop transformation.
  • Nor did those that destroyed RNA.
  • Those that destroyed DNA abolished it completely.

The purified material also had the chemical composition of DNA. In February 1944 they reported in the Journal of Experimental Medicine that the transforming principle was deoxyribonucleic acid.

Doubts and recognition

Many scientists were slow to accept it. DNA was thought too simple a molecule, with only four "letters", and genetic information was assumed to lie in proteins, which are far more varied. Some suspected traces of protein remained in the samples. Avery, cautious, avoided claiming in the paper that DNA was the gene, although in a letter to his brother he wrote that the substance behaved like "a virus or a gene".

Hershey and Chase's 1952 experiment with viruses that infect bacteria, and the double helix in 1953, convinced everyone. Avery was nominated for the Nobel Prize several times but died in 1955 without receiving it: one of the most discussed omissions in the prize's history.

Why it matters today

Everything that came afterwards (the double helix, the genetic code, sequencing, CRISPR) rests on the certainty that genes are DNA. The cover photograph of Geneticapedia shows McCarty, the youngest of the trio, with Watson and Crick.
