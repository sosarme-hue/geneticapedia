Mary-Claire King

In 1990 she showed that a gene predisposing to hereditary breast cancer exists: BRCA1. She also used DNA to give stolen children in Argentina back their identity.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

In this article

  1. Who she is
  2. The search for BRCA1
  3. The Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo
  4. Why it matters today
  5. Sources

Who she is

Mary-Claire King was born in Illinois in 1946. She studied mathematics and earned her PhD in genetics at the University of California, Berkeley, with Allan Wilson. Her thesis, published in 1975, showed that human and chimpanzee genes are more than 99 % identical and proposed that differences between species depend mainly on how genes are regulated. She now works at the University of Washington in Seattle.

The search for BRCA1

In the 1970s almost nobody believed breast cancer could have an important inherited component: it was thought too common and too varied. Over 17 years King gathered data from hundreds of families with many cases of breast cancer at young ages. Using statistical methods she showed that in some of them the risk was passed on like a high-penetrance dominant gene.

In 1990 her team reported in Science that this gene lay on chromosome 17 (region 17q21). She called it BRCA1. Four years later, in 1994, a team from the University of Utah and the company Myriad Genetics isolated the gene. A second gene, BRCA2, on chromosome 13, was identified in 1995.

The Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo

From 1984 King worked with the Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo in Argentina, who were searching for grandchildren stolen by the military dictatorship (1976-1983). Many of the children's parents had been murdered, so the children's DNA could not be compared with theirs. King used mitochondrial DNA, which is inherited only through the mother and allows a child to be matched with a grandmother. Thanks to these techniques, more than 130 grandchildren have recovered their identity. She has also worked with truth commissions to identify victims in other countries.

Why it matters today

Variants in BRCA1 and BRCA2 greatly increase the risk of breast and ovarian cancer, and also of prostate and pancreatic cancer. Testing makes prevention possible (more frequent screening, risk-reducing surgery) as well as targeted treatments such as PARP inhibitors. King argues that testing should be offered to all women from a certain age, not only to those with a family history.

See also: Hereditary cancer and Alec Jeffreys.
