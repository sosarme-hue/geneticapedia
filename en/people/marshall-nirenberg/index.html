Marshall Nirenberg

A young, almost unknown NIH researcher who in 1961 read the first "word" of the genetic code using a test tube and an RNA made only of uracil.

Who he was

Marshall Warren Nirenberg was born in New York in 1927 and grew up in Florida. He studied zoology and chemistry and earned his PhD in biochemistry at the University of Michigan. In 1957 he joined the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) in Bethesda, where he worked for the rest of his life.

The 1961 experiment

By the late 1950s it was known that DNA stores information, that RNA carries it and that proteins are made on ribosomes. Crick and Brenner suspected the code used triplets of bases. But nobody knew which triplet corresponded to which amino acid.

Nirenberg and the German researcher J. Heinrich Matthaei prepared a cell-free system: an extract of E. coli bacteria with ribosomes and enzymes, able to make proteins in a test tube. They added an artificial RNA made only of uracil (poly-U), produced with Severo Ochoa's enzyme. In the early hours of 27 May 1961, Matthaei saw that this RNA produced a protein made of a single amino acid: phenylalanine. The first word of the code was UUU = phenylalanine.

Nirenberg presented the result at a congress in Moscow in August 1961 to an almost empty room. Crick, on hearing of it, had him repeat the talk at the main session. The news electrified the field.

The race for the code

Five years of intense competition with other laboratories, including Ochoa's, followed. In 1964 Nirenberg and Philip Leder devised a more precise method that showed which transfer RNA bound to each triplet. By 1966 all 64 combinations had been assigned: 61 code for amino acids and 3 are stop signals.

In 1968 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Har Gobind Khorana, who synthesised RNAs of known sequence, and Robert Holley, who determined the structure of the first transfer RNA.

Why it matters today

The genetic code is almost universal: the same in bacteria, plants and humans. That is why human insulin can be produced in bacteria, and why one can read a variant in a report and know which amino acid changes. Nirenberg's laboratory notes, with the code table written by hand, are kept at the US National Library of Medicine.
