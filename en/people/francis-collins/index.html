People

Francis Collins

Physician-geneticist who co-discovered the genes for cystic fibrosis and neurofibromatosis and led the Human Genome Project to completion in 2003.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

  2. Hunting genes blind
  3. The Human Genome Project

Who he is

Francis Sellers Collins was born in 1950 on a farm in Virginia. He earned a PhD in physical chemistry at Yale, but, drawn to molecular biology, went on to study medicine at the University of North Carolina. During his medical training he saw patients with genetic diseases and decided to devote himself to finding their causes.

Hunting genes blind

At the University of Michigan he developed a technique, chromosome jumping, to move faster along a chromosome towards an unknown gene. With it he took part in some of the first great successes of positional cloning:

  • 1989: the cystic fibrosis gene, CFTR, with Lap-Chee Tsui and John Riordan.
  • 1990: the neurofibromatosis type 1 gene, NF1.
  • 1993: he was part of the consortium that found the Huntington's disease gene.

The Human Genome Project

In 1993 he succeeded James Watson as head of the NIH genome research centre. He led the international public consortium of 20 centres in six countries that sequenced the human genome. He insisted that all data be released openly within 24 hours (the Bermuda Principles, 1996).

Competition with the company Celera, led by Craig Venter, sped the project up. In June 2000 both announced a first draft with President Clinton, published in February 2001. In April 2003, fifty years after the double helix, the "essentially complete" version was declared finished.

Afterwards

He was director of the US National Institutes of Health from 2009 to 2021, under three presidents, and led the scientific response to COVID-19 there. He is also known for arguing that religious faith and science are compatible, the subject of his book The Language of God (2006), and for supporting a US law banning genetic discrimination in health insurance and employment (GINA, 2008).

Why it matters today

The reference sequence of the genome underpins every genetic test today: each variant in a report is described by comparison with it. And the open data policy he championed shaped how genomics works.

See also: The human genome, Lap-Chee Tsui and Craig Venter.
