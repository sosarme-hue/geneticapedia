James Watson

Co-discoverer of the double helix in 1953, first director of the Human Genome Project and a controversial figure because of his racist statements.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

  3. After 1953
  4. The fall
  5. Why it matters today
  6. Sources

Who he was

James Dewey Watson was born in Chicago in 1928. A prodigy, he entered the University of Chicago at 15 and earned his PhD at Indiana at 22, studying viruses that infect bacteria. In 1951, after seeing one of Maurice Wilkins's X-ray images of DNA in Naples, he became obsessed with finding its structure and secured a post at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, where he met Francis Crick.

The double helix

Watson and Crick did not do experiments on DNA: they built models out of metal and cardboard pieces based on other people's data. Their first attempt, in late 1951, was a failure that Rosalind Franklin took apart in minutes. In 1953 they brought together three clues:

  • Photo 51 and the measurements of Franklin and Gosling, indicating a two-stranded helix with the phosphates on the outside.
  • Chargaff's rules (A = T and G = C).
  • The correct shape of the bases, pointed out by the chemist Jerry Donohue.

Cutting out cardboard pieces, Watson saw that A with T and G with C formed pairs of the same size. The model was published in Nature on 25 April 1953. Its last sentence became famous: the pairing "immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material".

After 1953

He became a professor at Harvard, where he studied messenger RNA and wrote Molecular Biology of the Gene (1965), a highly influential textbook. In 1968 he took charge of Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory and made it a world centre for cancer and genetics research. That same year he published The Double Helix, a vivid and controversial account of the discovery that included a disparaging portrait of Franklin. From 1988 to 1992 he was the first director of the Human Genome Project at the US National Institutes of Health, and pushed for part of the budget to go to its ethical, legal and social implications.

The fall

In 2007 he made racist statements about the intelligence of Africans, with no scientific basis, which cost him his post at Cold Spring Harbor. In 2019 he repeated them and the laboratory stripped him of all his honorary titles. Current genetics rejects those ideas: human genetic variation is not organised into separate "races", and there is no evidence of genetic differences in intelligence between populations. He died in November 2025, aged 97.

Why it matters today

The double helix explains how DNA is copied and how it stores information. It is the basis of sequencing, PCR, genetic testing and CRISPR. Watson's story is also a reminder that a great discovery does not make anyone an authority on everything.
