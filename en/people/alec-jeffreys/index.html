Alec Jeffreys

The Leicester geneticist who in 1984 saw, by chance, that DNA can identify a person. He invented genetic fingerprinting.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

In this article

  2. The "eureka" moment
  3. The first cases
  4. Recognition
  5. Why it matters today
  6. Sources

Who he is

Alec John Jeffreys was born in Oxford in 1950. As a child he ran experiments with a chemistry set and a microscope his father gave him. He studied biochemistry at Oxford and has worked at the University of Leicester since 1977, studying how DNA varies between people and how genes evolve.

The "eureka" moment

At nine in the morning on 10 September 1984, Jeffreys was looking at an X-ray film showing the results of an experiment. He had analysed DNA from his technician and her parents, looking for minisatellites: regions of the genome where a short sequence is repeated many times, with a number of repeats that varies greatly between people.

The image showed a pattern of bands, like a barcode, different for each person but with parts shared between parents and daughter. Within minutes Jeffreys realised he had found a way to identify individuals and check family relationships. He called it DNA fingerprinting and published it in Nature in 1985.

The first cases

  • 1985, immigration. A boy of Ghanaian origin faced deportation from the UK because the authorities doubted he was his mother's son. Jeffreys's test proved the relationship and the boy was allowed to stay.
  • 1986-1988, the Narborough case. Two teenage girls had been murdered in Leicestershire. A young man had confessed to one of the crimes, but DNA showed he was not the culprit: the first time DNA cleared a suspect. After blood samples from some 5,000 local men were tested, DNA identified the real killer, Colin Pitchfork, convicted in 1988.

Recognition

He was knighted in 1994 and received the Royal Society's Copley Medal in 2014, the world's oldest scientific prize. Jeffreys has always argued that police DNA databases should be limited to convicted people and managed with strong privacy safeguards.

Why it matters today

Genetic profiles now use other, shorter repeated regions (STRs) and PCR, but the idea is Jeffreys's. It is used in criminal investigation, paternity testing and identifying victims of disasters and missing people.
