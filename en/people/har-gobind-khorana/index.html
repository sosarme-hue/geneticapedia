Har Gobind Khorana

The Indian chemist who made RNA fragments with known sequences in the laboratory to crack the genetic code, and who synthesised the first artificial gene.

Who he was

Har Gobind Khorana was born in 1922 in Raipur, a small village in the Punjab, then in British India and now in Pakistan. His father was a tax collector determined that his children should study; Khorana recalled that his family was almost the only literate one in the village. He studied chemistry in Lahore and, on a scholarship, earned his PhD at the University of Liverpool in 1948. He worked in Zurich, Cambridge and Vancouver, and later at the University of Wisconsin and MIT.

Cracking the genetic code

By the early 1960s it was known that DNA is copied into RNA and that RNA directs the making of proteins, but not how letters translate into amino acids. In 1961 Marshall Nirenberg showed that an RNA made only of the letter U produced a protein made only of phenylalanine: UUU means phenylalanine.

Khorana, an outstanding chemist, developed methods to synthesise RNA with known repeating sequences, such as UCUCUC... or UUCUUCUUC... By seeing which proteins they produced, he could assign many of the 64 triplets to their amino acid, and confirmed that the code is read three letters at a time, without overlap. By around 1966 the code was complete.

In 1968 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Nirenberg and Robert Holley, who had worked out the structure of a transfer RNA, "for their interpretation of the genetic code and its function in protein synthesis".

The first artificial gene

After the Nobel he set out to do something even harder: build a whole gene in the laboratory, letter by letter. In 1972 his team synthesised the gene for a yeast transfer RNA, 77 letters long. In 1976 they synthesised a bacterial gene that worked inside a living cell. Those methods for the chemical synthesis of DNA underpin the primers used in PCR, sequencing and today's synthetic biology.

Why it matters today

The genetic code he helped crack is almost universal: the same triplet means the same amino acid in bacteria, plants and people. That is why any gene can be read, the effect of a mutation predicted, or human proteins made in bacteria. See from gene to protein.
