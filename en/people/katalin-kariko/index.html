Katalin Karikó

The Hungarian biochemist who spent decades, with almost no funding, turning messenger RNA into a medicine. Her discovery with Drew Weissman made the COVID-19 RNA vaccines possible.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

In this article

  1. Who she is
  2. Years of rejection
  3. The discovery with Weissman
  4. COVID-19 vaccines
  5. Why it matters today
  6. Sources

Who she is

Katalin Karikó was born in 1955 in Szolnok, Hungary, and grew up in Kisújszállás in a house without running water, the daughter of a butcher. She earned her doctorate at the University of Szeged and worked at the Biological Research Centre there. In 1985, when her lab ran out of funds, she emigrated to the United States with her husband and two-year-old daughter, with a little over a thousand dollars hidden in the girl's teddy bear.

Years of rejection

At the University of Pennsylvania she pursued one idea: using messenger RNA as a medicine, so that a patient's own cells would make a therapeutic protein. Few believed in it. Her grant applications were rejected again and again, and in 1995 she was demoted. The big problem was that injected RNA caused strong inflammation and was quickly destroyed.

The discovery with Weissman

In 1997, at a photocopier, she met immunologist Drew Weissman, who was looking for an HIV vaccine. Together they discovered that the immune system recognised laboratory-made RNA as an intruder, but not the cell's own RNA, which carries slightly modified letters. In 2005 they reported that replacing uridine with a modified version, pseudouridine, made the RNA stop causing inflammation and produce much more protein.

The paper went almost unnoticed. In 2013 Karikó moved to the German company BioNTech.

COVID-19 vaccines

When the pandemic came, this technology, together with lipid nanoparticles to protect the RNA, allowed the Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna RNA vaccines to be designed within days. They were approved in December 2020, less than a year after the virus was identified, and billions of doses were given.

In 2021 she received the Princess of Asturias Award for Technical and Scientific Research, with Weissman and other vaccine scientists, and in 2023 the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Weissman. Her daughter, Susan Francia, won two Olympic gold medals in rowing for the United States.

Why it matters today

Messenger RNA is being tested in vaccines against flu, cytomegalovirus and cancer, and as a way to deliver gene-editing tools or missing proteins to cells in some rare diseases. See from gene to protein.

See also: Gene therapy and Severo Ochoa.
