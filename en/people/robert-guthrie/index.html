Robert Guthrie

The microbiologist and paediatrician who invented the heel-prick test. Thanks to his drop of blood on paper, millions of babies with phenylketonuria have grown up healthy.

Who he was

Robert Guthrie was born in 1916 in Marionville, Missouri. He earned a PhD in microbiology at the University of Minnesota and later studied medicine. He worked as a cancer researcher in Buffalo, New York. He had personal reasons to care about intellectual disability: his son John had one, and a niece had phenylketonuria.

The problem

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is a recessive inborn error of metabolism. The body cannot break down the amino acid phenylalanine, which builds up and damages the developing brain. In the 1950s the German paediatrician Horst Bickel had shown that a low-phenylalanine diet prevented the damage, but only if started in the first weeks of life, before symptoms appeared. A way was needed to detect the disease in every newborn.

The test

In 1961 Guthrie devised a simple, cheap test based on bacteria. A few drops of blood from the baby's heel are collected on filter paper and left to dry. A disc of this paper is placed on a plate with Bacillus subtilis bacteria and a substance that stops them growing. If the blood contains a lot of phenylalanine, it cancels the brake and the bacteria grow around the disc: the bigger the halo, the more phenylalanine.

The dried blood card, known ever since as the Guthrie card, could be posted to a central laboratory. In 1962 and 1963 more than 400,000 babies were tested in 29 US states, and the results were published in Pediatrics. Guthrie waived his patent so the test would stay cheap, and campaigned for decades for universal screening.

Why it matters today

The Guthrie card is the basis of the heel-prick test offered to every newborn in Spain and most of the world. Today bacteria are no longer used; tandem mass spectrometry and genetic tests detect dozens of conditions from the same drop, such as congenital hypothyroidism, cystic fibrosis or spinal muscular atrophy.

See also: Newborn and prenatal screening, Phenylketonuria and Archibald Garrod.
