Severo Ochoa

The Asturian biochemist who first synthesised RNA in the laboratory. His enzyme made the artificial messages with which the genetic code was cracked.

Who he was

Severo Ochoa de Albornoz was born in Luarca, Asturias, in 1905. He studied medicine in Madrid, where he lived at the Residencia de Estudiantes and trained with the physiologist Juan Negrín. He admired Santiago Ramón y Cajal, whose books led him to research. He continued his training in Berlin and Heidelberg with Otto Meyerhof, Nobel laureate of 1922, and in Oxford.

The Spanish Civil War forced him to leave Spain in 1936. After spells in Germany, England and Washington University in St Louis, with Carl and Gerty Cori, he settled in 1942 at New York University, where he would work for almost forty years. He became a US citizen in 1956.

The discovery

Ochoa was first a leading specialist in energy metabolism: how cells obtain energy from food. In 1955, studying this process in bacteria, he and the French researcher Marianne Grunberg-Manago found an enzyme that could link nucleotides into RNA chains in a test tube. They called it polynucleotide phosphorylase.

It was the first time a nucleic acid had been synthesised outside a cell. In 1959 he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, shared with Arthur Kornberg, who had done the same for DNA.

The genetic code

It later turned out that polynucleotide phosphorylase is not the enzyme that copies DNA into RNA inside the cell. But it had a huge advantage: it could make artificial RNAs with a chosen composition. In 1961 Nirenberg and Matthaei used an RNA made only of uracil (poly-U), produced with this enzyme, to discover the first "word" of the code. Ochoa's laboratory then joined the race to decipher the rest, contributing many codon assignments and showing that the code is read in a particular direction.

Return to Spain

Ochoa trained many Spanish scientists, including Margarita Salas and Eladio Viñuela, and supported the creation of the Centro de Biología Molecular in Madrid, which now bears his name. He returned to Spain in 1985 and died in Madrid in 1993. Together with Ramón y Cajal, he is one of the two Spaniards to have won the Nobel Prize in Medicine.

Why it matters today

Making RNA in the laboratory is the basis of current technologies such as mRNA vaccines and RNA therapies.
