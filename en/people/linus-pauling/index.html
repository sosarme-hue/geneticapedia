Linus Pauling

The chemist who showed in 1949 that sickle cell anaemia is caused by an abnormal haemoglobin. He invented the concept of "molecular disease".

Who he was

Linus Carl Pauling was born in Portland, Oregon, in 1901. He was one of the most influential chemists of the 20th century: he explained the nature of the chemical bond using quantum mechanics and wrote the standard book on the subject. He worked most of his life at the California Institute of Technology (Caltech). He is the only person to have received two unshared Nobel Prizes: Chemistry in 1954 and Peace in 1962, for his campaign against atmospheric nuclear tests.

Molecular disease

In 1945, on a train, the haematologist William Castle told him that the red blood cells of people with sickle cell anaemia became sickle-shaped when short of oxygen. Pauling suspected the problem lay in haemoglobin itself.

With Harvey Itano, Seymour Singer and Ibert Wells, he subjected haemoglobin from healthy and affected people to electrophoresis, a technique that separates proteins by electrical charge. Haemoglobin from patients moved differently. Healthy carriers had both forms at once, exactly as Mendel's laws predicted for a recessive trait.

In 1949 they published "Sickle cell anemia, a molecular disease" in Science. It was the first time an inherited disease had been linked to a specific change in a protein. In 1956-1957 Vernon Ingram showed the difference was a single amino acid: valine instead of glutamic acid in the beta chain.

The race for the double helix

Pauling also described the alpha helix of proteins in 1951. In early 1953 he proposed a DNA structure with three strands and the phosphates on the inside, which was chemically impossible. Watson and Crick, who feared being beaten to it, published their correct model weeks later. Pauling had not seen Rosalind Franklin's diffraction photographs; the US government had withdrawn his passport over his political views, and he could not travel to London in 1952.

Light and shade

In later years he advocated very high doses of vitamin C against colds and cancer, claims that later clinical trials did not confirm. It is a useful reminder: prestige in one field is no substitute for evidence in another.

Why it matters today

The idea of molecular disease is the basis of genomic medicine: knowing the altered protein makes it possible to design treatments. In 2023 sickle cell disease became the first disease with a CRISPR-based treatment approved by medicines regulators.
