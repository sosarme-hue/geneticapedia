Svante Pääbo

The founder of palaeogenomics. He sequenced the Neanderthal genome, discovered the Denisovans and showed that we carry their DNA.

Who he is

Svante Pääbo was born in Stockholm in 1955. He is the son of the biochemist Sune Bergström, Nobel laureate in Medicine in 1982, although he was raised by his mother, the Estonian chemist Karin Pääbo. He studied Egyptology before medicine and earned his doctorate at Uppsala University. Since 1997 he has led the genetics department of the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig, Germany.

DNA from mummies and fossils

In 1985, almost in secret, he managed to extract DNA from an Egyptian mummy. He showed that DNA can survive thousands of years, although heavily degraded and mixed with DNA from bacteria and from whoever handles the remains. Pääbo spent years developing methods to avoid this contamination, with clean rooms and rigorous controls.

In 1997 his team sequenced a fragment of mitochondrial DNA from a Neanderthal, from the original fossil from the Neander valley in Germany. It differed from that of present-day humans: the two lineages had split hundreds of thousands of years earlier.

The Neanderthal genome

With the arrival of high-throughput sequencing he set out to do what seemed impossible: read the complete genome of a Neanderthal. The draft was published in Science in 2010. The big surprise was that people of non-African descent carry 1-2 % Neanderthal DNA: our ancestors interbred with them after leaving Africa.

The Denisovans

That same year, DNA from a finger bone found in Denisova Cave in Siberia revealed an unknown human group: the Denisovans. It was the first time a human population had been discovered from its DNA alone. Populations in Melanesia and Australia retain up to 4-6 % Denisovan DNA.

Recognition

He received the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, unshared, "for his discoveries concerning the genomes of extinct hominins and human evolution".

Why it matters today

DNA inherited from these relatives influences our health. A Denisovan variant of the EPAS1 gene helps Tibetans live at high altitude. A region on chromosome 3 inherited from Neanderthals was associated with a higher risk of severe COVID-19, while another on chromosome 12 appears protective. And techniques for reading highly degraded DNA are also used in forensics and the study of past epidemics.
