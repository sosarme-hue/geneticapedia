Rare diseases

What is a rare disease?

Each one affects very few people, but together they add up to millions. Most are genetic, start in childhood and take years to diagnose.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

The paradox of rarity

In the European Union a disease is considered rare when it affects fewer than 5 people in 10,000. In the United States the threshold is fewer than 200,000 people nationwide.

Each one, on its own, is rare. But there are so many that, taken together, they are common: a 2020 study based on the Orphanet database estimated that they affect between 3.5 and 5.9 % of the world's population, or between 263 and 446 million people. In Spain about 3 million people are thought to live with a rare disease.

Many of the conditions on this site are rare: cystic fibrosis, spinal muscular atrophy, Huntington's disease, Duchenne muscular dystrophy and phenylketonuria. Others, by contrast, are fairly common, such as familial hypercholesterolaemia or haemochromatosis.

Common features

Although they are very different from one another, rare diseases share some features:

  • Genetic origin in about 70–80 % of cases. The rest may be infectious, autoimmune, toxic or of unknown cause.
  • Onset in childhood in about two out of three.
  • They are often chronic, serious and progressive, and many affect several organs.
  • Few treatments: only about 5 % have an approved treatment.
  • Little knowledge: many doctors will never see a case, and there is little research.

The diagnostic odyssey

One of the biggest problems is delay in diagnosis. Many families spend years going from one appointment to another, with repeated tests and wrong diagnoses, before they know what they have. Studies by the Spanish Federation of Rare Diseases (FEDER) suggest the average delay is more than four years, and in many cases over ten. This is called the diagnostic odyssey.

Exome and genome sequencing has changed this: it now diagnoses between 25 and 50 % of children with a suspected rare genetic disease. Even so, many people remain undiagnosed, and there are programmes specifically for them.

Orphan medicines

Because each disease affects few people, companies find it unprofitable to research treatments. To compensate, the European Union approved an orphan medicines regulation in 2000 that offers incentives such as ten years of market exclusivity. More than 200 have been authorised since. Some have transformed patients' lives, such as treatments for spinal muscular atrophy or cystic fibrosis, but their price is often very high.

Rare Disease Day

Since 2008, the last day of February (the 29th in leap years, the "rarest" day of the calendar) has been Rare Disease Day, promoted by the European patient organisation EURORDIS.
