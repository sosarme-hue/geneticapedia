Genetics and cancer

Li-Fraumeni syndrome

A very high inherited predisposition to several cancers from childhood, caused by variants in TP53, the so-called "guardian of the genome". A yearly whole-body MRI helps catch them early.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 20 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance
Gene TP53, chromosome 17
Inheritance Autosomal dominant; about 7–20 % of cases are new

What it is

Li-Fraumeni syndrome is one of the strongest inherited cancer predispositions known. It was described in 1969 by two physicians at the US National Cancer Institute, Frederick Li and Joseph Fraumeni, who studied four families in which several children had had sarcomas and their mothers or aunts had had breast cancer at very young ages.

People with Li-Fraumeni syndrome have a lifetime risk of developing some cancer of about 70 % in men and 90 % in women, and half of these cancers appear before age 30. Many people have more than one tumour during their lives.

The guardian of the genome

In 1990 David Malkin and colleagues discovered that the cause was variants in the TP53 gene. This gene makes the p53 protein, which has been called the "guardian of the genome": when it detects DNA damage, it stops the cell dividing so the damage can be repaired, and if the damage is too severe it orders the cell to self-destruct.

TP53 is the most frequently altered gene in cancer overall: it carries somatic variants in about half of all human tumours. In Li-Fraumeni syndrome, the person already inherits an altered copy in all their cells, and a "second hit" is enough for a cell to lose this control.

Interestingly, elephants have about 20 copies of the TP53 gene, and this is thought to explain why, despite being huge, long-lived animals, they rarely get cancer.

The most common cancers

The syndrome's "core" cancers are:

  • Breast cancer in young women, often before 35.
  • Sarcomas of soft tissue and bone (osteosarcoma).
  • Brain tumours, such as gliomas or choroid plexus tumours.
  • Adrenocortical carcinoma, especially in young children.

Leukaemias and lung, bowel and other cancers are also more common. In southern Brazil a particular variant, R337H, is very common in the population and mainly causes childhood adrenocortical carcinoma, with a somewhat lower risk than the classic variants.

Diagnosis

It is suspected using the Chompret criteria, which include, for example:

  • A person with a spectrum tumour before 46 and a close relative with another spectrum tumour before 56.
  • A person with several primary tumours.
  • Anyone with adrenocortical carcinoma, a choroid plexus tumour or a certain type of rhabdomyosarcoma, regardless of family history.
  • A woman with breast cancer before 31.

Confirmation is by genetic testing of TP53. Variants in TP53 are now also found when panels are done for other reasons, and not all carry the same risk, so the result should be interpreted in a genetics clinic.

An important point: with age, some blood cells acquire TP53 variants without the person having the syndrome (clonal haematopoiesis). So a positive blood result sometimes needs to be confirmed in another tissue.

Surveillance: the Toronto protocol

For years it was thought there was nothing to do but wait. But a team in Toronto, led by David Malkin, proposed intensive surveillance that includes:

  • A yearly whole-body MRI, with no radiation, from childhood.
  • A yearly brain MRI.
  • In women, a yearly breast MRI from age 20.
  • Abdominal ultrasound every few months in children, to detect adrenocortical carcinoma.
  • Regular colonoscopies from young adulthood.
  • Regular physical examinations and blood tests.

Studies have shown that this surveillance detects many tumours early and improves survival. International guidelines have recommended it since 2017.

Other recommendations: avoid radiation wherever possible (X-rays, CT scans and radiotherapy), because cells without working p53 are very sensitive to its effects, and for women consider preventive mastectomy.

The emotional burden

Living with Li-Fraumeni syndrome is a heavy psychological load: repeated cancers in the family, constant tests and difficult decisions about having children. Many families turn to preimplantation testing. Psychological support and contact with other families are an essential part of care.
