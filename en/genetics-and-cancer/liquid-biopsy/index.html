Genetics and cancer

Liquid biopsy

A blood test that looks for fragments of tumour DNA. It is already used to choose treatments and watch for relapse, and is being studied as a way to detect cancer before it causes symptoms.

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance
What it is Analysing tumour DNA circulating in the blood (or other fluids)
Used today for Finding treatable variants, monitoring response, detecting resistance and relapse
Under research Early detection of several cancers with one test
Main limitation If there is very little tumour DNA, the test may miss it

What it is

To find out which variants a tumour carries, a piece traditionally has to be taken with a biopsy: a needle, an endoscopy or an operation. A liquid biopsy looks for the same information in a simple blood test.

It works because cells, when they die, release small fragments of their DNA into the blood, about 160 letters long. This is cell-free DNA. In a person with cancer, part of this DNA comes from the tumour: circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA). Sometimes it is more than 10 % of the total; at other times less than 0.1 %.

The idea is not new. In 1948 two French researchers, Paul Mandel and Pierre Métais, described DNA outside cells in the blood. And in 1997 Dennis Lo discovered in Hong Kong fetal DNA in a mother's blood, the basis of non-invasive prenatal screening. The technology to find a few copies of tumour DNA among millions of normal copies arrived with digital PCR and high-throughput sequencing.

What it is used for today

Choosing treatment

In advanced lung cancer it is often hard to get enough tissue. A blood test can find variants in EGFR, ALK, KRAS and other genes that have targeted drugs. Guidelines accept it when there is no tissue or to save time. The same applies to PIK3CA or ESR1 in breast cancer.

Detecting resistance

A tumour that responds to a drug may develop resistance months later. For example, in lung cancer with EGFR variants, a new variant called T790M often appears. Detecting it in the blood allows a switch to another drug without a new biopsy.

Minimal residual disease

After a tumour is removed, tumour DNA usually disappears from the blood. If it remains or returns, it shows that cancer cells are still there, often months before they can be seen on a scan. A bowel cancer trial, DYNAMIC (2022), showed that guiding chemotherapy after surgery by ctDNA allowed many patients to avoid it without worse outcomes.

The big challenge: detecting cancer earlier

The most ambitious goal is a blood test that detects several cancers at once in people without symptoms. These tests, called MCED (multi-cancer early detection), look not only for variants but also for DNA methylation patterns, which reveal which tissue a fragment comes from.

The results are promising but not yet enough:

  • They detect advanced cancers well but early cancers less well, and those are exactly the ones we most want to find.
  • A positive result leads to further tests, and some will be false positives.
  • It has not yet been shown that they reduce deaths. In the UK, a large trial with about 140,000 NHS participants is studying this.

For now they do not replace established screening for breast, bowel and cervical cancer.

Limitations

  • If the tumour releases little DNA (some brain or kidney tumours, or very small ones), the test may come back negative even though cancer is present.
  • With age, blood cells build up their own variants (clonal haematopoiesis), which can be confused with tumour variants.
  • Sometimes it finds a variant that is actually inherited, which means genetic counselling must be offered.

Beyond blood

Researchers also look for whole tumour cells in the blood, and for tumour DNA in urine (for bladder cancer), cerebrospinal fluid (for brain tumours) or saliva.
