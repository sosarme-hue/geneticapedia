Genetics and cancer

Precision oncology

Treating cancer according to the genetic changes in each tumour. From imatinib to PARP inhibitors and CAR-T cells, targeted drugs have changed the outlook for many cancers.

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional or genetic counselling.

At a glance
Idea Analyse the tumour and choose the treatment that hits its weak spot
First big success Imatinib for chronic myeloid leukaemia (2001)
Tools Targeted drugs, PARP inhibitors, immunotherapy, CAR-T cells
Limits Not every tumour has a target; resistance is common

From chemotherapy to targets

For decades, medical treatment of cancer relied on chemotherapy, which attacks all rapidly dividing cells, healthy or not. That is why it causes hair loss, nausea or low blood counts.

Precision oncology starts from a different idea: if we know which altered gene drives a tumour (see how a tumour begins), we can design a drug that blocks exactly that protein. To do this, tumours are now analysed with sequencing panels that test hundreds of genes at once.

The case that changed everything: imatinib

In 1960 Peter Nowell and David Hungerford observed in Philadelphia an unusually small chromosome in the cells of chronic myeloid leukaemia: the Philadelphia chromosome. In 1973 Janet Rowley discovered that it was a translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, and it was later shown to fuse two genes, BCR and ABL1, creating a permanently active protein that tells cells to divide.

In the late 1990s Brian Druker and the company Novartis developed imatinib, a pill that blocks this protein. The results, published in 2001, were spectacular: most patients went into remission. Today a person with this leukaemia who is treated in time has an almost normal life expectancy.

Examples of targeted treatments

Tumour Alteration Type of drug
Breast HER2 amplification Trastuzumab and other anti-HER2 drugs
Lung EGFR variants Osimertinib and other EGFR inhibitors
Lung ALK fusions Alectinib, lorlatinib
Melanoma BRAF V600E variant BRAF and MEK inhibitors
Bowel, lung, pancreas KRAS G12C variant Sotorasib, adagrasib
Any tumour NTRK fusions Larotrectinib, entrectinib

For forty years KRAS, the most common oncogene, was considered "undruggable". The first inhibitors arrived in 2021, thanks to the work of Kevan Shokat and others, who found a pocket in the altered protein.

PARP inhibitors: exploiting a weakness

The cancer cells of people with BRCA1 or BRCA2 variants have already lost one DNA repair pathway. If another pathway, which depends on the PARP protein, is also blocked, the cell accumulates so much damage that it dies, while healthy cells, which keep a working copy of BRCA, survive. This principle is called synthetic lethality.

PARP inhibitors such as olaparib are used in ovarian, breast, prostate and pancreatic cancers with alterations in BRCA or other genes in the same pathway. They are a good example of how a finding from hereditary cancer becomes a treatment.

Immunotherapy

Instead of attacking the tumour, immunotherapy helps the immune system recognise it. Checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, anti-CTLA-4) release the brakes the tumour uses to hide. James Allison and Tasuku Honjo received the Nobel Prize in 2018 for these discoveries. They work especially well in tumours with many variants, such as melanoma, lung cancer in smokers, or the microsatellite-unstable tumours of Lynch syndrome.

CAR-T cells

CAR-T cells are a "living drug". T lymphocytes are taken from the patient and, in the laboratory, given a gene that makes a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) able to recognise a protein on tumour cells, such as CD19 in B-cell leukaemias and lymphomas. They are then multiplied and returned to the patient.

The first approval came in 2017 for children and young people with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia that had not responded to other treatments. They are now used in lymphomas and multiple myeloma, and in Spain the Hospital Clínic de Barcelona developed its own academic CAR-T, ARI-0001, authorised in 2021 by the Spanish Medicines Agency under the hospital exemption. They can cause serious side effects, such as cytokine release syndrome, and are only given in specialist centres.

Limits and challenges

  • Only some patients have a tumour with a treatable target.
  • Resistance is common, because the tumour evolves.
  • Some drugs are very expensive, and access is unequal.
  • Tumour biopsy and sequencing should be available to every patient who could benefit.
