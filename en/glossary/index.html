Glossary

Genetics glossary

150 genetics terms explained briefly and clearly, from allele to X-linked.

ABCDEFGHIKLMNOPRSTUVX

A

Adeno-associated virus
A harmless virus used as a vector in many gene therapies. Read more.
Allele
Each of the possible versions of the same gene. A person has two alleles of each autosomal gene, one from each parent. Read more.
Amino acid
The basic building block of proteins. There are 20 different amino acids, and the order in which they are linked is dictated by the gene.
Amniocentesis
A prenatal test that takes a sample of amniotic fluid, from week 15, to study the chromosomes or DNA of the fetus. Read more.
Ancient DNA
DNA recovered from remains hundreds or thousands of years old, such as bones or teeth. It is usually highly fragmented and contaminated. Read more.
Aneuploidy
An abnormal number of chromosomes in a cell, too many or too few, as in trisomy 21 or Turner syndrome. Read more.
Anticipation
A phenomenon in which a condition appears earlier or more severely in successive generations, typical of repeat expansions such as Huntington's disease. Read more.
Antisense oligonucleotide
A short synthetic piece of RNA or DNA that binds a specific RNA to change how it is used or destroy it. Read more.
Apoptosis
Programmed cell death: an orderly process by which the body removes damaged or unneeded cells. Read more.
Autosome
Any chromosome that is not a sex chromosome. Humans have 22 pairs of autosomes.

B

Bacteriophage
A virus that infects bacteria. Studying phages gave key clues about DNA and CRISPR.
Base pair
Two bases paired on opposite strands of DNA: A with T and G with C. Used as the unit of DNA length.
Biobank
An organised collection of biological samples and data from many people for research, with their consent. Read more.

C

Carrier
A person who has one copy of a recessive variant and usually does not have the condition, but can pass it on. Read more.
Cas9
A bacterial protein that cuts DNA at the point specified by a guide RNA. It is the most widely used CRISPR tool. Read more.
Cascade testing
Step-by-step testing of the relatives of a person with a known genetic variant, from one relative to the next. Very useful in familial hypercholesterolaemia or hereditary cancer. Read more.
Cell-free DNA
Fragments of DNA floating freely in the blood. Some may come from a tumour or, in pregnancy, from the placenta. Read more.
Central dogma
An idea proposed by Francis Crick in 1958: information flows from DNA to RNA and from RNA to protein. Read more.
Centromere
The narrow region of a chromosome that joins its two arms and by which the cell pulls it during division. Read more.
Chimera
An organism made of cells from two genetically different individuals.
Chorionic villus sampling
A prenatal test that takes a small sample of the placenta, from week 11, to study the chromosomes or DNA of the fetus. Read more.
Chromatid
Each of the two identical copies of a chromosome after it has been duplicated, joined at the centromere.
Chromatin
The mix of DNA and proteins that makes up chromosomes. It can be more or less tightly packed. Read more.
Chromosome
A long DNA molecule coiled and packaged with proteins. Human cells normally have 46, in 23 pairs. Read more.
Clone
An organism or cell with the same DNA as the one it came from. Read more.
Codominance
When both variants of a gene are expressed together, as in the AB blood group. Read more.
Codon
A group of three bases in messenger RNA that corresponds to an amino acid or a stop signal. Read more.
Collagen
The body's most abundant protein, which gives strength to skin, bone, tendons and vessels. Read more.
Consanguinity
Being related through common ancestors. It increases the chance that a couple share the same recessive variant. Read more.
CRISPR
A bacterial defence system that has become a tool for editing DNA precisely. Read more.

D

De novo variant
A variant that appears for the first time in a person and is not present in either parent.
Deletion
Loss of a piece of DNA, from a single letter to part of a chromosome. Read more.
Denisovans
An extinct human group known mainly from its DNA, which left traces in the genomes of populations in Asia and Oceania. Read more.
DNA
Deoxyribonucleic acid. A double-helix molecule that stores hereditary information. It is made of four bases: A, T, G and C. Read more.
DNA fingerprint
A profile of DNA markers that distinguishes one person from almost everyone else. Read more.
DNA methylation
The addition of a methyl group to certain cytosines in DNA. It is the most studied epigenetic mark and usually switches genes off.
Dominant
Describes an allele that shows its effect even when only one copy is present. Read more.
Double helix
The shape of DNA: two strands wound around each other, joined by their bases. Read more.
Duplication
An extra copy of a piece of DNA. Read more.

E

Electrophoresis
A technique that separates DNA or protein fragments by size by moving them through a gel with an electric current. Read more.
Enzyme
A protein that speeds up a chemical reaction in the body. Read more.
Epigenetics
The study of changes in gene activity that do not alter the DNA sequence, such as methylation. Read more.
Exome
All the exons of the genome, that is, the parts of genes that code for proteins. About 1–2 % of DNA. Read more.
Exon
Part of a gene that is kept in the mature messenger RNA and is mostly translated into protein.

F

Founder effect
A high frequency of a variant in a population descended from a few ancestors who carried it. Read more.

G

Gamete
A reproductive cell, egg or sperm. It has half the chromosomes of other cells. Read more.
Gene
A stretch of DNA with the information to make a product with a function, usually a protein. Humans have about 20,000 protein-coding genes. Read more.
Gene drive
A genetic construct inherited by almost all offspring that can spread through a population. Read more.
Gene editing
A set of techniques that change the DNA sequence at a specific place, such as CRISPR-Cas9, base editing or prime editing. Read more.
Gene expression
The process by which a gene's information is used to make its product, usually a protein.
Gene panel
A test that analyses at once a selected group of genes linked to a clinical problem, such as hereditary cancer or cardiomyopathies. Read more.
Gene therapy
Treatment that adds, changes or silences genes to cure or improve a disease. Read more.
Genetic counselling
A process of information and support that helps people understand an inherited condition, decide about testing and grasp its consequences. Read more.
Genetic drift
Random change in the frequency of genetic variants from one generation to the next, stronger in small populations. Read more.
Genetic genealogy
Using consumer DNA databases to find distant relatives, including to identify suspects. Read more.
Genetic marker
A DNA variant at a known position used as a signpost to follow inheritance or identify people.
Genetically modified organism
A living thing whose DNA has been changed using genetic engineering. Read more.
Genome
All the genetic material of an organism. The human genome has about 3.1 billion base pairs per set of chromosomes. Read more.
Genomic imprinting
An epigenetic phenomenon in which some genes are expressed only if they come from the father, or only if they come from the mother. Read more.
Genotype
The combination of alleles a person has for one or more genes.
Germ cell
A cell that gives rise to eggs or sperm. Changes in it can be passed to children.
Germline
Describes a variant present in eggs or sperm and therefore in every cell of the person. It can be inherited. The opposite of somatic.
GWAS
Genome-wide association study. Compares the DNA of thousands of people to find variants linked to a trait or disease.

H

Haplogroup
A group of people sharing a common ancestor through the maternal (mitochondrial DNA) or paternal (Y chromosome) line. Read more.
Haplotype
A set of nearby variants on a chromosome that tend to be inherited together.
Hemizygous
Having only one copy of a gene, as men do for X-chromosome genes. Read more.
Heritability
The proportion of differences in a trait within a population that is explained by genetic differences. It does not apply to individuals. Read more.
Heteroplasmy
The presence, in the same cell or person, of mitochondria with normal DNA and mitochondria with altered DNA.
Heterozygous
Having two different alleles of a gene.
Histone
A protein around which DNA is wound to be packaged in the nucleus.
Homozygous
Having two identical alleles of a gene.

I

Identical twins
Twins from a single embryo that split; they share almost all their DNA. Read more.
In vitro fertilisation
Joining egg and sperm in the laboratory, then transferring the embryo to the womb. Read more.
Incidental finding
A genetic test result unrelated to the reason the test was done. Also called a secondary finding.
Incomplete penetrance
When not everyone with a disease-causing variant develops the condition. Read more.
Insertion
The addition of one or more extra letters to a DNA sequence. Read more.
Intron
Part of a gene that is copied into RNA but removed before the protein is made.
Inversion
A piece of a chromosome that has been flipped round.

K

Karyotype
An ordered picture of a person's chromosomes. It shows whether one is missing or extra, or whether there are large rearrangements. Read more.

L

Liquid biopsy
A blood test that looks for tumour DNA to diagnose or monitor cancer without surgery. Read more.
Locus
The position a gene or sequence occupies on a chromosome. Plural: loci.
Lysosome
A cell organelle full of enzymes that break down and recycle molecules. Read more.

M

Meiosis
The cell division that produces eggs and sperm with half the chromosomes, one from each pair.
Mendelian
A trait or condition that depends on a single gene and follows Mendel's laws. Read more.
Messenger RNA
An RNA copy of a gene that carries instructions to the ribosomes to make a protein. Read more.
Metastasis
The spread of a cancer from where it started to other organs. Read more.
Microarray
A test that detects losses or gains of small chromosome segments across the whole genome. Also called array-CGH or CMA.
Mitochondrial DNA
A small circular genome found in mitochondria, with 37 genes. It is inherited only from the mother. Read more.
Mitochondrion
The organelle that produces the cell's energy. It has its own DNA, inherited from the mother.
Mitosis
Division of a cell into two daughter cells with the same number of chromosomes.
Monoclonal antibody
An immune-system protein made in the laboratory to recognise a specific target, such as a tumour protein. Read more.
Mosaicism
The presence in one person of two or more cell populations with different genetic make-up. Read more.
Mutation
Any change in the DNA sequence. The neutral term "variant" is now preferred. Read more.

N

Natural selection
The process by which variants that favour survival and reproduction become more common. Read more.
Neanderthals
A human species that lived in Europe and Asia until about 40,000 years ago and interbred with our ancestors. Read more.
Newborn screening
A test performed on all newborns with a few drops of blood from the heel to detect serious, treatable conditions in time. Read more.
Next-generation sequencing
Methods that read millions of DNA fragments at once, allowing whole genomes to be studied quickly and cheaply. Read more.
Nitrogenous base
Each of the "letters" of DNA: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C). In RNA, thymine is replaced by uracil (U).
Nucleotide
The unit that makes up DNA and RNA: a sugar, a phosphate and a nitrogenous base.

O

Oncogene
A gene that, when altered, speeds up cell division and promotes cancer. Read more.

P

Palaeogenomics
The study of whole genomes of ancient or extinct organisms. Read more.
Pangenome
A genomic reference built from the genomes of many people to better represent human diversity.
Pathogenic variant
A DNA change that clearly causes or contributes to a disease.
PCR
Polymerase chain reaction. A technique that copies a DNA fragment millions of times in a short time. Read more.
Pedigree
A drawing using standard symbols that shows a family's members and who has a condition. It helps work out how it is inherited. Read more.
Penetrance
The probability that a person with a variant develops the associated condition. If not everyone does, penetrance is called incomplete.
Pharmacogenetics
The study of how genetic variants influence the response to medicines. Read more.
Phenotype
The observable or measurable characteristics of a person, resulting from their genes, the environment and their interaction.
Pleiotropy
The effect of a single gene on several different traits or organs. Read more.
Polygenic
A trait that depends on many genes, each with a small effect, such as height. Read more.
Polygenic risk score
A calculation that adds up the effect of many small-effect variants to estimate the genetic risk of a common trait or disease.
Polymorphism
A genetic variant common in the population, usually with no harmful effect.
Predictive test
A genetic test done on a healthy person to find out whether they will develop a condition or have an increased risk. Read more.
Preimplantation genetic testing
Genetic analysis of embryos created by IVF so that only those without a serious family condition are transferred. Read more.
Prenatal diagnosis
Tests to find out whether a fetus has a genetic or chromosomal condition. Read more.
Protein
A molecule made of a chain of amino acids that carries out almost all of the cell's work. Read more.

R

Rare disease
In Europe, one affecting fewer than 5 in 10,000 people. About three in four have a genetic cause. Read more.
Recessive
Describes an allele that shows its effect only when there are two copies, or a single copy in X-linked genes in a male. Read more.
Recombinant DNA
DNA made in the laboratory by joining fragments from different sources, often from different species. Read more.
Recombination
The exchange of segments between matching chromosomes during meiosis, which shuffles parental genes. Read more.
Repeat expansion
An abnormal increase in the number of times a short DNA sequence, such as the CAG triplet, is repeated. It causes conditions such as Huntington's disease or fragile X syndrome. Read more.
Replication
Copying of DNA before a cell divides. Read more.
Residual risk
The probability that remains of having or passing on a condition after a negative result, because no test detects every variant.
Restriction enzyme
A bacterial enzyme that cuts DNA at a specific sequence. It was key to genetic engineering.
Ribosome
The cell structure that reads messenger RNA and makes proteins.
RNA
Ribonucleic acid. A molecule similar to DNA, usually single-stranded and with uracil (U) instead of thymine. Messenger RNA carries a copy of a gene to the ribosomes.

S

Sensitivity
A test's ability to give a positive result in people who do have the condition. Read more.
Sequencing
Reading the order of bases in a DNA fragment or a whole genome. Read more.
Sex chromosomes
The X and Y pair. Most women have XX and most men XY.
SNP
Single-nucleotide polymorphism. A single-letter variant common in the population. Pronounced "snip".
Somatic
Describes a variant that arises during life in some of the body's cells and is not inherited. It is the basis of most cancers.
Specificity
A test's ability to give a negative result in people who do not have the condition. Read more.
Stem cell
A cell able to divide and to turn into other cell types.
STR
Short tandem repeats: sequences of a few letters repeated a number of times that varies between people. The basis of forensic profiles. Read more.
Syndrome
A set of signs and symptoms that occur together and characterise a condition.

T

Targeted therapy
A cancer treatment designed to act on a specific genetic change in the tumour. Read more.
Telomere
The end of a chromosome, which protects it and shortens with each cell division.
Transcription
Copying a gene from DNA into an RNA molecule.
Transgenic
An organism that has been given a gene from another species. Read more.
Translation
Making a protein in the ribosome from the information in messenger RNA.
Translocation
An exchange of segments between two chromosomes. If no material is lost or gained it is called balanced.
Triplet
A group of three DNA or RNA letters. A codon is a triplet that corresponds to an amino acid. Read more.
Trisomy
Three copies of a chromosome instead of two, such as trisomy 21 in Down syndrome. Read more.
Tumour suppressor gene
A gene that restrains cell division or repairs damage. Losing both copies promotes cancer. Read more.

U

Uniparental disomy
When both copies of a chromosome come from the same parent. Read more.

V

Variable expressivity
Differences in severity or symptoms of the same genetic condition among people with the same variant. Read more.
Variant of uncertain significance
A DNA change that is not yet known to cause disease or not. Medical decisions should not be based on it. Abbreviation: VUS. Read more.
Vector
A vehicle, usually a modified virus, used to deliver genetic material into cells in gene therapy.

X

X inactivation
The random switching-off of one of the two X chromosomes in each cell of females. Read more.
X-linked
Inheritance of genes located on the X chromosome. X-linked recessive conditions mainly affect males. Read more.
