Everyday traits

Blood groups

The A, B, AB and O groups and the Rh factor are among the clearest examples of Mendelian inheritance. How they are inherited, why they matter for transfusions and pregnancy, and what to make of blood-type diets.

At a glance
ABO system One gene on chromosome 9 with three variants: A, B and O
Rh factor Depends mainly on the RHD gene, on chromosome 1
In Spain O+ and A+ are the most common, each about a third of the population
Discoverer Karl Landsteiner, in 1901 (Nobel Prize 1930)
Universal donor O negative, for red cells

A discovery that made transfusions safe

Until the early twentieth century, blood transfusions often killed the patient. In 1901, Austrian physician Karl Landsteiner mixed blood from different people and saw that the red cells sometimes clumped together. That is how he discovered blood groups. He received the Nobel Prize in 1930, and 14 June, his birthday, is World Blood Donor Day.

The ABO system

Red blood cells carry sugars on their surface that act as labels. A single gene, ABO, decides which:

  • The A variant adds the A label.
  • The B variant adds the B label.
  • The O variant does not work and adds none.

A and B are codominant: if you inherit both, you have both labels (group AB). O is recessive: you are group O only if you inherit two copies.

Your two copies Your group
A/A or A/O A
B/B or B/O B
A/B AB
O/O O

So two group A parents can have a group O child if both are A/O, and two group O parents can only have O children. It is a favourite example for explaining Mendel's laws, although rare variants such as the Bombay phenotype break these rules.

The body makes antibodies against the labels it lacks. A group A person has antibodies against B, and if given group B blood, those antibodies destroy it. Group O people have no labels and their blood can be given to anyone; AB people can receive from anyone.

The Rh factor

The second most important system is Rh, which depends mainly on having the D protein, encoded by the RHD gene. People who have it are Rh positive; people who lack it because both copies are non-functional, usually because the gene is missing, are Rh negative. In Spain, about 15 % of people are Rh negative.

Rh matters in pregnancy: if an Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive baby, she may make antibodies against the baby's blood, especially in a second pregnancy. This caused haemolytic disease of the newborn. Today it is prevented by giving the mother anti-D immunoglobulin during pregnancy and after birth. Many hospitals now test fetal DNA in the mother's blood to find out the baby's Rh group, as in prenatal diagnosis.

Myths

  • Blood-type diets have no scientific basis: a 2014 study of more than 1,400 people found that any benefits did not depend on blood group.
  • Personality by blood group, very popular in Japan, has no support either.
  • There are small differences in risk: group O people have slightly lower risk of thrombosis and slightly higher risk of stomach ulcers, but the effect is small.
