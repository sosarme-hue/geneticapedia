Human evolution

Atapuerca and the ancient DNA of Iberia

From the Sima de los Huesos, with the oldest known human DNA, to the migrations that shaped the present-day population of Spain. What genetics has revealed about Iberian history.

The Sima de los Huesos

In the Atapuerca hills (Burgos, Spain), a World Heritage Site, lies the Sima de los Huesos ("Pit of the Bones"), a shaft at the back of a cave where remains of at least 28 individuals from about 430,000 years ago have been recovered. It is the largest collection of human fossils from that period in the world.

In 2013 the team of Matthias Meyer and Svante Pääbo, working with Juan Luis Arsuaga, recovered mitochondrial DNA from a thigh bone. The result surprised everyone: it was more similar to that of the Denisovans of Siberia than to Neanderthals, even though the fossils had Neanderthal features.

In 2016 the same team sequenced nuclear DNA, the oldest human DNA then known. It confirmed that the people of the Sima were early ancestors of the Neanderthals. The most accepted explanation for the contradiction is that Neanderthal mitochondrial DNA was later replaced by mitochondrial DNA from a population related to modern humans.

Homo antecessor

At the Gran Dolina site, also at Atapuerca, remains of a human species from about 800,000 years ago were discovered in 1994 and named Homo antecessor. They are the oldest human remains in western Europe with enough information to define a species.

DNA does not survive that long, but tooth enamel proteins do. In 2020 a study analysed the proteins of a tooth and showed that Homo antecessor was a sister lineage very close to the common ancestor of Neanderthals, Denisovans and modern humans.

The genetic history of the Iberian Peninsula

Analysis of hundreds of ancient genomes has made it possible to reconstruct how the population of the peninsula was formed:

  1. Hunter-gatherers who arrived more than 40,000 years ago, with several waves after the last Ice Age.
  2. Neolithic farmers from Anatolia, who arrived about 7,500 years ago along the Mediterranean coast, brought farming and mixed with the hunters.
  3. Herders from the steppes of eastern Europe, whose ancestry reached Iberia about 4,500 years ago, in the Bronze Age. A 2019 study found that within a few centuries their Y-chromosome lineages almost completely replaced the earlier ones, while women's DNA changed much less.
  4. Later contributions from the eastern Mediterranean and North Africa, in Roman and Islamic times, seen mainly in the south and east.

The Basques, long considered a genetically isolated and very ancient people, turn out to have the same Bronze Age mixture as everyone else, but with fewer later contributions.

Why it matters

Atapuerca and ancient DNA studies show that human history is a history of movement and mixing. No population today is "pure" or has always lived in the same place. See why biological races do not exist.
