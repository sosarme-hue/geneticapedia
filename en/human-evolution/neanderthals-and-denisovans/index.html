Human evolution

Neanderthals and Denisovans

Our ancestors interbred with other human species, and we carry their DNA. Some of these legacies help us; others make us more vulnerable.

At a glance
Neanderthals Lived in Europe and western Asia until about 40,000 years ago
Denisovans Known mainly from their DNA, found in Denisova Cave (Siberia)
Neanderthal DNA in us About 1–2 % in non-African people
Denisovan DNA Up to 4–6 % in populations of Papua New Guinea and Australia
Pioneer Svante Pääbo, Nobel Prize 2022

Reading the DNA of the dead

For a long time it was thought impossible to recover DNA from fossils tens of thousands of years old: DNA breaks down, degrades and is easily contaminated by that of the people handling the bones. Svante Pääbo devoted his career to overcoming these problems with clean rooms, techniques to detect the typical damage of ancient DNA and high-throughput sequencing methods.

In 2010 his team at the Max Planck Institute in Leipzig published the first Neanderthal genome. The big surprise: people outside Africa carry about 2 % Neanderthal DNA. Our ancestors had interbred with them, probably in the Near East, about 50,000–60,000 years ago.

A species discovered by its DNA

That same year Pääbo's team analysed a small finger bone found in Denisova Cave, in the Altai Mountains of Siberia. Its DNA was neither modern human nor Neanderthal: it belonged to an unknown human group, which they called Denisovans. It was the first time a human species had been discovered from its DNA rather than its fossils.

We now know that Denisovans spread across Asia, and that populations in Melanesia, Australia and parts of Southeast Asia carry up to 4–6 % of their DNA. In 2018, in the same cave, a bone turned up from a girl, nicknamed Denny, whose mother was a Neanderthal and whose father was a Denisovan: a first-generation child of the two groups.

Useful legacies

Some genes inherited from these species helped our ancestors adapt to new environments:

  • People in Tibet carry a variant of the EPAS1 gene, inherited from Denisovans, which lets them live at high altitude without producing too many red blood cells.
  • Several Neanderthal-derived genes of the immune system and of skin and hair may have helped people cope with new pathogens and cold climates.

Problematic legacies

Other variants raise the risk of some diseases:

  • In 2020 Hugo Zeberg and Svante Pääbo showed that a stretch of chromosome 3 inherited from Neanderthals is linked to a higher risk of severe COVID-19. About 16 % of Europeans and half of people in South Asia carry it.
  • Other Neanderthal variants are associated with depression, nicotine addiction or blood clotting, although their effects are usually small.

Why they disappeared

Neanderthals died out about 40,000 years ago. There is no single explanation: climate change, competition with modern humans, small and isolated populations, and probably absorption through interbreeding. In a sense they did not disappear completely: they live on in our DNA.
