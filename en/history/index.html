History of genetics

A timeline of genetics, from ancient plant and animal breeding to CRISPR and the human pangenome.

  1. c. 10,000 BC

    Domestication

    By selecting the most useful plants and animals, the first farmers and herders changed their genes without knowing it.

    Read more

    In the Fertile Crescent, Mesoamerica, China and the Andes, generations of farmers kept the seeds of plants that did not shed their grain or that bore larger fruit. Teosinte, a grass with hard kernels, became maize; the wolf gave rise to the dog. We now know that a few genes of large effect lie behind these changes, such as tb1 in maize. It was the first "genetic engineering", done blindly over thousands of years.

  2. 1859

    Darwin and variation

    In On the Origin of Species, Charles Darwin explains evolution by natural selection, but lacks a theory of heredity.

    Read more

    Darwin knew that offspring resemble their parents and that small variations exist, the raw material of selection. But like almost everyone, he believed in "blending inheritance", in which parental traits mix like paints. That was a problem: an advantageous variation would be diluted away within a few generations. His own hypothesis, pangenesis (1868), turned out to be wrong. The solution had already been published in a Brno journal he never read.

  3. 1865-1866

    Mendel and the peas

    Gregor Mendel describes the rules of heredity. His work goes almost unnoticed for 34 years.

    Read more

    After eight years of crosses involving some 28,000 pea plants, Mendel showed that traits are inherited as whole units ("factors") that come in pairs and separate when sex cells form, with predictable ratios such as 3:1. He was the first to use statistics in biology. His 1866 paper was rarely cited, partly because its mathematical approach was alien to the botanists of his day. Mendel's profile.

  4. 1869

    DNA is discovered

    Friedrich Miescher isolates from white blood cell nuclei a substance he calls "nuclein".

    Read more

    In Tübingen, the young Swiss physician washed pus-soaked bandages from a nearby hospital to obtain white blood cells, and from their nuclei extracted a phosphorus-rich substance that resisted protein-digesting enzymes. It was DNA. Albrecht Kossel later identified its four bases (Nobel Prize 1910), and Phoebus Levene described how sugar, phosphate and base are linked. But for decades it was thought too simple a molecule to carry information. Miescher's profile.

  5. 1900

    Mendel rediscovered

    De Vries, Correns and Tschermak independently reach similar results and rescue Mendel.

    Read more

    The three botanists found Mendelian ratios in their own crosses and, when searching the literature, came across the 1866 paper. Britain's William Bateson became Mendel's great champion and translated his work into English. Within a few years the same rules were shown to apply to hens, mice and people. The century of genetics had begun.

  6. 1902

    Garrod and inborn errors

    Archibald Garrod describes alkaptonuria as the first human trait to follow Mendel's laws.

    Read more

    Garrod, a London physician, noticed that alkaptonuria (urine that darkens on exposure to air) was more common in children of first cousins and behaved like a recessive trait. In 1908 he coined the phrase inborn errors of metabolism: a faulty gene leads to a missing enzyme, and a substance builds up. It was the first idea that genes direct the body's chemistry, forty years before it was accepted. Garrod's profile.

  7. 1905-1909

    Genetics, gene, genotype

    Bateson proposes the word "genetics" and Johannsen the words "gene", "genotype" and "phenotype".

    Read more

    Naming things helped people think about them. Wilhelm Johannsen, working with beans, distinguished between what is inherited (the genotype) and what is seen (the phenotype), which also depends on the environment. He chose "gene" as a neutral word, uncommitted about its physical nature, which nobody yet knew.

  8. 1910-1915

    Morgan's flies

    Thomas Hunt Morgan shows that genes lie on chromosomes.

    Read more

    In the "fly room" at Columbia University, Morgan found in 1910 a white-eyed male among thousands of red-eyed flies. The trait was inherited in a sex-linked way, which could only be explained if the gene were on the X chromosome. His student Alfred Sturtevant, aged 19, used how often two traits separated to draw the first genetic map in 1913. Morgan received the Nobel Prize in 1933. Morgan's profile.

  9. 1900-1945

    The shadow of eugenics

    Misreading genetics, many countries adopt forced sterilisation policies.

    Read more

    Francis Galton had coined "eugenics" in 1883. In the early twentieth century the idea of "improving the race" by preventing people deemed "unfit" from reproducing spread widely. More than 60,000 people were forcibly sterilised in the United States, and thousands in Sweden, Switzerland and Canada. Nazi Germany sterilised some 400,000 people and murdered tens of thousands of disabled people before the Holocaust. It remains a permanent warning: misused genetics can be used to justify crimes.

  10. 1928

    Griffith's experiment

    Frederick Griffith observes that a harmless bacterium can become dangerous after receiving "something" from a dead one.

    Read more

    Griffith, a British public health doctor, was looking for a pneumonia vaccine. When he mixed dead virulent pneumococci with live harmless ones, the mice died, and live virulent bacteria were recovered from them, which passed the change on to their offspring. He called it the "transforming principle". He died in 1941 in a bombing raid on London, without knowing what it was.

  11. 1941

    One gene, one enzyme

    George Beadle and Edward Tatum show that each gene controls an enzyme.

    Read more

    Using the bread mould Neurospora, irradiated with X-rays, they obtained mutants that could not make a particular substance, each with a single altered enzyme. They confirmed Garrod's intuition and finally connected genetics with biochemistry. They received the Nobel Prize in 1958.

  12. 1944

    DNA is the genetic material

    Avery, MacLeod and McCarty show that the transforming principle is DNA.

    Read more

    At the Rockefeller Institute they purified Griffith's extract and found that if proteins or RNA were destroyed, transformation still occurred, but if DNA was destroyed it disappeared. Many continued to believe for years that genes were proteins. Avery's profile · The story on the blog.

  13. 1948-1951

    Jumping genes

    Barbara McClintock discovers elements in maize that move around the genome.

    Read more

    Studying coloured maize kernels and their chromosomes under the microscope, McClintock saw that certain pieces of DNA moved and switched nearby genes on or off. Her idea of a dynamic genome clashed with the view of genes as fixed beads on a string. We now know that transposons make up almost half of the human genome. She received an unshared Nobel Prize in 1983. McClintock's profile.

  14. 1950

    Chargaff's rules

    Erwin Chargaff observes that in DNA the amount of A equals that of T, and G equals C.

    Read more

    Using paper chromatography, Chargaff measured the bases in DNA from many species. Composition varied between species, disproving the idea that DNA was a monotonous molecule, but A = T and G = C always held. The explanation would come with the base pairing of the double helix. Chargaff's profile.

  15. 1951

    The alpha helix

    Linus Pauling describes the alpha helix of proteins and shows the power of models.

    Read more

    Pauling built models with the exact distances and angles between atoms and predicted the alpha helix, one of the basic shapes of proteins. Watson and Crick adopted his method. In 1953 Pauling proposed a three-stranded DNA model, which was wrong. In 1949 he had also shown that sickle cell disease is caused by an abnormal haemoglobin: the first "molecular disease". Pauling's profile.

  16. 1952

    Hershey, Chase and Photo 51

    Hershey and Chase confirm that DNA directs viruses; Franklin and Gosling obtain "Photo 51".

    Read more

    Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase radioactively labelled the DNA and proteins of a bacterial virus: only the DNA entered the cell. The same year, at King's College London, Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling obtained the X-ray diffraction image that showed a helix. Franklin's profile · Photo 51.

  17. 1953

    The double helix

    James Watson and Francis Crick propose the double-helix structure of DNA.

    Read more

    On 25 April 1953 Nature published Watson and Crick's one-page paper alongside those of Wilkins and of Franklin and Gosling. Two intertwined strands, with A opposite T and G opposite C, immediately suggested how DNA is copied. In 1958 Meselson and Stahl showed that copying is semi-conservative. The 1962 Nobel Prize went to Watson, Crick and Wilkins; Franklin had died in 1958. Watson · Crick · Wilkins.

  18. 1956

    46 chromosomes

    Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan establish that human cells have 46 chromosomes.

    Read more

    For thirty years textbooks had said 48. In Lund, Sweden, Tjio improved the techniques: treating cells with a solution that swells them and with colchicine, which stops division, spread the chromosomes apart so they could be counted. Modern human cytogenetics was born.

  19. 1959

    First chromosome disorder

    Lejeune, Gautier and Turpin discover that Down syndrome is caused by an extra chromosome 21.

    Read more

    Marthe Gautier set up the cell culture laboratory in Paris and prepared the slides; Jérôme Lejeune photographed them and presented the finding. The same year the karyotypes of Turner (45,X) and Klinefelter (47,XXY) syndromes were described. Decades later Gautier reclaimed her role, which official history had played down. Gautier's profile.

  20. 1959

    Severo Ochoa and RNA

    Severo Ochoa receives the Nobel Prize for synthesising RNA in the laboratory.

    Read more

    The Asturian biochemist discovered in New York polynucleotide phosphorylase, an enzyme that made it possible to produce artificial RNA. That tool was key for Nirenberg and Ochoa's own group to crack the genetic code. Ochoa trained scientists such as Margarita Salas and Eladio Viñuela. Ochoa's profile.

  21. 1961-1966

    The genetic code is cracked

    Scientists work out which amino acid corresponds to each three-letter codon.

    Read more

    In 1961 Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei added an artificial RNA made only of U (poly-U) to a bacterial extract and obtained a protein made only of phenylalanine: UUU = phenylalanine. The same year Crick and Brenner showed that the code is read in threes. Har Gobind Khorana, Ochoa and others completed the table by 1966. Nirenberg, Khorana and Holley received the Nobel Prize in 1968. Nirenberg's profile.

  22. 1961

    How genes are regulated

    François Jacob and Jacques Monod describe the lac operon, the first genetic switch.

    Read more

    At the Pasteur Institute they explained how the bacterium E. coli switches on the genes to digest lactose only when lactose is present: a repressor protein blocks the gene until the sugar removes it. They also proposed the existence of messenger RNA. It was the start of the study of gene regulation, key to understanding why each cell uses different genes. Nobel Prize 1965.

  23. 1963

    The heel-prick test

    Robert Guthrie invents a simple test to detect phenylketonuria in every newborn.

    Read more

    Guthrie, a microbiologist with a son and a niece with intellectual disability, devised a bacterial assay that detected excess phenylalanine in a drop of blood dried on paper. It made it possible to treat babies with diet from birth and prevent brain damage. The "Guthrie card" is the basis of all current newborn screening programmes. Guthrie's profile.

  24. 1966

    McKusick's catalogue

    Victor McKusick publishes Mendelian Inheritance in Man, the catalogue of hereditary diseases.

    Read more

    The first edition listed about 1,500 traits. Today, in its online version, OMIM, it describes more than 16,000 genes and thousands of disorders. McKusick is considered the father of medical genetics. McKusick's profile.

  25. 1972-1975

    Recombinant DNA and Asilomar

    Berg, Cohen and Boyer cut and paste DNA from different organisms. Scientists regulate themselves at Asilomar.

    Read more

    In 1972 Paul Berg joined DNA from two viruses; in 1973 Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer put a foreign gene into bacteria that copied it. Faced with possible risks, the researchers themselves called for a moratorium and in 1975 met at Asilomar, California, to set safety rules, a pioneering example of scientific responsibility. In 1982 the first human insulin made by bacteria was approved. Berg's profile.

  26. 1977

    Sanger sequencing

    Frederick Sanger develops a method to read DNA letter by letter.

    Read more

    The dideoxy method, which stops DNA copying at each letter, was the standard for 30 years and was used to read the first human genome. Sanger had already won a Nobel Prize in 1958 for sequencing insulin; in 1980 he received his second, shared with Berg and Walter Gilbert. Sanger's profile.

  27. 1983-1985

    PCR

    Kary Mullis invents the polymerase chain reaction.

    Read more

    As he told it, the idea came to him while driving at night in California: heat and cool DNA in cycles so that an enzyme copies it over and over, doubling it each cycle. With a heat-resistant polymerase from a hot-spring bacterium, the technique was automated. It is now basic to diagnostics, forensics and research. Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1993. Mullis's profile.

  28. 1983

    Huntington's gene, on a chromosome

    The Huntington's disease gene is mapped to chromosome 4, thanks to families in Venezuela.

    Read more

    Nancy Wexler, whose mother had the disease, organised the study of large families on the shores of Lake Maracaibo. Using DNA markers, James Gusella's group placed the gene on chromosome 4 in 1983. The gene, HTT, and its CAG expansion were identified in 1993. It was the first disease gene located this way, without knowing what protein it made. Wexler's profile.

  29. 1984-1985

    DNA fingerprinting

    Alec Jeffreys discovers that DNA can identify individuals.

    Read more

    In Leicester, one September morning in 1984, Jeffreys saw on an X-ray film a pattern of bands that differed in every person. In 1986 it was used for the first time in a criminal case: it cleared a suspect and led to the conviction of the real perpetrator of two murders. Since then it has resolved paternity and immigration cases and identified victims. Jeffreys's profile.

  30. 1980-1990

    Phi29 DNA polymerase

    Margarita Salas discovers in Madrid an enzyme that copies long DNA with great accuracy.

    Read more

    Studying a bacterial virus, Salas's group found a polymerase able to copy very long stretches of DNA without letting go. Patented by the Spanish National Research Council (CSIC), it is used worldwide to amplify DNA from tiny amounts, and became the most profitable patent in the institution's history. Salas's profile.

  31. 1989-1990

    Disease genes: CFTR and BRCA1

    The cystic fibrosis gene is identified and the first hereditary breast cancer gene is mapped.

    Read more

    In 1989 the teams of Lap-Chee Tsui, Francis Collins and John Riordan found the CFTR gene and the F508del variant. In 1990 Mary-Claire King showed through family studies that a hereditary breast cancer gene existed on chromosome 17; it was isolated in 1994 (BRCA1). Tsui · King · Collins.

  32. 1990

    Genome Project and first gene therapy

    The Human Genome Project begins and Ashanti DeSilva receives the first authorised gene therapy.

    Read more

    The Human Genome Project set out to read the 3 billion letters in 15 years, with 3–5 % of its budget devoted to ethical, legal and social implications. The same year, 4-year-old Ashanti DeSilva received her own lymphocytes carrying a healthy copy of the ADA gene. Also in 1990 the first babies were born after preimplantation genetic diagnosis, in London.

  33. 1996

    Dolly the sheep

    Dolly, the first mammal cloned from an adult cell, is born in Scotland.

    Read more

    Ian Wilmut and Keith Campbell, at the Roslin Institute, transferred the nucleus of a cell from a sheep's udder into an egg without a nucleus. Dolly showed that an adult cell retains all the information needed to form an organism. The announcement in 1997 sparked an intense debate on human cloning, which most countries banned.

  34. 2001-2003

    The human genome

    The first drafts are published (2001) and the Human Genome Project is completed (2003).

    Read more

    The public consortium, led by Francis Collins and with John Sulston as a great advocate of open access, competed with Craig Venter's company Celera. In June 2000 both presented a first draft together at the White House. The big surprise was the number of genes: about 20,000, far fewer than expected. Collins · Venter.

  35. 2005

    Mojica and CRISPR

    Francisco Mojica proposes that bacterial CRISPR sequences are a defence system against viruses.

    Read more

    From 1993 Mojica had been studying strange repeats in the DNA of archaea from the salt marshes of Santa Pola. Comparing sequences, he saw that the spacers matched fragments of viruses and deduced that they were an immune memory. Getting it published was hard: several journals rejected it. With Ruud Jansen he also coined the name CRISPR. Mojica's profile.

  36. 2005-2008

    High-throughput sequencing

    New technologies read millions of fragments at once and the cost of a genome collapses.

    Read more

    "Next-generation" platforms multiplied reading capacity. In 2008 the first complete individual genomes were published, including James Watson's. The cost of a genome fell from around 100 million dollars in 2001 to under 1,000 by about 2020. This opened the door to panels, exomes and genomes in clinical practice.

  37. 2010

    The Neanderthal genome

    Svante Pääbo publishes the Neanderthal genome and shows that living humans carry some of its DNA.

    Read more

    With techniques to recover badly degraded ancient DNA, Pääbo's team in Leipzig showed that people outside Africa carry about 2 % Neanderthal DNA. The same year he identified the Denisovans from a finger bone. He received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2022. Pääbo's profile.

  38. 2012

    CRISPR-Cas9 as a tool

    Charpentier and Doudna show that CRISPR-Cas9 can be programmed to cut DNA wherever desired.

    Read more

    Their paper in Science showed that changing a guide RNA was enough to direct Cas9. In 2013 Feng Zhang, George Church and others applied it in human cells. Within a few years it became the standard laboratory tool. Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2020. Charpentier · Doudna · CRISPR explained.

  39. 2018

    The CRISPR babies

    He Jiankui announces the birth of two girls from edited embryos.

    Read more

    The scientific community condemned it almost unanimously: there was no medical need, the editing was imprecise and consent was inadequate. He was sentenced to three years in prison in China. The case accelerated international rules against heritable editing. Ethics of gene editing.

  40. 2022

    The complete genome

    The Telomere-to-Telomere consortium publishes the first complete, gap-free human genome sequence.

    Read more

    Thanks to long-read sequencing, the centromeres and other highly repetitive regions that were missing, about 8 % of the genome, were finally read. Some 200 million base pairs and nearly 100 new protein-coding genes were added. The complete Y chromosome followed in 2023.

  41. 2023

    Pangenome and first CRISPR medicine

    The human pangenome is presented and the UK authorises the first CRISPR-based treatment.

    Read more

    The first draft of the pangenome brought together genomes from 47 people of diverse ancestries to better represent human variation. In November the UK authorised exagamglogene autotemcel for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassaemia; the United States and the European Union followed shortly after.

  42. 2025

    Made-to-measure gene editing

    A baby with a rare metabolic disease receives the first base editor designed just for him.

    Read more

    In Philadelphia, a team designed and manufactured in about six months a base-editing treatment to correct the variant of KJ, a baby with severe CPS1 deficiency. It opened the door to personalised therapies for ultra-rare diseases, with major regulatory and cost challenges. CRISPR explained.
